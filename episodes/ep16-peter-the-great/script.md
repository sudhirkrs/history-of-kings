# Ep 16 — Peter the Great: The Tsar Who Dragged Russia into Europe
Target: ~9 min (1355 words) · Status: DRAFT — must pass fact-check (docs/04) before voiceover

## 1. HOOK (0:00–0:50)
**NARRATION**
In 1697, a tall young man arrived in the Dutch town of Zaandam, calling himself Peter Mikhailov. He wore workman's clothes, lived in a small wooden house, and worked as a shipwright. He was actually the Tsar of Russia, 25 years old, and over two metres tall. No Russian ruler had ever left the country before.
[VISUAL: a very tall man in workman's clothes among Dutch shipbuilders]
Peter spent eighteen months in Europe, learning shipbuilding, medicine, printing and war. When he came back, he made the boyars cut off their beards, built a navy from nothing, defeated the strongest army in Europe, and founded a new capital on a swamp. This is the story of Peter the Great.
[VISUAL: map of Peter's Russia, St Petersburg]

## 2. A CHILD IN A DANGEROUS COURT (0:50–3:00)
**NARRATION**
Peter was born on 9 June 1672, the son of Tsar Alexei and his second wife, Natalia Naryshkina. When Alexei died in 1676, the throne passed to Peter's half-brother Feodor, then in 1682 to the two boys together: the sickly sixteen-year-old Ivan V and the ten-year-old Peter, as co-tsars, with their older half-sister Sophia as regent.
[VISUAL: family tree, Peter and Ivan on the double throne]
In May 1682 the streltsy, the Moscow musketeers, revolted. Peter, aged ten, watched as the mob killed his mother's relatives in the Kremlin. It left a deep impression on him, a lifelong fear of the streltsy, and a hatred of the old Moscow court.
[VISUAL: streltsy revolt, boy Peter watching]
For seven years Sophia ruled. Peter stayed in a village outside Moscow, Preobrazhenskoe, and played military games with a group of boys. These games grew into real regiments, the Preobrazhensky and Semyonovsky Guards, which became the core of his army. In the German quarter near Moscow, he met foreign merchants and officers, and learned about science, ships and Western ways. In 1689, after a power struggle, Peter overthrew Sophia and sent her to a convent.
[VISUAL: toy regiments, German quarter, Sophia in a convent]

## 3. AZOV AND THE GREAT EMBASSY (3:00–6:00)
**NARRATION**
Peter took full power only when his mother died in 1694. Ivan V died in 1696. His first campaign was against the Ottoman fortress of Azov on the Black Sea. In 1695 it failed because he had no navy. In a winter, he built a fleet of about thirty ships on the Voronezh river, and took Azov in 1696.
[VISUAL: shipyard at Voronezh, Azov siege]
Then came the Great Embassy. In March 1697, a delegation of 250 people left Moscow for Europe, with Peter travelling incognito. They visited Riga, Prussia, Holland, England and Vienna. In Zaandam and Amsterdam, Peter worked in the shipyard of the Dutch East India Company, and learned shipbuilding. In England, he studied at the Deptford dockyard, visited the Royal Observatory, the Royal Mint and Oxford. He studied anatomy, learned to pull teeth, and bought tools, books and instruments.
[VISUAL: Peter at the Dutch shipyard, Deptford, Greenwich observatory]
He hired over 700 foreign experts, including shipbuilders, doctors and engineers. He planned to go to Venice, but in 1698 news came that the streltsy had risen again. He rushed home.
The revolt was crushed before he returned. Peter had it investigated, and ordered mass executions. Over a thousand streltsy were executed, some by his own hand, according to foreign diplomats. The regiments were disbanded. It was brutal.
[VISUAL: Red Square executions, streltsy regiments disbanded]

## 4. FORCING A NEW RUSSIA (6:00–9:00)
**NARRATION**
When Peter came home in 1698, he began a series of rapid changes.
[VISUAL: montage of changes]
He ordered the nobles to shave their beards, and personally cut them off. Those who wanted to keep beards had to pay a tax. He ordered them to wear Western clothes and organised social events, assemblies, where men and women mixed. This was a social revolution.
He changed the calendar. In 1700, Russia moved the start of the year from 1 September to 1 January. The year count changed from the Byzantine era to counting from Christ's birth. Russia still used the Julian calendar, which later fell behind Europe's Gregorian one.
He founded schools of navigation and mathematics, an Academy of Sciences, which opened after his death, the first Russian newspaper and a Western-style alphabet, civil script, simpler than the old Church Slavonic.
[VISUAL: beard cutting, new alphabet, first newspaper]
He built factories to produce iron, cloth, weapons and sailcloth. The Urals became a centre for ironworking. The workers were mostly serfs, forced to work in the factories.
His reforms had a human cost. Taxes were heavy, conscription was brutal, and thousands of peasants died in labour. The burden fell on the serfs. Peter did not end serfdom; he strengthened it.

## 5. THE GREAT NORTHERN WAR (9:00–13:00)
**NARRATION**
Peter's real goal was the Baltic Sea. Sweden, under its young king Charles XII, held the Baltic coast and was the dominant power in northern Europe. In 1700, Peter allied with Denmark and Saxony-Poland to attack. It was the start of the Great Northern War, which lasted twenty-one years.
[VISUAL: map of the Baltic, Sweden's empire]
It started badly. In November 1700, at Narva, the eighteen-year-old Charles XII, with about 10,000 men, destroyed a Russian army of about 35,000 in a snowstorm. Peter had left the camp just before the battle. It was a humiliating defeat.
[VISUAL: Narva snowstorm, Swedish charge]
Peter learned from it. He is said to have remarked that the Swedes would beat the Russians for some time, but would teach them how to win. While Charles went to fight in Poland, Peter rebuilt his army with conscription, new regiments and cannons made by melting church bells. He took Swedish forts at the mouth of the Neva river in 1702 and 1703.
In May 1703, on a marshy island, he laid the foundation of the Peter and Paul Fortress, the first building of St Petersburg.
[VISUAL: Neva river, Peter and Paul Fortress, founding of St Petersburg]
In 1708, Charles invaded Russia. Peter used a scorched-earth strategy, destroying supplies in his path. The Swedes ended up in Ukraine in a harsh winter. Then, on 8 July 1709, at Poltava, Peter's army of about 40,000 destroyed Charles's weakened force. Charles fled to the Ottoman Empire. Poltava ended Sweden's role as a great power and made Russia one.
[VISUAL: Poltava battle, Charles carried on a litter]
Peter's attempt against the Ottomans in 1711 failed, and he had to return Azov. But in 1714, his new navy beat the Swedes at Gangut, and in 1721, the war ended with the Treaty of Nystad, giving Russia Livonia, Estonia, Ingria and part of Karelia.

## 6. THE EMPEROR AND HIS STATE (13:00–15:30)
**NARRATION**
After Nystad, the Senate and the Synod asked Peter to take the title of Emperor of all Russia, with the epithet Great. He accepted in October 1721.
[VISUAL: ceremony, Senate]
He built a government to run the empire. A Senate (1711) replaced the old boyar council. Ten administrative colleges, similar to Swedish ministries, took over from the old chancelleries. The Church was brought under state control: the Patriarchate was abolished and replaced by the Holy Synod in 1721.
In 1722 he issued the Table of Ranks, which created fourteen ranks for the army, navy and civil service. Anyone who reached the eighth rank became a hereditary noble. Birth mattered less, and service mattered more.
[VISUAL: Table of Ranks chart]
St Petersburg was his window to Europe, designed by foreign architects like Domenico Trezzini. He forced nobles to move there and build houses. It cost tens of thousands of lives, because workers died in the marshes. It became the capital in 1712.
[VISUAL: St Petersburg in 1725, canals and buildings]

## 7. THE SON AND THE END (15:30–17:30)
**NARRATION**
Peter's private life was difficult. He divorced his first wife, Eudoxia, in 1698 and later married Catherine, a Livonian peasant girl who became his empress. His son and heir, Alexei, from the first marriage, opposed Peter's reforms and fled to Vienna in 1716. He was lured home in 1718, put on trial for treason, and sentenced to death. He died in prison on 26 June 1718, after torture, according to many accounts.
[VISUAL: Alexei, trial, prison cell]
Peter died on 8 February 1725, aged fifty-two, from a urinary illness. A famous story says that in November 1724 he jumped into freezing water to rescue sailors, which worsened his illness. He had not named an heir.
[VISUAL: Peter on his deathbed]
His wife became Empress Catherine I. A series of palace coups followed.

## 8. LEGACY AND DEBATE (17:30–19:30)
**NARRATION**
Peter's Russia was a different country: it had a navy, a modern army, a Baltic coast, a new capital, a bureaucracy, and a European identity. He made Russia a great power.
[VISUAL: map of Russia in 1725]
But historians debate his legacy. For Pushkin, he was a titan. For Slavophiles, he betrayed Russian traditions. For Soviet historians, he was a precursor. More recently, scholars like Lindsey Hughes stress that many of his changes had earlier roots, and that his methods, based on terror and forced labour, left a long shadow.
[VISUAL: Pushkin's Bronze Horseman statue]
