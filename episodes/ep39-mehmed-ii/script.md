# Ep 39 — Mehmed the Conqueror: The Sultan Who Took Constantinople
Target: ~7 min (1079 words) · Status: DRAFT, facts checked by search first (see factcheck/), still needs a human check against books

## 1. HOOK (0:00–0:50)
**NARRATION**
In the spring of 1453, a twenty-one-year-old sultan surrounded the greatest city of Christian Europe with an army of tens of thousands, a fleet of hundreds of ships, and a bronze cannon so large that it took dozens of oxen to move. For almost two months, the walls of Constantinople, which had stood for a thousand years, held out.
[VISUAL: Theodosian walls, the great cannon, the Ottoman fleet]
On 29 May, the city fell. The Byzantine Empire, the last remnant of ancient Rome, ended. The sultan was Mehmed II, later known as Fatih, the Conqueror. He was also a scholar, who spoke several languages, invited a Venetian painter to his court, and rebuilt the city as an imperial capital. This is the story of Mehmed the Conqueror.
[VISUAL: Mehmed portrait, Constantinople]

## 2. A PRINCE AND TWO REIGNS (0:50–3:00)
**NARRATION**
Mehmed was born on 30 March 1432, the son of Sultan Murad II. As a boy he was sent to govern a province, and educated by tutors in religion, languages and military training. At twelve, in 1444, his father gave up the throne, and Mehmed became sultan for the first time. His reign was short. When a crusader army threatened, the viziers called Murad back, and Mehmed was sent away to govern again. In 1446 he was removed.
[VISUAL: Murad II, boy Mehmed on the throne]
The humiliation stayed with him. Murad died in 1451, and Mehmed, nineteen, came to the throne for the second time. One of his first acts was said to be the order for the death of his infant half-brother, to remove any rival. This was in line with an old Ottoman habit. Mehmed later made it a rule.
Almost immediately he started planning the conquest of Constantinople.

## 3. THE CITY AND THE GUN (3:00–6:00)
**NARRATION**
By 1451 Constantinople was a shadow of its former self. The Byzantine Empire consisted of the city itself and a few lands in Greece, surrounded by the Ottomans. The city had perhaps fifty thousand people, down from several hundred thousand at its peak. Yet it had massive land walls, built in the fifth century under Theodosius, with a double wall, a moat, and about a hundred towers.
[VISUAL: Constantinople in 1450, land walls cross-section]
Mehmed prepared carefully. He built a fortress on the European side of the Bosphorus, Rumeli Hisari, to control the strait. He gathered an army of perhaps fifty to eighty thousand men, according to different estimates, and a fleet.
He also recruited a Hungarian engineer called Orban, who offered his services to the Byzantine emperor, Constantine XI. The emperor lacked the money to pay him, and Orban turned to Mehmed. In 1452, Orban cast a huge cannon at Edirne, said to be around eight metres long. The Ottomans also had over seventy other guns.
[VISUAL: Rumeli Hisari, Orban, the great bombard]

## 4. THE SIEGE (6:00–9:30)
**NARRATION**
The siege began in early April 1453. The Ottoman guns pounded the walls, but at first the walls held. The huge cannon was slow to load, firing perhaps seven times a day, and the defenders repaired breaches each night with timber, stones and barrels.
[VISUAL: bombardment, defenders repairing breaches]
At sea, the Ottomans could not break the chain across the Golden Horn. According to the traditional account, Mehmed had ships dragged overland on greased logs, around the Genoese colony of Galata, and launched into the Golden Horn. This forced the defenders to stretch their thin forces along the sea walls.
The defenders were a few thousand, with Greeks, Venetians, Genoese, and others, led by the Emperor Constantine XI and the Genoese captain Giovanni Giustiniani. Help from the West did not arrive.
On the night of 28 to 29 May, Mehmed ordered a general assault. After hours of fighting, Giustiniani was wounded and withdrew, and the defence collapsed. The Ottomans poured into the city on 29 May. Constantine XI is believed to have died in the fighting. His body was never certainly identified.
[VISUAL: ships on land, final assault, Constantine's last stand]
The city was plundered for three days, according to custom. Many were killed, and thousands enslaved. Then Mehmed ordered the looting to stop.

## 5. REBUILDING THE CITY (9:30–12:00)
**NARRATION**
Mehmed made Constantinople, later called Istanbul, his capital. He needed it repopulated. He ordered people from across the empire, Turks, Greeks, Armenians, Jews and others, to move there. He rebuilt walls and markets, built a new palace, and from 1459, began the Topkapi Palace.
[VISUAL: Istanbul rebuilding, Topkapi]
The great church of Hagia Sophia was converted into a mosque. Mehmed also supported the Orthodox Church, allowing a patriarch, Gennadios, to be appointed, as the head of the Orthodox community. This became the basis for what was later called the millet system, in which religious communities had their own laws. The details of this system developed later. Historians debate how much of it was Mehmed's design.
[VISUAL: Hagia Sophia, patriarch, communities]
Mehmed was a man of wide interests. He spoke Turkish, Persian, Arabic, Greek, and apparently some Latin and Serbian. He collected books and maps, and invited artists and scholars from Italy. In 1479 to 1481 the Venetian painter Gentile Bellini lived in Istanbul, as part of the peace treaty between Venice and the Ottomans, and painted Mehmed's portrait.
[VISUAL: Bellini portrait, library]

## 6. A CONQUEROR TO THE END (12:00–15:00)
**NARRATION**
After 1453, Mehmed continued to expand. He took Serbia, in stages in the 1450s. In 1456, he lost: his army besieged Belgrade, and was repulsed by the forces of the Hungarian commander John Hunyadi. In 1461 he took Trebizond, the last Greek state on the Black Sea. In 1463 he conquered Bosnia, and in the long Ottoman-Venetian war of 1463 to 1479, took parts of Albania and the Aegean. He also expanded in Anatolia, and in the Crimea.
[VISUAL: animated map of conquests]
In 1480, one of his commanders, Gedik Ahmed Pasha, landed in southern Italy and took the city of Otranto. It looked as if Mehmed might invade Italy. But he died in the spring of 1481, on 3 May, while on campaign, and Otranto was given up.
[VISUAL: Otranto, Mehmed's death]
His succession was disputed between his sons, Bayezid and Cem. Bayezid won.

## 7. LEGACY AND DEBATE (15:00–17:00)
**NARRATION**
Mehmed's conquest of Constantinople is seen as a turning point in history. It marked the end of the Byzantine Empire and the beginning of the Ottoman Empire as a world power. Some historians use it as the end of the Middle Ages, though this is a convenient label.
[VISUAL: 1453 dividing line]
He is a hero in Turkey, a conqueror whose name is borne by streets and bridges. In Greece and elsewhere in the Balkans, the memory is different.
Historians debate several points. How decisive was the great cannon? Was the siege plan as brilliant as the story says, or did the weak defence count more? How tolerant was he really? And was he the ruler who made the empire, or was he building on his father's work?
