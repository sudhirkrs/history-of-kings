# Ep 34 — Ranjit Singh: The Lion of the Punjab

## 1. HOOK (0:00–0:50)
**NARRATION**
In 1799 a nineteen-year-old with one eye rode into the city of Lahore at the head of a small army and took it almost without a fight. Forty years later, when he died, he ruled an empire that stretched from the Khyber Pass to the borders of Tibet, with an army trained by French and Italian officers and a capital that was the richest city in northern India.
[VISUAL: a young rider entering Lahore, then a map of the Sikh Empire]
He could not read or write. He had survived smallpox, which blinded him in one eye. He is remembered as Sher-e-Punjab, the Lion of the Punjab. He was also the one ruler who, for four decades, kept the British East India Company at arm's length. This is the story of Maharaja Ranjit Singh.
[VISUAL: Ranjit Singh portrait]

## 2. THE PUNJAB OF HIS YOUTH (0:50–2:06)
**NARRATION**
To understand Ranjit Singh we need to understand Sikh history. The Sikh faith was founded by Guru Nanak in the fifteenth century. Over the next two hundred years, the Sikhs were persecuted by the Mughals, and organised themselves for defence. The tenth Guru, Gobind Singh, founded the Khalsa, the community of the pure, in 1699.
[VISUAL: Guru Nanak, Guru Gobind Singh, Khalsa]
After the Mughal power collapsed in the eighteenth century, and the Afghans under Ahmad Shah Durrani raided northern India repeatedly, the Sikhs formed twelve confederacies, called misls, each led by its own chief. They fought the Afghans and also each other. By 1780, the Punjab was divided among misls, Afghan governors and other powers.
Ranjit Singh was born on 13 November 1780 in Gujranwala, the son of Maha Singh, the chief of one of the misls, the Sukerchakia, and his wife Raj Kaur. As a child he caught smallpox. It blinded one eye, and left his face scarred. His father died when he was about ten, and for years he was under the guidance of his mother and others. At about fifteen, he took over his own affairs.
[VISUAL: twelve misls map, boy Ranjit Singh]

## 3. LAHORE AND THE MAHARAJA (2:06–3:44)
**NARRATION**
The Afghan ruler Zaman Shah had invaded the Punjab in 1798 and 1799. When he withdrew, he gave Ranjit Singh some authority in Lahore, according to some accounts, but the city was held by three Sikh chiefs of the Bhangi misl, who were disliked by the people. In 1799, Ranjit Singh took Lahore, with the help of local leaders who opened the gates. He made it his capital.
[VISUAL: Lahore in 1799, local notables opening the gate]
In 1801, he was proclaimed Maharaja of the Punjab, in a ceremony in Lahore, with the blessing of a Sikh religious leader, Baba Sahib Singh Bedi. Sources vary on the exact date.
His method was a mix of force, alliances and marriages. He took over or absorbed the other misls one by one, and then moved outwards. He understood that to unite the Sikhs he had to bring the chiefs under one ruler. In 1802 he took Amritsar, the holy city. He built his state slowly, over twenty years.
[VISUAL: misl chiefs submitting, Amritsar]
In 1809, he made a treaty with the British East India Company, at Amritsar. The British were then expanding in India. Ranjit Singh wanted to extend his rule south of the Sutlej river, but the British made it clear they would not allow it. The treaty fixed the Sutlej as the boundary and was kept by both sides for thirty years. He understood he could not defeat the British, and he turned his armies to the north and west instead.

## 4. A NEW ARMY (3:44–4:37)
**NARRATION**
Ranjit Singh had seen the British army and its discipline. He knew the traditional Sikh cavalry would not be enough. He wanted an army trained in the European style.
[VISUAL: Sikh cavalry, then drilled infantry in rows]
He hired European officers, mostly veterans of Napoleon's wars who were looking for work. Among them were the Frenchman Jean-François Allard, who trained the cavalry, and the Italian Jean-Baptiste Ventura, who trained the infantry, as well as the French artillery officer Claude Auguste Court, and the Italian Paolo Avitabile, who later governed Peshawar. In 1822, an elite division, the Fauj-i-Khas, was organised under Allard and Ventura. The Sikh army became one of the best in Asia.
He also kept control. The soldiers were paid regularly. The artillery was strong. The empire was held together by loyalty to the Maharaja himself.
[VISUAL: Allard, Ventura, Court, Avitabile; Fauj-i-Khas]

## 5. EXPANDING THE EMPIRE (4:37–5:34)
**NARRATION**
With his army, Ranjit Singh expanded in all directions except south.
[VISUAL: animated map, 1799 to 1839]
In 1818, his forces under Misr Diwan Chand captured Multan from the Afghan governor. In 1819, he won Kashmir, after the battle of Shopian. In 1834, his general Hari Singh Nalwa took Peshawar, pushing the borders of the empire to the Khyber Pass. Hari Singh Nalwa became legendary among the Afghans, and the Sikhs.
In all, by 1839, the Sikh Empire covered the Punjab, Kashmir, Peshawar and parts of what is today Pakistan, and territories reaching toward Ladakh.
He also got the famous Koh-i-Noor diamond. It had been in the possession of the Afghan ruler Shah Shuja, who took refuge in Lahore, and gave it to Ranjit Singh in 1813. Later it passed to the British after the fall of the Sikh Empire in 1849.
[VISUAL: Multan, Kashmir, Peshawar, Koh-i-Noor]

## 6. A TOLERANT COURT (5:34–6:40)
**NARRATION**
Ranjit Singh was a devout Sikh, but he ruled over a population that was mostly Muslim, with many Hindus too. His court included people of all faiths. His foreign minister was a Muslim, Fakir Azizuddin. His finance minister, Dina Nath, was a Hindu. His generals included Sikhs, Hindus, Muslims and Europeans.
[VISUAL: the court: Fakir Azizuddin, Dina Nath, Hari Singh Nalwa]
He gave gifts to mosques and temples as well as gurdwaras. He ordered the Harmandir Sahib in Amritsar, the holiest Sikh shrine, to be covered in gold leaf and marble, which is why people call it the Golden Temple.
Historians note some limits. The state was a personal monarchy. He took revenue from the peasantry. The Muslim majority in the countryside did not always feel represented. But in comparison with many rulers of his age, he was seen as fair.
He was also a man of simple habits in dress, and unaffected by ceremony. A British visitor described him as a small man, with one eye, who asked endless questions.

## 7. LAHORE AND AMRITSAR: THE SEATS OF POWER (6:40–7:50)
**NARRATION**
Two cities mattered most to Ranjit Singh. Amritsar was the holy city of the Sikhs. When he took it in 1802, he also gained the Zamzama, a famous big gun, which had earlier been captured from the Afghans by the Bhangi chiefs. Sources differ on the exact date of the city's surrender, and on the history of the gun, but the gun came into his hands.
[VISUAL: Amritsar, the Zamzama gun]
Lahore was his capital. Beside the Lahore Fort he laid out a garden, the Hazuri Bagh, with a marble pavilion where he held court in the open air, next to the great Badshahi Mosque. His state called itself the Khalsa Darbar, and its main income was land revenue, collected through local officials called kardars, which was a large share of the harvest, from a third to a half by one estimate. Over time the state moved from simply taking a share of the crop to assessing cash payments. It was effective, but it also meant that the peasants carried the cost of the army.
[VISUAL: Lahore Fort, Hazuri Bagh, a revenue official]

## 8. THE KHYBER FRONTIER AND JAMRUD (7:50–8:55)
**NARRATION**
The last great frontier of the empire was in the northwest, against the Afghans. In late 1836 Hari Singh Nalwa, the commander on this front, took the small fort of Jamrud, at the mouth of the Khyber Pass. That gave the Sikhs a border with Afghanistan, and alarmed Dost Mohammad Khan, the ruler in Kabul.
On 30 April 1837 an Afghan army under his son Akbar Khan attacked Jamrud. The battle was fierce, and Hari Singh Nalwa was killed. Accounts differ on the result. Some say the Sikhs held the fort and the Afghans fell back to Kabul. Others call it an Afghan success because the great Sikh commander was dead. Some sources also say that reinforcements were slow, because many of the best troops had been called back to Lahore for a wedding. Either way, the empire never again pushed beyond the Khyber, and the Sikhs mourned the loss of the man the Afghans had feared.
[VISUAL: Khyber Pass, Jamrud fort, Hari Singh Nalwa's last stand]

## 9. DEATH AND COLLAPSE (8:55–10:01)
**NARRATION**
Ranjit Singh died on 27 June 1839, in Lahore, aged fifty-eight, after a series of strokes. His successor was his son Kharak Singh, but the empire depended on the Maharaja's own personality.
[VISUAL: Ranjit Singh's funeral, successors]
In the next six years, the empire fell into chaos. Kharak Singh was overthrown in 1839 and died in custody in 1840, widely believed poisoned. His son died in an accident. Another son, Sher Singh, was crowned in 1841, and later assassinated. Rival factions of the court, the Sikh Sindhanwalias and the Hindu Dogras, fought for power, and the army, grown to eighty thousand or more, became a political force of its own.
[VISUAL: succession tree, factions, army in revolt]
The British had been watching. In 1845 the First Anglo-Sikh War began, and ended in 1846 with the Sikh defeat. The Second Anglo-Sikh War followed in 1848 and 1849. The Punjab was annexed on 29 March 1849, when the boy Maharaja Duleep Singh, then about ten, was made to give up his throne.
[VISUAL: Anglo-Sikh wars, Duleep Singh]

## 10. LEGACY AND DEBATE (10:01–10:47)
**NARRATION**
Ranjit Singh built the only major Indian state that the British did not conquer in his lifetime. His memory is strong in the Punjab, on both sides of the modern border.
Historians still discuss several things. How much of the empire was his own genius, and how much was luck? How secular was his rule? And could the state have survived his death with a stronger heir? Some also point out that his heavy dependence on a personal army was part of what ended his dynasty.
