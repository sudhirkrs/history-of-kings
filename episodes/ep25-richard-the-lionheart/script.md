# Ep 25 — Richard the Lionheart: The Crusader King Who Barely Saw England
Target: ~11 min (1599 words) · Status: DRAFT — must pass fact-check (docs/04) before voiceover

## 1. HOOK (0:00–0:50)
**NARRATION**
He is the most famous king in English history. His statue stands outside the Houses of Parliament. He is the Lionheart of legend, the hero of Robin Hood stories, the crusader who faced Saladin. And yet in a ten-year reign, he spent only about six months in England.
[VISUAL: Richard's statue at Westminster, crusader on horseback]
He spent his life on horseback in France, on crusade in the Holy Land, and in a German prison. He never took Jerusalem. He ordered the execution of 2,700 prisoners at Acre. Yet he is remembered as a hero. This is the story of Richard I, and of how a king became a legend.
[VISUAL: map of Richard's travels]

## 2. THE DEVIL'S BROOD (0:50–3:30)
**NARRATION**
Richard was born on 8 September 1157 in Oxford. His father was Henry II, King of England and ruler of the Angevin empire, which stretched from Scotland to the Pyrenees. His mother was Eleanor of Aquitaine, who had been Queen of France before her first marriage was annulled, and was the heiress of Aquitaine in south-west France.
[VISUAL: Henry II, Eleanor of Aquitaine]
The family was famous for its quarrels. A saying attributed to St Bernard, who died before they were born, called them the Devil's brood: from the devil they came, and to the devil they will go.
Richard grew up mostly in Aquitaine with his mother. He was tall, red-haired, and talented. He wrote poetry in French and Occitan, as a troubadour. In 1172, aged fourteen, he was invested as Duke of Aquitaine. In 1173, with his brothers, he rebelled against his father at his mother's urging. The revolt failed, and Eleanor was imprisoned for sixteen years.
[VISUAL: Aquitaine, troubadour, rebellion]
Richard reconciled with his father and became a fine soldier, putting down rebellious barons in Aquitaine. He won a name for sieges, especially the storming of the castle at Taillebourg in 1179. 
In 1189, Henry II, old and sick, was at war with Richard and the new French king, Philip II. Richard joined Philip against his father. Henry was beaten, and died on 6 July 1189 at Chinon, reportedly after learning that his favourite son John had also betrayed him. Richard became king.

## 3. A KING FOR SALE (3:30–5:30)
**NARRATION**
Richard was crowned in Westminster Abbey on 3 September 1189. During the celebrations, a riot broke out in London, in which Jews, who had come to give gifts, were attacked. Many were killed. Richard punished some of the rioters, but the violence continued, and in March 1190 reached a peak in York, where about 150 Jews died in the castle in a mass suicide and massacre.
[VISUAL: coronation, riot, Clifford's Tower in York]
Richard had a plan: the Third Crusade. In 1187, Saladin, the Sultan of Egypt and Syria, had crushed the Crusader army at the Horns of Hattin and taken Jerusalem. Pope Gregory VIII called for a new crusade. Richard, who had taken the cross in 1187, needed money fast. He raised it by every means. He sold offices, lands, and castles. He said he would sell London itself if he could find a buyer. He also imposed the Saladin tithe, a tax of a tenth of income and property.
[VISUAL: Saladin at Hattin, Richard selling offices]
Before leaving, he appointed William Longchamp as chancellor. In July 1190, he left from Vezelay in France with Philip II.

## 4. SICILY, CYPRUS AND ACRE (5:30–8:30)
**NARRATION**
The journey was eventful. The two kings wintered in Sicily, where Richard fell out with the new king, Tancred, over the dowry of his sister Joan, the widow of the old king. Richard took Messina by storm. He also fell out with Philip, over Richard's engagement to Philip's sister Alys, which Richard broke so as to marry Berengaria of Navarre.
[VISUAL: Sicily, Messina, Berengaria]
In May 1191, Richard's fleet was scattered in a storm, and some of his ships ran aground at Cyprus. The ruler of Cyprus, Isaac Komnenos, treated the shipwrecked men badly and refused help. Richard conquered the island in a few weeks. He married Berengaria at Limassol. Cyprus gave the Crusaders a useful base, and Richard sold it to the Knights Templar, later to Guy of Lusignan, who stayed on as king.
[VISUAL: Cyprus conquest, wedding at Limassol]
Richard arrived at Acre on 8 June 1191. The Crusaders had been besieging the city for almost two years. Richard brought fresh troops, siege engines and energy. On 12 July, the city surrendered. Philip II, ill and quarrelling with Richard, left for France in August, leaving a force behind.
The terms of surrender included a ransom for the garrison, and the return of the True Cross. Saladin was slow in paying. On 20 August 1191, Richard had about 2,700 Muslim prisoners taken outside the walls (sources give from 2,600 to 3,000), in view of Saladin's army, and executed them. It was a brutal act. Medieval chroniclers on both sides recorded it. Some Christian writers accepted it as a military necessity. Muslim writers condemned it.
[VISUAL: siege of Acre, prisoners outside the walls, solemn]

## 5. ARSUF AND THE ROAD TO JERUSALEM (8:30–11:30)
**NARRATION**
From Acre, Richard led his army south along the coast towards Jaffa, which would be the base for an advance on Jerusalem. The march was a masterpiece of discipline. The army moved in a tight formation, with the fleet along the coast, and crossbowmen holding back the Muslim horse archers who attacked all day.
[VISUAL: the march along the coast, formation diagram]
On 7 September 1191, at Arsuf, Saladin's army attacked. Richard held his knights back for as long as he could. When the Hospitallers charged too early, Richard joined with the entire army, and broke the Muslim line. It was the biggest victory the Crusaders had had in years. It showed that Saladin could be beaten.
Richard then took Jaffa. But he did not march on Jerusalem. He was twice close, in the winter of 1191 and in June 1192, and each time he turned back. His reasons were military: Jerusalem was well defended by Saladin, with poor water supplies around, and the Crusader army might be trapped. He also knew that, even if he took Jerusalem, he could not hold it once the army went home. He is said to have covered his face with his shield when he came within sight of the city, saying he was not worthy to see the city he could not save. This is a later legend.
[VISUAL: Arsuf battle, the view of Jerusalem from the hills]
The Muslim sources praise Richard's courage and tactics, and Saladin respected him. They exchanged gifts. Saladin sent fruit and ice when Richard was ill. They never met in person.
Meanwhile bad news came from home. His brother John was plotting with Philip to seize the crown. In September 1192, Richard and Saladin signed the Treaty of Jaffa, a three-year truce. Jerusalem stayed in Muslim hands, but Christian pilgrims could visit. The coast from Tyre to Jaffa was Crusader. Richard left Palestine in October 1192.
[VISUAL: Richard and Saladin's exchange of gifts, treaty]

## 6. CAPTIVE (11:30–14:00)
**NARRATION**
On the way home, Richard's ship was wrecked in the Adriatic. He tried to cross Austria in disguise, as a merchant. But he had made an enemy there: Duke Leopold V of Austria, whose banner Richard's men had torn down at Acre. In December 1192, Leopold's men found Richard in a village near Vienna, and arrested him.
[VISUAL: disguised king, Leopold]
Leopold handed him over to the Holy Roman Emperor, Henry VI, who held him in captivity in castles in Germany, including Trifels. Henry demanded a huge ransom of 150,000 marks, about twice the annual income of the English crown. It was collected from taxes in England, and from the Church, including plate and jewels.
A famous legend tells of the minstrel Blondel, who went from castle to castle singing a song only he and Richard knew, until he heard Richard reply. This is a later legend, written in the thirteenth century.
[VISUAL: Trifels castle, Blondel at the tower window]
Meanwhile Philip II invaded Normandy, and John took control of England. Richard was released on 4 February 1194, after paying about two thirds of the ransom and giving hostages. In England he was crowned again. John was forgiven. Then Richard left England for the last time, never to return.

## 7. THE LAST FIVE YEARS (14:00–16:00)
**NARRATION**
He spent his last years fighting Philip II in France. He built Chateau Gaillard on a cliff above the Seine, in less than two years, 1196 to 1198, a fortress designed to protect Normandy. He called it his beautiful one-year-old daughter. He won several victories, and recovered most of what he had lost.
[VISUAL: Chateau Gaillard, battles in Normandy]
In March 1199, Richard was besieging a small castle at Chalus-Chabrol in the Limousin, in central France, because a local lord had found treasure and refused to hand it over. Richard went out to look at the defences without armour. A crossbowman shot him in the shoulder. The wound turned gangrenous. He died on 6 April 1199, aged forty-one, in the arms of his mother Eleanor.
[VISUAL: siege of Chalus, crossbow bolt, deathbed]
He forgave the crossbowman, according to a chronicler, though in some versions the man was flayed alive after Richard's death, by one of his captains. The story varies, and so does the shooter's name: legend says Bertrand de Gourdon, and one chronicle says Peter Basil. His heart was buried in Rouen, his body at Fontevraud, near his father and mother.
He had no children. John became king.

## 8. LEGACY AND DEBATE (16:00–18:00)
**NARRATION**
How do we judge Richard? To his contemporaries, he was a model of knightly honour, a great commander and a poet. Later writers contrasted him with his brother John. In the sixteenth century, the Robin Hood legends were set in his reign, and Richard became the good king away at the Crusades.
[VISUAL: Robin Hood legend, Richard's return]
Historians are more critical. John Gillingham, his best biographer, shows him as an able ruler, who raised huge sums, but who put his French lands before England. Others point out that the burden of his ransom and wars fell hard on England. His treatment of Jews in 1189 to 1190, and the Acre massacre, are part of his story too.
Historians still debate whether he was homosexual, as some modern writers suggest, based on a vague medieval chronicle. The evidence is thin. They also debate whether he could have taken Jerusalem, and how good a king he was for England.
