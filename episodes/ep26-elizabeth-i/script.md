# Ep 26 — Elizabeth I: The Queen Who Defied an Armada
Target: ~10 min (1464 words) · Status: DRAFT — must pass fact-check (docs/04) before voiceover

## 1. HOOK (0:00–0:50)
**NARRATION**
In August 1588, a queen in a silver breastplate rode a white horse through her army at Tilbury, on the Thames, as the largest fleet in Europe sailed towards England. She said: I know I have the body of a weak and feeble woman, but I have the heart and stomach of a king, and of a king of England too.
[VISUAL: Tilbury, queen on a white horse, army cheering]
Elizabeth I was a woman whom many in Europe thought should not have been on a throne. Her mother had been beheaded. She had been declared illegitimate. She was twice imprisoned. And yet she ruled for 44 years, defeated Spain, and gave her name to an age. This is her story.
[VISUAL: Armada portrait, map of England]

## 2. THE DAUGHTER OF ANNE BOLEYN (0:50–3:00)
**NARRATION**
Elizabeth was born on 7 September 1533, at Greenwich, the daughter of Henry VIII and his second wife, Anne Boleyn. Henry had broken with Rome, and with the Catholic Church, to marry Anne, hoping for a son. The baby was a girl.
[VISUAL: Henry VIII, Anne Boleyn, baby Elizabeth]
When Elizabeth was two years and eight months old, her mother was executed for adultery and treason, on charges that most historians believe were false. Elizabeth was declared illegitimate. Her half-sister Mary, the daughter of Henry's first wife Catherine of Aragon, was also illegitimate. 
Despite this, Elizabeth got an excellent education. Her tutors, such as Roger Ascham, taught her Latin, Greek, French and Italian. She became one of the most learned women of her age. After Henry's death in 1547, she survived dangerous episodes, including a scandal with the Lord Admiral Thomas Seymour in 1548, who was beheaded in 1549 for treason.
[VISUAL: young Elizabeth studying, Roger Ascham]
Her half-brother Edward VI died in 1553. After the failed attempt to put Lady Jane Grey on the throne, Mary became queen. Mary was a devout Catholic, and married Philip of Spain. When Protestant rebels rose in Wyatt's Rebellion in 1554, Elizabeth was suspected and sent to the Tower of London, the same place where her mother had died. She was held there for two months, then kept under house arrest at Woodstock. She survived by being careful and saying little.
[VISUAL: Elizabeth entering the Tower, Woodstock]

## 3. QUEEN AT TWENTY-FIVE (3:00–5:30)
**NARRATION**
Mary died on 17 November 1558, childless. Elizabeth, aged twenty-five, became queen. According to legend, she was sitting under an oak tree at Hatfield when the news came. She quoted Psalm 118: this is the Lord's doing, and it is marvellous in our eyes.
[VISUAL: Hatfield oak, courtiers kneeling]
She faced huge problems. England was weak, poor and divided by religion. It had lost Calais, its last foothold in France, in 1558. The Crown was in debt. Many Europeans considered her a bastard queen. Catholic powers, France and Spain, were stronger.
She chose her advisors well. William Cecil, later Lord Burghley, was her chief minister for forty years. Sir Francis Walsingham ran her spy network. And she knew when to wait. Her motto, Video et taceo, I see and say nothing.
[VISUAL: William Cecil, Walsingham]
Her first great settlement was religion. In 1559, Parliament passed the Acts of Supremacy and Uniformity. Elizabeth became Supreme Governor of the Church of England, not Supreme Head, which a woman could not be, in the view of many. The Book of Common Prayer was restored, with wording that was deliberately vague, so that both moderate Catholics and Protestants could accept it. It was a compromise, not a perfect solution, but it gave England stability.

## 4. THE VIRGIN QUEEN (5:30–8:00)
**NARRATION**
Everyone expected her to marry. A queen needed an heir, and a husband to lead armies. Suitors included Philip II of Spain, Archduke Charles of Austria, and French princes. Her favourite was Robert Dudley, Earl of Leicester, a friend since childhood. His wife Amy Robsart died in 1560, falling down stairs, which caused a scandal. Rumours blamed Dudley, though the coroner called it an accident.
[VISUAL: suitors, Robert Dudley, Amy Robsart]
Elizabeth never married. She used the prospect of marriage as a diplomatic tool, keeping foreign courts hoping for years. She also built an image: the Virgin Queen, married to her kingdom. Poets and painters presented her as Gloriana, Astraea, the moon goddess Cynthia. The cult of Elizabeth was a deliberate political strategy.
[VISUAL: portraits of Elizabeth as Gloriana]
Historians debate her reasons. Some say she feared losing power to a husband. Others say her mother's fate scarred her. The question of the succession haunted her reign. She refused to name an heir.

## 5. MARY, QUEEN OF SCOTS (8:00–10:30)
**NARRATION**
The biggest threat was her cousin Mary, Queen of Scots. Mary was a Catholic, and the granddaughter of Henry VIII's sister, so she had a strong claim to the English throne. In the eyes of Catholics, Elizabeth, the daughter of an unlawful marriage, had no right to rule, and Mary was the true queen.
[VISUAL: Mary Queen of Scots portrait]
Mary was forced out of Scotland after a scandal over the murder of her husband Lord Darnley, and in 1568 fled to England. Elizabeth could not send her back, nor free her. She held Mary captive for nineteen years. In 1570, Pope Pius V excommunicated Elizabeth and declared her subjects free of loyalty. Catholic plots followed: the Ridolfi plot in 1571, the Throckmorton plot in 1583, and the Babington plot in 1586.
In the Babington plot, Walsingham's agents intercepted letters in which Mary approved a plan to assassinate Elizabeth. Mary was put on trial. Elizabeth hesitated for months. Finally she signed the death warrant, and Mary was beheaded at Fotheringhay Castle on 8 February 1587. Elizabeth claimed it had been done without her wish, and punished her secretary. Historians doubt that she was sincere.
[VISUAL: Babington letters, Fotheringhay, the execution]

## 6. THE ARMADA (10:30–14:00)
**NARRATION**
Mary's death gave Philip II of Spain the excuse he needed. The most powerful monarch in Europe, he had been Elizabeth's brother-in-law and suitor. Now he saw her as a heretic and a pirate. English privateers, such as Francis Drake and John Hawkins, had been raiding Spanish ships and ports. In 1587, Drake attacked Cadiz and burnt dozens of ships, which he called singeing the King of Spain's beard.
[VISUAL: Philip II, Drake at Cadiz]
In May 1588, the Spanish Armada set out from Lisbon: about 130 ships, with around 26,000 sailors and soldiers aboard. A separate Spanish army of about 30,000 waited in the Netherlands. The plan was to sail up the Channel, link up with the Duke of Parma's army in the Netherlands, and ferry it across to England.
The English fleet, under Lord Howard of Effingham, with Drake and Hawkins, met the Armada off Plymouth in July. Spanish ships were bigger but the English had longer-range guns and faster ships. After a week of fighting, the Armada anchored at Calais. On the night of 7 to 8 August, by the Spanish and modern calendar, which was 28 July in England's old calendar, the English sent eight fire ships into the anchored fleet. The Spaniards panicked, cut their cables, and scattered. At the Battle of Gravelines the next day, the English attacked the disordered Spanish ships.
[VISUAL: Armada map, fire ships at Calais, Gravelines]
A wind drove the Armada north. The Spanish had to sail around Scotland and Ireland, where storms wrecked many ships. Perhaps 50 ships and some 20,000 men were lost.
At Tilbury, Elizabeth gave her famous speech. The exact words come from a letter by Leonel Sharp, written years later, so they may not be exact. But the spirit is clear.
[VISUAL: storms, wrecks, Tilbury speech]
The war with Spain lasted until 1604. The Armada's defeat did not end Spanish power, and later Spanish fleets also threatened. But it was a powerful symbol.

## 7. THE GOLDEN AGE AND ITS LIMITS (14:00–16:30)
**NARRATION**
Elizabeth's reign is called a golden age. It saw the plays of William Shakespeare and Christopher Marlowe, the poems of Edmund Spenser, the music of William Byrd, and the voyages of Drake, Raleigh and Frobisher. In 1600 she granted a royal charter to the East India Company. The first English colony in Virginia, at Roanoke, was founded in the 1580s, and vanished.
[VISUAL: Globe Theatre, Shakespeare, Drake's Golden Hind]
But the age had darker sides. Prices rose, the poor suffered, and vagrancy was a major concern. The Poor Law of 1601 made each parish responsible for its poor. Catholics were fined and persecuted. Around 180 Catholic priests and laymen were executed during her reign. In Ireland, English forces waged brutal wars, in the Desmond Rebellions and the Nine Years' War.
[VISUAL: poor people, Catholic priest hiding, Irish campaigns]
In her last years, her court was divided between factions. Her favourite, the young Earl of Essex, led a failed rebellion in 1601 and was executed.

## 8. THE END (16:30–18:00)
**NARRATION**
Elizabeth grew old and sad. Her old friends died. In her last days she refused to eat, stood for hours, and would not go to bed. She died on 24 March 1603 at Richmond Palace, aged sixty-nine. On her deathbed she is said to have signalled that James VI of Scotland, son of Mary Queen of Scots, should succeed her. The two crowns were joined.
[VISUAL: Elizabeth's last days, funeral procession]
She left no child, a navy, a stable Church, a Parliament that learned to speak up, and a national self-image.

## 9. LEGACY AND DEBATE (18:00–19:30)
**NARRATION**
Historians debate Elizabeth. Was she a decisive leader or a cautious one who often let events decide? Did she win the Armada through skill or luck? Was her reign really a golden age? John Guy, Susan Doran and Christopher Haigh give very different views.
[VISUAL: three views on cards]
