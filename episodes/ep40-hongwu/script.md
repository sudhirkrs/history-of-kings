# Ep 40 — Zhu Yuanzhang: The Beggar Who Became Emperor of China
Target: ~9 min (1323 words) · Status: DRAFT, facts checked by search first (see factcheck/), still needs a human check against books

## 1. HOOK (0:00–0:52)
**NARRATION**
In 1344, a famine and plague swept through a poor farming district in eastern China. A teenage boy lost most of his family. With nothing left, he went to a Buddhist temple to survive as a novice monk, and when the temple could no longer feed him, he went out begging for food.
[VISUAL: a starving village, a boy at a temple gate]
Twenty-four years later, that boy became emperor of all China. He drove the Mongols out of Beijing, founded the Ming dynasty, and ruled for thirty years as the Hongwu Emperor. He was an efficient and tireless ruler, and a terrifying one: he had tens of thousands of officials and generals killed. This is the story of Zhu Yuanzhang, one of the very few men in history to rise from beggar to emperor.
[VISUAL: Hongwu portrait, map of Ming China]

## 2. A CHINA IN COLLAPSE (0:52–1:58)
**NARRATION**
By the 1340s, the Mongol Yuan dynasty, founded by Kublai Khan, was falling apart. Floods on the Yellow River, famines, heavy taxes, and corrupt officials had reduced many ordinary people to desperation. Rebel groups appeared across the country. The largest were the Red Turbans, a movement with religious roots, who wore red headcloths and called for the end of Mongol rule.
[VISUAL: Yellow River flood, Red Turban rebels]
Zhu Yuanzhang was born in 1328, into a poor peasant family in the Huai river region, in what is now Anhui province. His parents and brothers died during the famine and plague of the 1340s. He was about sixteen. He survived as a monk and wandering beggar for several years.
In 1352, he joined a Red Turban band led by Guo Zixing. He was intelligent, brave and capable, and Guo made him a commander and gave him his adopted daughter in marriage. She would become Empress Ma, a steady influence on him for the rest of her life.

## 3. THE RISE TO POWER (1:58–3:15)
**NARRATION**
After Guo Zixing died, Zhu took command of his forces. In 1356, his army captured Nanjing, which became his base. He treated the people better than other rebels did, and he listened to scholars, who advised him not to be a mere bandit, but to build a government. He banned looting, in principle, and tried to protect farmers.
[VISUAL: Nanjing captured, scholars advising]
His main rivals were other rebel leaders. The most dangerous was Chen Youliang, who held the middle Yangtze with a huge fleet. In 1363, on Lake Poyang, Zhu's smaller fleet defeated Chen's in a battle of several weeks, among the largest naval battles in history. Chen was killed. Zhu then defeated the other main rival, Zhang Shicheng, in 1367.
[VISUAL: Lake Poyang naval battle]
In 1364, he took the title King of Wu. In early 1368, he declared himself emperor, took the dynasty name Ming, which means bright or radiance, and chose the era name Hongwu, meaning vast martial. He sent armies north, and the Mongol court fled from Dadu, today's Beijing, in 1368. The Mongols retreated to the steppe, and fighting continued for years, but China was once more ruled by a Chinese dynasty.
[VISUAL: proclamation in Nanjing, Mongols leaving Dadu]

## 4. BUILDING THE MING STATE (3:15–4:36)
**NARRATION**
Hongwu had seen the suffering of ordinary peasants, and he built a state for them, as he saw it. But he also built a state that gave him control over everything.
[VISUAL: peasants in the fields]
He made the army self-supporting through the weisuo system: soldiers and their families were registered as hereditary military households, and given land to farm, so that the empire had a large army without heavy taxes on civilians.
He ordered a census and land surveys. The Yellow Registers, begun in 1381, recorded the population by household, with place of residence and labour service owed. The fish-scale registers, begun in 1387 and finished by 1393, recorded landholdings for taxation. A system called lijia, introduced in 1382, organised villages into groups of households that were collectively responsible for paying taxes and doing labour service.
[VISUAL: Yellow Registers, fish-scale land maps, village groups]
He had a great city wall, of about 48 kilometres, built around Nanjing, and new palaces. He kept a standing army of a million men or more, according to one estimate. He also issued a new legal code, the Great Ming Code. He tried to restore order after the Mongol period, brought back Confucian examinations for officials, and promoted education in village schools.
[VISUAL: Nanjing, examination hall]

## 5. MONEY, TRADE AND THE SEA (4:36–5:38)
**NARRATION**
Hongwu also wanted a self-contained, agrarian empire. He kept the Yuan habit of paper money, called baochao, and by 1393 stopped minting coins in favour of paper. Paper money lost value over time, because there was little to back it.
[VISUAL: a baochao note, Ming coins]
Foreign trade he controlled even more tightly. Around 1371 he issued a sea ban, the haijin, which confined legal overseas trade to official tribute missions. Private trade with foreign countries could be punished severely. Historians give several reasons for it: pirates on the coast, the wish to keep silver and coin from flowing abroad, and the desire to make other states accept the emperor's authority. He sent envoys across Asia to invite rulers to send tribute, but in 1394 he cut ties with most overseas states. The ban was written into his Ancestral Injunctions, which later emperors were expected to obey. It was kept, with changes, for much of the dynasty.
[VISUAL: coastline, tribute envoys, a Ming ship]

## 6. THE EMPEROR WHO TRUSTED NO ONE (5:38–7:08)
**NARRATION**
Hongwu hated corruption. As a boy he had seen what greedy officials did to the poor. He imposed harsh punishments on officials who stole. But his suspicion grew as he grew older, and became a reign of terror.
[VISUAL: the emperor on his throne, officials kneeling]
In 1380 he accused his chancellor, Hu Weiyong, of treason. Hu was executed, and a purge followed in which, according to different estimates, thirty thousand or more people were killed. Hongwu then abolished the office of chancellor, and the central secretariat. The six ministries now reported directly to the emperor. After that, no Ming ruler had a chief minister with real power, and every emperor had to do the work of the chancellor himself, or delegate it to others.
[VISUAL: Hu Weiyong case, the empty chancellor's seat]
He set up the Embroidered Uniform Guard, a secret police force, to watch officials. Another purge followed in 1393, directed at the general Lan Yu, in which about fifteen thousand people are said to have died. By the end of his reign, many of the generals who had helped him win the throne had been killed.
Historians debate the numbers, and the reasons. Some think Hongwu really feared plots. Some think he was removing powerful men so that his heir would be safe. Whatever the reason, the emperor's power was now greater than that of any Chinese ruler before him.
[VISUAL: Embroidered Uniform Guard, the purges]

## 7. THE SUCCESSION PROBLEM (7:08–8:05)
**NARRATION**
Hongwu had twenty-six sons. He gave his sons the title of prince, and sent them to garrison key points around the empire, with their own armies. Zhu Di, the fourth son, was made Prince of Yan in 1370 and went to Beijing in 1380.
[VISUAL: princes in their territories]
The heir, Zhu Biao, died in 1392. Hongwu named his teenage grandson, Zhu Yunwen, as the heir. When Hongwu died in 1398, aged 69, the new emperor, known as Jianwen, tried to cut down the power of the princes. Zhu Di rebelled in 1399, in what he called a campaign to clear the court of bad advisers, and fought for three years. In 1402 he took Nanjing, and the palace was burned. The Jianwen Emperor vanished, perhaps in the fire, though a legend says he escaped. Zhu Di became the Yongle Emperor.
[VISUAL: Jianwen, Zhu Di's march, burning palace]

## 8. LEGACY AND DEBATE (8:05–9:07)
**NARRATION**
Hongwu founded a dynasty that lasted nearly three hundred years, until 1644. He restored Chinese rule, rebuilt the countryside, and set up the system of government that the Ming used throughout. But he also left a highly centralised, suspicious regime. Historians argue that the lack of a chancellor and the rule of the secret police both weakened the Ming in later centuries.
[VISUAL: Ming timeline 1368 to 1644]
Historians debate several questions. How many people did Hongwu really kill, and why? Was he a gifted ruler warped by fear, or a tyrant from the beginning? How much of his image comes from official histories written under his successors? And to what extent was his rule a return to Chinese traditions, and to what extent a new kind of autocracy?
