# Ep 33 — Babur: The Exile Who Founded the Mughal Empire

## 1. HOOK (0:00–0:50)
**NARRATION**
In April 1526, on a flat plain north of Delhi, a king with an army of perhaps twelve to fifteen thousand men faced a Sultan with many times that number, and at least a thousand war elephants. The smaller army had something the larger one lacked: cannon and muskets, used in an organised way.
[VISUAL: Panipat plain, elephants, a line of carts and cannon]
The man who won was Babur, a prince from Central Asia who had lost his homeland twice, and who had once been so poor that he was reduced to wandering with a handful of followers. He did not just win a battle. He wrote down his whole life in a book that is still read today. This is the story of Babur, and how an exile became the founder of the Mughal Empire.
[VISUAL: Babur portrait, Baburnama manuscript]

## 2. A PRINCE OF FERGANA (0:50–3:30)
**NARRATION**
Babur was born on 14 February 1483, in Andijan, in the Fergana valley, in what is today Uzbekistan. His father, Umar Shaikh Mirza, ruled a small principality. Through his father he was a descendant of the great conqueror Timur. Through his mother he was descended from Genghis Khan. That double ancestry mattered. It gave him a claim to rule, and also a great many rivals.
[VISUAL: Fergana valley, family tree: Timur on one side, Genghis Khan on the other]
In 1494, when Babur was eleven, his father died in an accident, when part of his palace collapsed, according to the sources. Babur took the throne of Fergana, a boy surrounded by uncles, cousins and ambitious nobles.
His great dream was Samarkand, Timur's old capital, the most glittering city of Central Asia. He took it in 1497, after a long siege, according to most accounts. But while he was away, his own nobles in Fergana rebelled, and his troops deserted him in Samarkand. He lost both. In his memoirs, he describes this period of his life as a time of great hardship.
[VISUAL: Samarkand's blue domes, a young prince in retreat]
He tried again. By 1501 he retook Samarkand, but was then crushed by the Uzbek leader Muhammad Shaybani Khan, a descendant of Genghis Khan with a bigger and better organised army. Babur lost his last holdings in Central Asia. He was about eighteen, with no kingdom.

## 3. KABUL AND THE TURN TO INDIA (3:30–6:00)
**NARRATION**
After years of wandering, in 1504 Babur crossed the snowy Hindu Kush with a small band and took Kabul, which became his base. It was a poor kingdom, but he loved it. He planted gardens, some of which are visited to this day, and wrote poetry. He also grew into a capable commander.
[VISUAL: Hindu Kush crossing, gardens of Kabul]
For the next twenty years he tried to get back to Samarkand, and failed. The Uzbeks ruled the north. Persia, under the Safavids, was powerful in the west. The only open direction was south-east: India.
Babur had a claim, as he saw it, because Timur had invaded India in 1398. In the 1500s, the Delhi Sultanate was weak. Its ruler, Ibrahim Lodi, was unpopular with his nobles. Some of them, including Daulat Khan Lodi, governor of the Punjab, even invited Babur to come. After several raids, he decided to make a serious attempt in 1525.
[VISUAL: map from Kabul to Delhi, Lodi nobles sending messages]

## 4. THE FIRST BATTLE OF PANIPAT (6:00–9:00)
**NARRATION**
On 21 April 1526, near Panipat, Babur met Ibrahim Lodi. The numbers are uncertain. Sources put Babur's force at about twelve to fifteen thousand, with fifteen to twenty or more cannon. Lodi had perhaps thirty to forty thousand fighting men, at least a thousand elephants, and, by Babur's estimate, up to a hundred thousand people in the camp, counting servants and followers.
[VISUAL: two armies on the plain, numbers on screen with a note: sources differ]
Babur used methods he had learned in Central Asia and from the Ottomans. His army was divided into centre, right and left wings, with further units to attack from the sides, a plan called tulughma. In the centre, he lined up carts tied together with ropes, with the cannon and gunners behind them, and gaps for musketeers. Ibrahim Lodi had no field artillery.
When the Delhi army came forward, they were trapped between the carts and the flanking attacks. The noise of the guns frightened the elephants, who turned and trampled their own side, according to the accounts. By the afternoon it was over. Ibrahim Lodi was dead. Historians point out that tactics, as much as the guns, decided the day.
[VISUAL: carts and cannon, flank attack, elephants fleeing]
Babur took Delhi and Agra within days. The Delhi Sultanate was finished, and a new dynasty was born. Babur himself did not yet know whether he would stay.

## 5. KHANWA AND THE FIGHT FOR HINDUSTAN (9:00–12:00)
**NARRATION**
His men did not want to stay in India. It was hot, the people were strange to them, and they wanted to go home to Kabul. Babur, in the Baburnama, writes of his difficulties. He did not like many things about Hindustan, as he called it. He found it without good horses, grapes, ice, or running water, and without the pleasant gardens he loved. But he saw its wealth, and he decided to remain.
[VISUAL: soldiers wanting to leave, Babur persuading them]
The greater danger came from the Rajputs. Rana Sanga of Mewar, a veteran warrior who had defeated Delhi sultans in the past, formed a large confederacy. On 16 or 17 March 1527 at Khanwa, near Agra, Babur faced him. Sources say Sanga's army had a large numerical advantage, with figures up to eighty thousand men and five hundred elephants, though these are contested.
[VISUAL: Rana Sanga, the confederacy of Rajput chiefs]
Babur's army was shaken. According to his own account, he gave up drinking wine, broke his wine cups, and called on his men to swear on the Quran to fight to the end. He declared the war a holy war and took the title ghazi. Again he used artillery, carts and flanking cavalry. Sanga was beaten, and some of his allies were divided and uncertain. Rana Sanga died in 1528.
[VISUAL: Babur breaking wine cups, battle of Khanwa]
In 1528 Babur took Chanderi, and in 1529, at Ghaghra, he defeated the Afghan chiefs of the east. By then he ruled from the Oxus to Bihar.

## 6. THE MAN AND HIS BOOK (12:00–15:00)
**NARRATION**
What kind of man was Babur? We know him better than almost any ruler of his age, because he told us himself. The Baburnama, written in Chagatai Turkic, his native language, is one of the first autobiographies in the Islamic world. It is honest in a way that most royal chronicles are not.
[VISUAL: Baburnama manuscript]
He describes his battles and mistakes. He writes about his love of nature, with careful descriptions of the plants and animals of Afghanistan and India. He describes the fruits he missed, the melons of Fergana, and writes about the people he met. He writes poetry, and speaks of friendship, wine, and grief. He admits his failures, including how he lost Samarkand.
He was a strong man, according to his own account. He once carried two men on his shoulders, one under each arm, and ran along a rampart, jumping the gaps in the battlements. This is his own story, and he seems to have enjoyed telling it.
[VISUAL: Babur carrying two men on the wall, illustrated]
He did not have time to build a state. He had no system of administration, and no clear plan for succession. But he laid the foundation: an army using firearms, a base in Agra, and a record of success.

## 7. DEATH AND LEGACY (15:00–17:00)
**NARRATION**
Babur died at Agra on 26 December 1530, aged forty-seven, probably from illness. A popular story says that when his son Humayun lay dying, Babur walked around his bed, offering his own life in exchange, and that Humayun recovered while Babur fell ill. This is a tradition, told in later sources, and cannot be confirmed.
[VISUAL: Babur walking around a bed, labelled tradition]
He was first buried in Agra, and later, according to his wish, his remains were moved to Kabul, to a garden, which is still known as Bagh-e Babur.
Humayun inherited the throne, and soon lost it to Sher Shah. But Humayun's son Akbar would win it back and make the empire that Babur began.
[VISUAL: Bagh-e Babur, family line: Babur, Humayun, Akbar]
