# Ep 19 — Louis XIV: The Sun King
Target: ~10 min (1501 words) · Status: DRAFT — must pass fact-check (docs/04) before voiceover

## 1. HOOK (0:00–0:50)
**NARRATION**
In 1715, a very old man died at the palace of Versailles. He had been king for 72 years and 110 days, the longest reign of any monarch in European history. When he took the throne, he was four years old. Over those seven decades, he made France the strongest country in Europe, built the most famous palace in the world, and led it into wars that ruined its finances.
[VISUAL: the Hall of Mirrors, an old king's portrait]
He is known as Louis XIV, the Sun King. In this video, we follow his rise from a child king who ran away from rebels to the monarch who made the nobles of France his courtiers. And we will ask what the cost was.
[VISUAL: sun emblem, map of France]

## 2. A CHILD KING AND THE FRONDE (0:50–3:30)
**NARRATION**
Louis was born on 5 September 1638 after twenty-two years of childless marriage between Louis XIII and Anne of Austria. He was called Louis-Dieudonne, Louis the God-given. His father died in May 1643, and the four-year-old became king. The regent was Anne of Austria, and her chief minister was a cardinal from Italy, Jules Mazarin.
[VISUAL: baby Louis, Anne of Austria, Cardinal Mazarin]
France was fighting the Thirty Years' War against Spain and the Habsburgs. War costs led to heavy taxes, and the government was unpopular. In 1648, the Parlement of Paris, the highest court, and the nobles revolted. This was the Fronde, named after the slings that street boys used to throw stones at windows.
In January 1649, the royal family had to flee Paris in the middle of the night. Louis, ten years old, was brought to Saint-Germain, and slept on straw. He is said to have never forgotten the humiliation. The Fronde went on until 1653. It taught Louis to distrust Paris, the nobles and the Parlement.
[VISUAL: barricades in Paris, royal family fleeing by night]
He was declared of age at thirteen in 1651. But Mazarin ran the government until his death on 9 March 1661. The next day, the twenty-two-year-old king called his ministers and told them that from now on he would be his own first minister.

## 3. THE PERSONAL RULE (3:30–7:00)
**NARRATION**
Louis took his duty seriously. He worked up to eight hours a day. His rule was organised in a council system, with ministers from the middle class, not great nobles. This was deliberate.
[VISUAL: Louis at his desk, council of ministers]
His first act was to arrest Nicolas Fouquet, the superintendent of finance, in September 1661. Fouquet had built a magnificent chateau at Vaux-le-Vicomte and held a party there in August, to show off. Louis saw it as a challenge. Fouquet was tried and imprisoned for life.
Louis chose Jean-Baptiste Colbert as his finance minister. Colbert reformed taxes, built factories and supported trade. He founded companies to trade with the East and West Indies, built a navy of over 100 ships, and promoted industries like tapestries at Gobelins, and mirrors. He also paid for the Canal du Midi, completed in 1681, linking the Atlantic and the Mediterranean.
[VISUAL: Fouquet's arrest, Colbert, Canal du Midi]
The war minister, the Marquis de Louvois, built a large professional army. By the 1690s, the French army had around 400,000 men at war, the largest in Europe since Roman times. Military engineer Vauban designed fortresses along the borders, and perfected siege warfare.
[VISUAL: Louvois, Vauban's fortress plans]
Louis also centralised power by sending out intendants, royal officials with wide powers, into the provinces. They collected taxes and supervised the courts. This eroded the power of local nobles.

## 4. VERSAILLES AND THE SUN KING (7:00–10:30)
**NARRATION**
The Sun King image started in 1662, when Louis danced in a ballet dressed as the sun. The sun was a perfect symbol: it gave light to all, and everything turned around it.
[VISUAL: ballet costume, sun emblem]
Louis's hunting lodge at Versailles, 20 km west of Paris, was gradually transformed into a palace. The work started in the 1660s under the architect Louis Le Vau, with gardens by Andre Le Notre and interiors by Charles Le Brun. Jules Hardouin-Mansart built the Hall of Mirrors, with 357 mirrors, between 1678 and 1684. In 1682, Louis moved the court there permanently.
[VISUAL: Versailles from the air, Hall of Mirrors, gardens]
The palace housed around 3,000 courtiers and perhaps up to 10,000 servants and soldiers. The nobility were invited, almost forced, to live there. Daily life was a ritual: the king's rising and going to bed were public ceremonies, called the lever and coucher, and it was an honour to hand him his shirt. Rank was shown by who could sit, who could stand, and who could carry the candlestick.
[VISUAL: the levee ritual, courtiers]
Historians have debated why Louis did this. The traditional view is that he wanted to tame the nobility by keeping them occupied with court politics and far from their provinces. Newer research, by scholars like Jeroen Duindam, points out that the court was also a place where nobles bargained for favours, and the king depended on them. The cost was huge: at its peak, Versailles took about 5 to 6 percent of the royal budget, not the 50 percent that was once claimed.
Louis was also the patron of great artists. Molière's comedies, Racine's tragedies, Lully's music and Lebrun's paintings made France the cultural centre of Europe. He founded the Academies of Sciences and of Painting. French became the language of diplomacy.
[VISUAL: Molière, Lully, Racine]

## 5. LOUIS AND WAR (10:30–14:00)
**NARRATION**
Louis fought wars for nearly all of his personal reign. He saw glory as the king's duty.
[VISUAL: map of Louis's wars]
The War of Devolution in 1667 and 1668 gained him part of the Spanish Netherlands. The Dutch War in 1672 to 1678 started with a French invasion of the Netherlands. The Dutch opened the dikes and flooded the country. William of Orange organised a coalition against France. At the end, the Treaty of Nijmegen gave Louis Franche-Comte.
In the 1680s, Louis grabbed territory by legal claims, in the so-called Reunions, including Strasbourg in 1681. This alarmed Europe. The Nine Years' War, 1688 to 1697, pitted France against a Grand Alliance led by William of Orange, who was by then King of England. It ended without clear winners.
[VISUAL: Dutch flood defence, Strasbourg, William of Orange]
The last and costliest conflict was the War of the Spanish Succession, from 1701 to 1714. The Spanish king Charles II died childless in 1700 and left his throne to Louis's grandson, Philip of Anjou. The rest of Europe feared a Franco-Spanish empire. For thirteen years, France fought England, the Dutch and the Habsburgs. The English commander, the Duke of Marlborough, won big victories, at Blenheim in 1704, Ramillies in 1706 and Oudenarde in 1708. France was nearly bankrupt.
[VISUAL: Blenheim battle, map of Spanish empire]
In the end, the Treaty of Utrecht in 1713 allowed Philip to keep Spain, but only if the crowns were never united. France lost land in North America and some other territories to Britain.

## 6. THE DARK SIDE (14:00–16:30)
**NARRATION**
Louis's France had a harsh side. In 1685, he revoked the Edict of Nantes, the law of 1598 that had given French Protestants, the Huguenots, the right to worship. His reasons were religious and political: he wanted unity in the kingdom, and he thought that he was winning back lost souls. Protestant churches were destroyed, and children were taken to be raised as Catholics. Around 200,000 Huguenots fled abroad, taking skills and capital to England, the Netherlands and Prussia.
[VISUAL: Huguenots fleeing, closed church]
In the 1690s and 1700s, the country also suffered terrible famines, especially in 1693 to 1694 and in the winter of 1709. Historians estimate that around 1.3 million people died in the first crisis. The weather was a factor, but so were war taxes and requisitions.
Taxes were heavy. The nobility and clergy were largely exempt, and the burden fell on peasants. By 1715 the state's debt was enormous, and France was in a deep financial crisis.
[VISUAL: famine, tax collectors, empty granary]

## 7. THE END OF THE REIGN (16:30–18:00)
**NARRATION**
Louis's personal life was long and complicated. He had a queen, Maria Theresa of Spain, and many mistresses, including Louise de La Valliere, Madame de Montespan and finally Madame de Maintenon, whom he secretly married after the queen died in 1683. He had many children, though most legitimate ones died young. His son, the Dauphin, died in 1711, and his grandson in 1712. In 1712, his great-grandson was the only surviving direct heir.
[VISUAL: family tree with deaths, Madame de Maintenon]
Louis died on 1 September 1715, a few days before his seventy-seventh birthday, of gangrene in his leg. On his deathbed, he told his great-grandson and heir, then five years old: I have loved war too much. Do not imitate me. The quote is recorded in the Marquis de Dangeau's journal, but historians disagree about its accuracy.
[VISUAL: deathbed with child heir]
The child became Louis XV. The famous quote, I am the state, attributed to Louis, was not said by him, according to historians. It is a later invention.

## 8. LEGACY AND DEBATE (18:00–19:30)
**NARRATION**
Louis XIV set the model of absolute monarchy in Europe. Other kings copied Versailles. But France's finances and the royal prestige were damaged, and many historians argue that his heirs could not repair them. Some see a line from his reign to the French Revolution in 1789, though others think this is too simple.
[VISUAL: Versailles copied across Europe, line to 1789]
Historians still ask whether he was truly an absolute monarch, or if he had to bargain with elites. Was the revocation of the Edict of Nantes a disaster? Did his wars make France stronger or weaker?
Our final video in this series brings us to the man who said he found the French crown on the ground and picked it up with the point of his sword: Napoleon. Subscribe, and tell me which king you want after that.
[VISUAL: end screen]
