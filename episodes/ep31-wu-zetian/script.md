# Ep 31 — Wu Zetian: The Only Woman Emperor of China
Target: ~9 min (1311 words) · Status: DRAFT, fact-checked by web search (see factcheck/); still needs a human check against the cited books

## 1. HOOK (0:00–0:50)
**NARRATION**
In 690, in Luoyang, a sixty-six-year-old woman took the throne of China in her own name. She renamed the dynasty from Tang to Zhou, adopted the title Emperor, and ruled for fifteen years. She is the only woman in more than two thousand years of imperial Chinese history to rule as emperor in her own right.
[VISUAL: Luoyang court, a woman in imperial robes]
Her enemies said she murdered her own baby daughter, poisoned her rivals, and tortured officials. Her defenders say she ran a competent government, supported the arts, and was blamed by male historians who could not accept a woman ruler. Her tomb has a monument with no inscription at all. This is the story of Wu Zetian.
[VISUAL: the blank stele at Qianling]

## 2. FROM CONCUBINE TO EMPRESS (0:50–4:00)
**NARRATION**
Wu was born in 624 or 625, the daughter of a timber merchant who had become a Tang official, Wu Shihuo. Her family was not part of the old aristocracy. She was clever and well read, and at 14 she was brought into the palace as a low-ranking concubine of Emperor Taizong, with the title Cairen, and the nickname Mei, beauty.
[VISUAL: young Wu entering the palace]
A story says that Taizong had a wild horse called Lion Beauty that nobody could tame. Wu told him she could, with three things: an iron whip, an iron hammer, and a dagger. First she would whip it, then hit its head, then cut its throat. The story is from an official history, and many historians think it is invented to show Wu's ruthlessness.
When Taizong died in 649, his concubines without children were sent to a Buddhist nunnery, as was custom. Wu was among them. But Taizong's son, the new emperor Gaozong, had noticed her. He brought her back to the palace in 651 or 652, against all convention.
[VISUAL: Wu in a nunnery, Gaozong summoning her]
Gaozong's Empress Wang had no children, and rivalled with Consort Xiao. Wu gave birth to a daughter, who died in infancy. Official histories allege Wu strangled her baby and framed Empress Wang. Modern historians consider the story doubtful; the account appears in sources hostile to Wu, written later.
In 655, Gaozong deposed Empress Wang and Consort Xiao, accusing them of witchcraft. Wu became Empress. The old Tang ministers, including Zhangsun Wuji and Chu Suiliang, had opposed it. Over the next years, Wu's supporters had them removed, banished or forced to commit suicide.
[VISUAL: the deposed empress, ministers banished]

## 3. THE POWER BEHIND THE THRONE (4:00–7:00)
**NARRATION**
From about 660, Gaozong suffered from dizziness and headaches, perhaps from high blood pressure, and Wu began to handle government business. She sat behind a screen at court sessions. In 674 she and Gaozong took the titles of Heavenly Emperor and Heavenly Empress.
[VISUAL: Wu behind a screen, the two thrones]
Wu used her power with energy. She expanded the examination system to recruit officials from outside the great aristocratic clans. She introduced a form of palace examinations in 690 and a military exam. She issued a list of twelve proposals for reform, including lower taxes, less spending on war, and respect for mothers by extending mourning for them to the same length as for fathers.
She supported Buddhism, and in 672 donated to the carving of the huge Vairocana Buddha at the Longmen Grottoes. It is said that the face was modelled on hers. Historians can't prove it.
[VISUAL: the exam hall, the Vairocana Buddha at Longmen]
Under her, Tang armies conquered the Korean kingdoms of Baekje in 660 and Goguryeo in 668, with Silla as an ally. But Silla then drove the Chinese out of the peninsula by 676, and Tibetan forces defeated China in 670.

## 4. THE EMPRESS DOWAGER (7:00–10:00)
**NARRATION**
Gaozong died in December 683. The throne passed to Wu's son Li Zhe, Emperor Zhongzong. But when he tried to promote his wife's family, Wu deposed him after only 54 days in early 684 and put another son, Li Dan, Emperor Ruizong, on the throne, keeping him a prisoner in the palace. She ruled as Empress Dowager.
[VISUAL: Zhongzong deposed, Ruizong shut in the palace]
Opposition grew. In 684, the grandson of the famous general Li Ji, Li Jingye, rebelled in Yangzhou. His forces numbered about 100,000. The rebellion was crushed within three months. A famous manifesto by the poet Luo Binwang denounced her, and the story goes that when she read the line, "Look at today's China, whose is it?", she asked who wrote it, and, hearing it was Luo Binwang, said it was the chancellor's fault that such a talent had not been employed.
[VISUAL: the rebellion, Luo Binwang's manifesto]
To find traitors, she established a network of informers. She installed a bronze box in the capital, with slots, where people could drop in anonymous denunciations. And she gave huge power to secret police: officials like Suo Yuanli, Zhou Xing and Lai Junchen, who developed techniques for forced confessions. Lai Junchen's Manual of the Net of Accusations was a guide to torture. The Tang imperial family, great ministers, and generals were killed or exiled in the hundreds, perhaps thousands. Lai Junchen was himself executed in 697, and the people celebrated.
[VISUAL: the bronze box, informers, the reign of terror]

## 5. EMPEROR OF THE ZHOU (10:00–12:30)
**NARRATION**
In 690, at sixty-five, Wu took the final step. Officials, monks and ordinary people, orchestrated by her supporters, petitioned her to take the throne. Monks produced a Buddhist text, the Great Cloud Sutra, which said that a goddess would be reborn as a ruler. She set aside Ruizong, moved to the Zhou dynasty, a name taken from an ancient dynasty, and proclaimed herself Emperor, Huangdi, not Empress, with the name Shengshen Huangdi, Holy and Divine Emperor.
[VISUAL: the Great Cloud Sutra, her proclamation]
She made Luoyang the capital, built a vast ceremonial hall called the Mingtang in 688, and made new Chinese characters, such as the one for the word for sun and moon, to be used in writing. She also changed the names of government offices.
She was strict and energetic. She appointed excellent ministers, like Di Renjie, a famous judge, Yao Chong and Song Jing. And she promoted people for talent, even if they had been critics, such as Di Renjie. When he was accused of treason, he endured it, and later became her chancellor.
[VISUAL: the Mingtang, Di Renjie]

## 6. THE LAST YEARS (12:30–15:00)
**NARRATION**
Wu was getting old. She took two young men, the Zhang brothers, Zhang Yizhi and Zhang Changzong, as her companions. Officials were angry at their power. In 698, Wu recalled her exiled son, Zhongzong, to be heir, after a period in which her nephews had hoped to succeed. A famous minister argued that a nephew would never sacrifice to an aunt, but a son would sacrifice to his mother.
[VISUAL: the Zhang brothers, Zhongzong recalled]
In early 705, Wu fell ill. Officials led by Zhang Jianzhi staged a coup on about 20 February. They killed the Zhang brothers and surrounded the palace. Wu was forced to abdicate in favour of Zhongzong. The Tang dynasty was restored. She died on 16 December 705, aged about 81, and was buried at Qianling, the tomb that she shared with her husband Gaozong. Her last request was to be buried as Gaozong's wife, and to have her name changed from Emperor to Empress.
[VISUAL: coup at the palace, Qianling mausoleum]
At Qianling, there is a huge stone stele with no inscription. Some say she ordered it left blank so that history would judge her. Others say that the inscription was never completed, or erased later. Historians do not know.
[VISUAL: the blank stele]

## 7. LEGACY AND DEBATE (15:00–17:30)
**NARRATION**
Wu's sources are hostile. The main histories, the Old and New Book of Tang, were compiled by Confucian men who believed that a woman on the throne was against nature. The stories of her crimes, such as the murder of her own infant daughter and the poisoning of her son, are suspect.
[VISUAL: Confucian historians writing]
Modern historians, such as Jonathan Spence, Richard Guisso and Denis Twitchett, have reassessed her. She was ruthless. She had enemies killed and used terror. But she also ruled competently, kept the borders, expanded the exam system, and left the economy in decent shape. Under her, the population continued to grow.
Is her image as a monster fair? Or is it the product of misogyny? Historians also ask whether she planned to take the throne from the start, or whether events pushed her.
[VISUAL: two portraits, villain and ruler]
Next, we reach the last great dynasty. A Manchu emperor who was crowned at seven and ruled for 61 years: Kangxi. Subscribe, and tell me which king you want after that.
[VISUAL: end screen]
