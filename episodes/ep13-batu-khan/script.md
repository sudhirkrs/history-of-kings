# Ep 13 — Batu Khan: The Mongols Arrive in Russia
Target: ~9 min (1360 words) · Status: DRAFT, fact-checked by web search (see factcheck/); still needs a human check against the cited books

## 1. HOOK (0:00–0:50)
**NARRATION**
It is December 1237, and the river Oka is frozen solid. Across the ice comes an army that the Russians have never seen: tens of thousands of riders on small, shaggy horses. They camp outside the town of Ryazan, and send envoys to demand submission and tribute. In the Tale of the Destruction of Ryazan, a literary work written afterwards, the demand is for a tenth of everything, and the prince's proud answer is: when none of us are left, then all will be yours. Historians treat the tale as a mixture of memory and legend.
[VISUAL: frozen river, a Mongol army, Ryazan's walls]
Five days later, Ryazan was burned to the ground. The army was led by Batu, a grandson of Genghis Khan. Over the next three years, he would conquer almost all of Russia, and found the khanate that would rule it for over two centuries.
[VISUAL: map of Kievan Rus' falling]
This is the story of Batu Khan, and the beginning of the Mongol rule over Russia.

## 2. THE RUS' BEFORE THE MONGOLS (0:50–3:00)
**NARRATION**
In the early 1200s, the Russian lands were known as Rus'. They had once been united under Kiev, with its famous prince Vladimir the Great, who adopted Christianity in 988 and Yaroslav the Wise, who wrote the first law code. But after Yaroslav died in 1054, the lands were divided among his descendants.
[VISUAL: Kiev, St Sophia cathedral, Yaroslav the Wise, map of principalities]
By the 1200s, there were a dozen major principalities: Kiev, Chernigov, Galicia-Volhynia, Suzdal, Vladimir, Ryazan, and the republic of Novgorod in the north. The princes fought each other constantly. Trade had shifted to other routes, and Kiev was no longer the great city it had been.
The Rus' were therefore weak and divided when the Mongols arrived. They had had a warning. In 1223 Subutai and Jebe, on their long raid, had crushed an alliance of Russian princes and Cumans at the Kalka river. Then the Mongols disappeared. Many Russians thought it was a freak event, like a plague. They were wrong.
[VISUAL: Kalka River battle, Mongols vanishing eastward]

## 3. WHO WAS BATU? (3:00–5:00)
**NARRATION**
Batu was born around 1205, the second son of Jochi, the eldest son of Genghis Khan, whose paternity was always questioned. When Jochi died in 1225, his sons inherited the western territories, which lay between the Irtysh river and the "farthest extent of Mongol horses' hooves", that is, lands that remained to be conquered. Batu took the lead, his older brother Orda taking the eastern portion.
[VISUAL: Jochi's family tree, Batu and Orda]
In 1235, the kurultai decided on a major campaign west. Batu was the commander in name, but the real strategist was the aged general Subutai. Batu was a good commander, though some sources describe him as cautious, with a mild nature. Nonetheless, he was a firm ruler.
In 1236 the Mongols attacked the Volga Bulgars and destroyed their cities. By 1237 they had also subdued the Kipchaks, or Cumans, many of whom fled westward to Hungary.
[VISUAL: Volga Bulgar city in flames, fleeing Cumans]

## 4. THE WINTER CAMPAIGN (5:00–8:30)
**NARRATION**
In the winter of 1237, Batu crossed the frozen rivers into north-eastern Russia.
[VISUAL: Mongol army on the ice]
Ryazan fell on 21 December 1237 after five days of siege. The Russian chronicles describe the horrors. A knight named Yevpaty Kolovrat, they say, gathered a small band and attacked the Mongols, and was killed after great deeds. The tale of the destruction of Ryazan, a literary work, is moving but not entirely historical.
Then came Moscow, then a small town, which was burned in January 1238. Next was Vladimir, the capital. Grand Prince Yuri II had left to raise an army. On 7 February 1238, the Mongols stormed the city, and the family of the prince died in a burning church.
[VISUAL: Moscow burned, Vladimir stormed]
On 4 March, on the river Sit, they destroyed Yuri's army, and killed him. Over a few months, they took fourteen towns.
The Mongols then headed towards Novgorod, the richest city in the north. But in the spring thaw, the land turned to marshes, which made it impossible for cavalry. About a hundred versts from Novgorod, they turned back.
Before that they encountered a small town, Kozelsk, whose boy-prince Vasily had resisted fiercely. The Russian chronicles say the siege lasted seven weeks, and that the Mongols afterwards called it the Evil City. Other sources date the siege differently, so the details are uncertain.
[VISUAL: Kozelsk siege, spring thaw]

## 5. THE FALL OF KIEV (8:30–11:30)
**NARRATION**
In 1239, Batu rested his army on the southern steppe. Then, in 1240, he attacked the south. Pereyaslavl and Chernigov fell. In the autumn, he arrived at Kiev.
[VISUAL: Kiev's golden gates, city on the hills]
Kiev, with perhaps 40,000 people, had been the mother of Russian cities. Its prince, Michael, had fled. A governor named Dmitr held the city. The Mongols brought huge siege engines, and the sound of their camp, according to the chronicle, was so loud that people in the city could not hear each other.
After a short siege, Kiev fell on 6 December 1240. Dmitr was captured, and according to a chronicle, Batu spared his life because of his bravery. The city was ravaged. When the Franciscan envoy John of Plano Carpini passed through in 1246, he said he saw countless skulls and bones in the fields, and that of the once great city, only 200 houses remained.
[VISUAL: Kiev's fall, skulls and ruins as seen by Plano Carpini]
Then the Mongols turned west and attacked Galicia-Volhynia, and the doors to Hungary and Poland were open.

## 6. EUROPE AND THE WITHDRAWAL (11:30–13:30)
**NARRATION**
In early 1241, Batu and Subutai invaded Poland and Hungary in separate columns. On 9 April, a Mongol force defeated a Polish and German army at Legnica, and Henry II of Silesia was killed. Two days later, Batu and Subutai destroyed the army of King Bela IV of Hungary near the Sajo river, near Mohi.
[VISUAL: Legnica and Mohi, Bela IV fleeing]
Hungary was devastated. Some estimates say that a quarter to a half of the population died, though historians debate the figures. The Mongols pursued King Bela to the Adriatic. Then, in early 1242, news came of the death of the Great Khan Ogedei. Batu retreated east, ravaging Bulgaria on the way. He never returned to the West.
[VISUAL: the retreat, Danube]

## 7. THE GOLDEN HORDE (13:30–16:00)
**NARRATION**
Batu did not go back to Mongolia. He chose to remain in the steppe near the lower Volga, where he founded a new capital, Sarai. Europeans called his state the Golden Horde; Russians called it the Tatar or Mongol Khanate. One theory says the name comes from the golden tent of the khan; others link it to the colour gold in Mongol symbolism.
[VISUAL: Sarai, golden tent]
Batu did not rule the Russian lands directly. Instead, he allowed the Russian princes to rule their own territories, as vassals. They had to travel to Sarai, and some even to Karakorum, to get a patent to rule, called a yarlyk. They had to pay tribute and provide troops. In the 1250s, the Mongols took a census, to fix the taxes. Russians later called this the Mongol yoke or Tatar yoke.
[VISUAL: princes at Sarai, tribute, census]
Some Russian princes used this system. Alexander Nevsky, prince of Novgorod, who, according to Russian sources, defeated the Swedes on the Neva in 1240, and who fought the Teutonic knights at Lake Peipus in April 1242, decided to cooperate with the Mongols rather than fight. He travelled to Sarai and to Karakorum. He saw that the Mongols were too strong. The decision made him a controversial figure: a hero to many, a collaborator to some.
[VISUAL: Alexander Nevsky, Lake Peipus]
Others, like Daniel of Galicia, tried to find western allies, but never got effective help.
Batu died in 1255, aged around fifty. His son Sartaq, a Christian, succeeded briefly, then Berke, Batu's brother, who converted to Islam. The Golden Horde would convert to Islam in the fourteenth century under Ozbeg Khan.

## 8. LEGACY AND DEBATE (16:00–18:00)
**NARRATION**
The Mongol rule shaped Russia. The princes of Moscow, who collected taxes for the Mongols, grew rich and powerful, and eventually took over from the Horde. The Mongols also left traces in the Russian language, the postal system, military organisation and taxation. At the same time, Russia was cut off from Europe for some two centuries.
[VISUAL: Moscow's rise, loanwords, postal station]
Historians disagree about the effects. The older view, from the nineteenth century, saw the Mongol yoke as a catastrophe, which delayed Russian development. A newer view, put forward by scholars such as Charles Halperin and Donald Ostrowski, argues that Mongol rule was less destructive in the long run, and gave Moscow its model of autocracy. Others, like Janet Martin, stress the trade and wealth. The debate continues.
[VISUAL: two schools on cards]
