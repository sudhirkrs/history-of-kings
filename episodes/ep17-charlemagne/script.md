# Ep 17 — Charlemagne: The Father of Europe

## 1. HOOK (0:00–0:50)
**NARRATION**
On Christmas Day in the year 800, in St Peter's Basilica in Rome, a tall king of the Franks knelt in prayer. The Pope, Leo III, stepped forward, placed a crown on his head, and the crowd cried out: life and victory to Charles, the most pious Augustus, crowned by God, great and peace-giving emperor of the Romans.
[VISUAL: St Peter's, kneeling king, Pope with crown]
There had been no emperor in the West for over three hundred years. The man was Charlemagne, Charles the Great. His biographer Einhard says he was surprised, and would not have entered the church that day if he had known what the Pope planned. Historians doubt this. In this video, we follow his wars, his empire, his reforms and what his coronation really meant.
[VISUAL: Charlemagne portrait, map of his empire]

## 2. THE FRANKS BEFORE CHARLES (0:50–3:00)
**NARRATION**
After the Western Roman Empire fell in 476, Germanic kings took over its provinces. The most successful were the Franks, who under Clovis around 500 became Christians, and held much of Gaul, today's France, and parts of Germany.
[VISUAL: map of the Frankish kingdom, Clovis baptism]
By the 700s, the Merovingian kings were weak, and the real power was held by their chief officials, the mayors of the palace. One of them, Charles Martel, defeated a Muslim raiding army near Tours in 732, though historians today see it as one battle in a longer series. His son Pepin the Short in 751 deposed the last Merovingian and became king, with the Pope's approval. In return, Pepin protected the Pope against the Lombards in Italy, and gave him land that became the Papal States.
[VISUAL: Charles Martel at Tours, Pepin crowned, Pope Stephen]
Charlemagne was Pepin's son. He was born around 742 to 748; the exact year is uncertain, because no one recorded it. When Pepin died in 768, the kingdom was divided between his sons, Charles and Carloman. Then in 771 Carloman died suddenly, and Charles became the sole king.

## 3. WAR AND CONQUEST (3:00–8:30)
**NARRATION**
Einhard says that Charlemagne fought fifty-three campaigns in forty-six years. Most were in the spring and summer, once the horses could graze and the roads were dry.
[VISUAL: campaign map with arrows]
First, Italy. In 773, Pope Hadrian asked for help against the Lombard king Desiderius. Charlemagne crossed the Alps and besieged Pavia. In 774 the city fell, and Charles took the title King of the Lombards.
[VISUAL: Alpine passage, siege of Pavia]
Second, Spain. In 778 he led an army into Spain, hoping to gain land from the Muslim rulers. The campaign failed. On the way home, his rearguard was ambushed in the Pyrenees at Roncesvalles. The attackers were Basques. The event inspired the later epic Song of Roland, in which the attackers became Muslim Saracens. The real Roland, Hruodland, Count of the Breton March, is just a name in Einhard.
[VISUAL: Roncesvalles ambush, Song of Roland manuscript]
Third, the Saxons. The longest war was with the pagan Saxons in northern Germany, from 772 to 804. Charles insisted on baptism and submission. In 782, at Verden, he ordered the execution of about 4,500 Saxon prisoners, according to the Royal Frankish Annals. Historians debate the number and the details, but the massacre was real. In 785, he issued a law code that made death the penalty for refusing baptism. Eventually the Saxons were defeated and absorbed.
[VISUAL: Saxon forest, Verden, capitulary text]
Fourth, the Avars. Between 791 and 796, he destroyed the Avar khanate in the Danube basin, and seized a huge treasure, which the Annals say needed fifteen wagons, each pulled by four oxen. This wealth financed his court.
[VISUAL: Avar ring fortress, treasure wagons]
In all, he roughly doubled the Frankish realm. It covered modern France, Belgium, the Netherlands, Germany, Austria, Switzerland, much of Italy and a strip of north-east Spain.

## 4. THE CORONATION OF 800 (8:30–11:30)
**NARRATION**
In 799, Pope Leo III was attacked by Roman nobles, who beat him and accused him of crimes. He fled to Charlemagne for protection. Charles sent him back to Rome with an escort. In November 800, Charles himself went to Rome, and held a council that cleared the Pope's name.
[VISUAL: Pope Leo III's flight, Charlemagne's arrival in Rome]
Then, on 25 December 800, in the Basilica, the Pope crowned Charles Emperor. What was the meaning of the act?
Einhard writes that Charles said he would not have gone into the church, had he known the Pope's plan. Some historians think Charles was truly irritated, because the Pope had taken the initiative, implying that the imperial title was the Pope's gift. Others think the whole ceremony was planned in advance, and Einhard was just trying to protect Charlemagne's dignity.
[VISUAL: two interpretations, Pope-led and Charles-led]
There was also a rival power: the Byzantine Empire in Constantinople, which still claimed to be the Roman Empire. In 800, the throne was held by the Empress Irene, who had blinded and deposed her own son. Some in the West said the throne was vacant, since a woman could not be emperor. Eventually, in 812, after years of negotiation, Byzantine envoys greeted Charles as emperor at Aachen, though not as emperor of the Romans, a title the Byzantines kept for themselves.
Whatever the intention, the coronation created a model. A western emperor, chosen by the Pope, heir to Rome, defender of the Church. It would shape Europe for a thousand years, ending only in 1806, with the end of the Holy Roman Empire.
[VISUAL: Irene and the Byzantines, timeline to 1806]

## 5. GOVERNING AN EMPIRE (11:30–14:30)
**NARRATION**
How do you rule such a vast realm with no roads, no postal service and few literate officials? Charlemagne's answer was a mix of personal rule and system.
[VISUAL: map with counties and borders]
He divided the realm into hundreds of counties, each under a count, who held court, raised troops and collected fines. Along the borders were marches under margraves. Counts were often moved around to avoid local roots.
To check them, he sent out pairs of royal envoys, the missi dominici, one lay and one cleric. They travelled each year through a district, hearing complaints, reading out laws and checking on the counts.
[VISUAL: missi dominici on the road]
His laws were written as capitularies, short orders divided into chapters. They covered everything from church reform to weights and measures to the size of the royal estates, and even to the management of his hens and eggs. The most famous is the Capitulare de villis, a list of rules for his farms.
He reformed the money. He introduced the silver denarius, or penny, as the main coin, with a weight standard. The system, based on a pound divided into twenty shillings of twelve pennies each, lasted in Britain until 1971.
[VISUAL: silver denier, pound, shilling, pence chart]
Aachen became his main residence, with a palace and a chapel, finished around 800, modelled on Ravenna's San Vitale. It still stands.
[VISUAL: Palatine Chapel at Aachen]

## 6. THE CAROLINGIAN RENAISSANCE (14:30–17:00)
**NARRATION**
Charlemagne could speak Frankish and Latin, and understand Greek. He loved books, though he struggled to write. Einhard says he kept tablets under his pillow to practise letters, but started too late and made little progress.
[VISUAL: Charlemagne trying to write, tablets]
In 780 or 781 he met the English scholar Alcuin of York in Parma, and by 782 had invited him to the court. Alcuin led a palace school and then became abbot of Tours. Scholars from Italy, Spain and Ireland came too. They copied the classic works of Rome; very many Latin classics survive only because they were copied in Carolingian monasteries.
A new, clear handwriting called Carolingian minuscule was developed, with lower-case letters, spaces between words and punctuation. It became the base of the lower-case letters we use today.
[VISUAL: Carolingian minuscule script, monastery scriptorium]
In 789, he ordered every monastery and cathedral to open a school to teach boys to read. He tried to standardise the liturgy, correct the Bible text and bring order to the Church.
Historians call this the Carolingian Renaissance. It was a revival of learning in a poor, rural society. It was not a mass literacy movement, but it saved a lot of ancient writing.

## 7. THE MAN AND THE END (17:00–19:00)
**NARRATION**
What was he like? Einhard, who knew him, describes a tall, strong man, with a high-pitched voice, a thick neck, a big belly and white hair. A 2010 study of a leg bone, his tibia, estimated his height at about 1.84 metres, very tall for his time. He loved hunting, swimming and hot springs. He wore Frankish dress, not Roman robes, except twice, when the Pope asked. He had four official wives in turn, several concubines, and, depending on the count, eighteen to twenty children.
[VISUAL: portrait based on Einhard, Frankish dress]
Charlemagne died on 28 January 814 in Aachen, aged about seventy. Einhard says he fell ill with a fever and a pain in his side, which some have read as pneumonia. Modern tests on his bones found no clear answer. He was buried in the Palatine Chapel. Only one of his legitimate sons survived him: Louis the Pious.
[VISUAL: Aachen funeral]
Louis could not hold the empire together. After years of civil war, the Treaty of Verdun in 843 divided it into three parts, among Louis's three sons. The western part became France, the eastern part became Germany, and the middle part was fought over for centuries.
[VISUAL: Treaty of Verdun map: West Francia, Middle Francia, East Francia]

## 8. LEGACY AND DEBATE (19:00–20:30)
**NARRATION**
Charlemagne is claimed as a founder by both France and Germany. In the 1950s the Charlemagne Prize was set up for those who promote European unity. In 1165 he was declared a saint by an antipope. He appears on the Chanson de Roland, in tapestries and in the Aachen shrine.
[VISUAL: Charlemagne statue, Charlemagne Prize]
Historians still discuss several questions. Was his empire a real state, or a personal rule that depended on his energy? Was the coronation a surprise? How far was the Carolingian Renaissance a break or a continuation? And how should we remember the violence of the Saxon wars?
