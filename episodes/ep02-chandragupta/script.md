# Ep 2 — Chandragupta Maurya: The Man Who Built India's First Empire
Target: ~19 min · Status: DRAFT — must pass fact-check (docs/04) before voiceover

## 1. HOOK (0:00–0:45)
**NARRATION**
About 2,300 years ago, a young man with no royal throne is said to have stood in front of Alexander the Great's army. A few years later, that same man ruled more land than any Indian ruler before him. He forced the successor of Alexander's empire to sign a treaty. And at the end of his life, according to Jain tradition, he gave up the throne and starved himself to death as a monk.
[VISUAL: Alexander's army silhouette → empire map expanding → lone monk on a hill]
This is Chandragupta Maurya. But here is the strange part. We know less about him than about his grandson Ashoka. There are no royal edicts from his time. His story comes from Greek writers, Indian texts and legend. In this video we will build the story piece by piece and tell you which piece is solid and which is not.

## 2. THE WORLD BEFORE HIM (0:45–3:30)
**NARRATION**
Around 400 BCE, north India was divided into many kingdoms and republics. The most powerful was Magadha, in the Ganges plain, in today's Bihar.
[VISUAL: map of 16 mahajanapadas fading to Magadha]
By about 345 BCE, the Nanda dynasty ruled Magadha. Greek writers say the last Nanda king, known in Indian sources as Dhana Nanda, had a huge army: tens of thousands of cavalry and infantry, and thousands of elephants. The numbers are probably exaggerated, but the message is clear. He was rich and feared.
[VISUAL: Nanda army, elephants, gold coins (punch-marked)]
Then, in 326 BCE, Alexander the Great crossed the Indus. He defeated the king Porus at the Hydaspes river and pushed on. But his soldiers refused to march further east. Alexander turned back, and left India in 325 BCE. He died in Babylon in 323 BCE.
[VISUAL: Alexander route map, return]
He left behind governors in the north-west. But after his death, his generals fought over his empire. This created a power gap in the north-west, and that is where our story begins.

## 3. WHO WAS CHANDRAGUPTA? (3:30–6:30)
**NARRATION**
The first problem: we don't actually know where he came from.
[VISUAL: question mark over a map]
Different traditions give different answers. Buddhist texts from Sri Lanka say he belonged to a clan called the Moriyas, who lived in the Himalayan foothills. The word is linked to peacocks, which is why peacock imagery appears in Mauryan art.
The play *Mudrarakshasa*, written many centuries later, hints at a humble background and calls him "Vrishala". Some Brahmanical sources suggest he was Nanda-related. Jain sources call him a Kshatriya.
[VISUAL: four manuscript cards, each a different tradition]
Historians cannot settle this. What the sources do agree on is this: he was ambitious, he was young, and he had help.
The Greek writer Plutarch even says that as a young man, called "Androcottus", Chandragupta saw Alexander in person. He says the young man later claimed that Alexander could easily have conquered the Nandas, because the king was hated for being cruel and of low birth. This is a Greek report, written centuries later, so we treat it carefully. But it shows that Greeks knew his name.

## 4. CHANAKYA: HISTORY OR LEGEND? (6:30–9:00)
**NARRATION**
Now the second problem: his famous teacher.
[VISUAL: Chanakya illustration, labelled "traditional depiction"]
Indian tradition says Chandragupta was guided by a Brahmin scholar named Chanakya, also called Kautilya or Vishnugupta. The story goes that Chanakya was insulted by Dhana Nanda, vowed revenge, and found Chandragupta to carry it out.
This story appears in later texts, such as the Puranas, Buddhist and Jain works and the play *Mudrarakshasa*. Notably, the Greek writers never mention Chanakya.
The *Arthashastra*, the great Indian book on statecraft, is traditionally credited to Kautilya. But modern scholars believe it was compiled over time, possibly from the Mauryan period to the early centuries CE. So we can say: Chanakya may well have been real, but the exact details of his role come from later stories.
[VISUAL: Arthashastra manuscript; "authorship and date debated"]
Whatever the truth, the pair is one of the most famous partnerships in Indian history.

## 5. THE RISE: NORTH-WEST AND MAGADHA (9:00–13:00)
**NARRATION**
How did he rise? Our sources disagree on the order.
Greek writers like Justin say Chandragupta first raised a force in the north-west, fought Alexander's Greek governors and freed the region. Indian sources emphasise the war against the Nandas.
[VISUAL: two-arrow map: north-west first / Magadha first]
Historians usually combine these: around 321 BCE, Chandragupta took Magadha from the Nandas, and made Pataliputra his capital. Around the same period he took control of the north-west.
The Buddhist and Jain tradition describes a long war. The Mudrarakshasa shows political intrigue, with spies and double agents. These details are literary, but they show how people later remembered him: as a man who won by cunning and force.
[VISUAL: Pataliputra on the Ganges; wooden palisade reconstruction]
By about 305 BCE, Chandragupta ruled a large part of northern India, from Bengal to the Indus.

## 6. SELEUCUS AND THE TREATY (13:00–15:30)
**NARRATION**
Meanwhile, in the west, one of Alexander's generals, Seleucus Nicator, had taken over Babylon and a huge part of Asia. Around 305 BCE, he marched east to reclaim Alexander's Indian lands.
[VISUAL: map of Seleucid Empire marching to the Indus]
What happened next is known only in brief. Greek writers say they made peace. Seleucus gave up territories in what is now Afghanistan and Balochistan, such as Arachosia, Gedrosia and Paropamisadae. In return, Chandragupta gave him 500 war elephants.
[VISUAL: elephants crossing river; border map]
These elephants helped Seleucus win the battle of Ipsus in 301 BCE against another of Alexander's successors. The treaty may also have included a marriage alliance, but the sources are unclear.
Seleucus then sent an ambassador, Megasthenes, to Pataliputra.

## 7. MEGASTHENES AND THE MAURYAN STATE (15:30–17:30)
**NARRATION**
Megasthenes wrote a book called *Indika*. It is lost, but later Greek and Roman writers quoted large parts of it.
[VISUAL: book icon "Indika – fragments only"]
From these fragments, we learn about a large, well-planned capital with wooden walls and many towers. A board of officials ran the city. Spies reported to the king. There were roads, markets and taxes. Some details, like Pliny's army numbers of 600,000 foot soldiers and 9,000 elephants, are probably inflated.
The Arthashastra gives a theory of how such a state works: taxes, officers, a spy network, an army and a strong king. Historians debate how far it describes the real Mauryan state, but together with the Greek reports, it gives us a rough picture.

## 8. THE END AND THE LEGACY (17:30–19:30)
**NARRATION**
Chandragupta ruled for around 24 years and died around 297 BCE.
[VISUAL: timeline end]
Jain tradition says he gave up his throne, followed the monk Bhadrabahu to South India, and died at Shravanabelagola in Karnataka by the ritual fast known as *sallekhana*. This is a strong tradition in Jainism, though historians cannot confirm it.
[VISUAL: Shravanabelagola hills (Chandragiri)]
His son Bindusara took the throne and expanded the empire, and his grandson, Ashoka, would carry it to its peak.
His legacy is clear: he united a large part of the subcontinent under one rule for the first time, and he created the template for the empire: capital, bureaucracy, army, and taxation.
[VISUAL: empire map at Chandragupta's death]
