# Ep 18 — William the Conqueror: The Bastard Who Became King of England
Target: ~11 min (1636 words) · Status: DRAFT, fact-checked by web search (see factcheck/); still needs a human check against the cited books

## 1. HOOK (0:00–0:50)
**NARRATION**
On the morning of 14 October 1066, two armies faced each other on a ridge near the coast of southern England. One was a tired English army, which had marched 400 kilometres in two weeks after beating a Viking invasion in the north. The other was a Norman army, with archers and armoured horsemen, led by a duke who was known in his youth as William the Bastard.
[VISUAL: ridge near Hastings, English shield wall and Norman cavalry]
By the evening, the English king lay dead, the shield wall was broken, and England would never be the same. William would rule England for twenty-one years, change its language, its aristocracy, its landscape and its law, and order the survey called Domesday Book. This is his story.
[VISUAL: Bayeux Tapestry panel, Domesday Book]

## 2. THE BASTARD DUKE (0:50–3:30)
**NARRATION**
William was born around 1028 in Falaise in Normandy, the son of Robert I, Duke of Normandy, and Herleva, the daughter of a local tanner or undertaker. His parents were not married, but his father named him heir. In 1034, Robert went on a pilgrimage to Jerusalem and died on the way back in 1035. William, aged seven or eight, became Duke of Normandy.
[VISUAL: Falaise castle, young William]
Normandy was a land founded by Vikings. In 911, the Viking chief Rollo was given the land around Rouen by the French king, in exchange for protection against other raiders. By William's time, the Normans spoke French, were Christian, and were among the best warriors in Europe.
[VISUAL: Rollo and the French king, Norman longships]
William's childhood was dangerous. Three of his guardians were murdered, and his tutor was killed in front of him. Barons built private castles and fought each other. As a teenager, William learned to survive by being quick, firm and cold.
In 1047, a rebellion by his own nobles was crushed at Val-es-Dunes, with help from the French king Henry I. Later, Henry turned against him, and William defeated French invasions at Mortemer in 1054 and Varaville in 1057. By the 1060s he was master of Normandy, and one of the strongest princes in France.
[VISUAL: Val-es-Dunes battle, Norman castles]
Between about 1050 and 1053 he married Matilda of Flanders, a woman of royal blood. The marriage was a success, and they had at least nine children.

## 3. THE QUESTION OF THE ENGLISH THRONE (3:30–6:30)
**NARRATION**
Across the Channel, England was ruled by Edward the Confessor, a pious king who had spent his youth in exile in Normandy. He had no children. Who would succeed him?
[VISUAL: Edward the Confessor on his throne, Westminster Abbey]
There were several claimants. The strongest in England was Harold Godwinson, Earl of Wessex, the most powerful noble. Harald Hardrada, King of Norway, claimed the throne through an old agreement. And William claimed that Edward had promised him the crown, perhaps around 1051, when he visited England. Historians doubt this: the evidence comes from Norman sources, and Edward was unlikely to promise the throne when the English earls controlled the kingdom.
Then comes a famous story. In 1064 or 1065, Harold travelled to Normandy. Why he went is a mystery; one theory is that his ship was blown off course. The Bayeux Tapestry and Norman writers say that Harold swore an oath to support William's claim, on holy relics. The English sources do not mention it. Historians are divided: the oath may have been real, or invented to justify the invasion.
[VISUAL: Harold's journey, oath on relics from the Bayeux Tapestry]
On 5 January 1066 Edward died. The next day, the Witan, the council of the English nobles, chose Harold, and he was crowned in Westminster Abbey. William was furious.

## 4. THE YEAR OF THREE KINGS (6:30–9:30)
**NARRATION**
1066 is called the year of three kings. First Edward died, then Harold, and then William was crowned.
[VISUAL: three kings, timeline]
William moved quickly. He obtained the Pope's blessing, with a banner to carry in battle, and the Pope declared the invasion a holy cause. He built a fleet of hundreds of ships, though the often-quoted figure of about 700 is uncertain, and gathered an army of perhaps 7,000 to 10,000 men. Nobody knows the exact numbers. He recruited from Normandy, Brittany, Flanders and elsewhere with the promise of land in England.
Harold held his army on the south coast through the summer, waiting for William. But the Normans were delayed by contrary winds. On 8 September, Harold had to send his men home, as the harvest and supplies ran out.
[VISUAL: fleet being built, ships waiting in harbour]
Then Harald Hardrada landed in the north with 300 ships, together with Harold's exiled brother Tostig. They beat the English earls at Fulford on 20 September. Harold marched north in four days, and surprised them at Stamford Bridge on 25 September. Hardrada and Tostig were killed, and the Vikings were crushed.
[VISUAL: Stamford Bridge battle, dying Viking king]
Three days later, the wind changed. On 28 September, William's fleet landed unopposed at Pevensey. Harold rushed south again, covering about 300 kilometres in about a week, and gathered what troops he could.

## 5. THE BATTLE OF HASTINGS (9:30–13:00)
**NARRATION**
On 14 October, Harold took up a position on Senlac Hill, about 10 kilometres from Hastings. His army, perhaps 7,000 men, was made up of the housecarls, professional soldiers with two-handed axes, and the fyrd, part-time levies. They formed a shield wall along the top of the ridge. English fought on foot.
[VISUAL: shield wall on the hill, housecarls with axes]
William's army included knights on horseback, archers and infantry. Early on, the Norman attacks failed against the wall. A rumour spread that William had been killed. The Breton wing broke and fled. Many English broke ranks to chase them. William, lifting his helmet to show his face, rallied his men, and cut off the pursuers.
[VISUAL: Norman cavalry charge, William raising his helmet]
Some historians think the Normans then used feigned retreats to draw the English down from the ridge. Others say it was accidental, and the account was exaggerated by later writers. Either way, the shield wall weakened over the day.
Harold died late in the afternoon. The famous story, based on the Bayeux Tapestry, is that he was hit in the eye by an arrow. Other sources say he was cut down by knights. The tapestry may show both. Historians do not agree, and the arrow detail may be later.
[VISUAL: Harold's death in the tapestry, with a note: debated]
By nightfall the English had broken. William had won. He ordered an abbey built on the site of the battle, Battle Abbey, with its high altar where Harold fell.

## 6. BECOMING KING (13:00–15:30)
**NARRATION**
William did not rush straight to London. He took Dover, Canterbury and other places in a loop, cutting off the capital. English leaders, including the young Edgar Atheling, the nominal heir, and the archbishops, submitted to him at Berkhamsted. On Christmas Day 1066 he was crowned in Westminster Abbey.
[VISUAL: coronation at Westminster Abbey]
When the crowd shouted approval in English and in French, the guards outside thought it was a riot, and set fire to nearby houses. William was trembling, according to one chronicler.
England did not accept the new king easily. Over the next five years there were rebellions in the west, the Midlands, East Anglia, and the north. In 1069, northern earls, helped by a Danish fleet, took York and killed a Norman garrison. William responded with the Harrying of the North in the winter of 1069 to 1070. His army destroyed crops, animals and villages from York to Durham. Contemporary writers say that tens of thousands died of famine. The monk Orderic Vitalis gives a figure of as many as 100,000, which modern historians consider too high, but tens of thousands is entirely possible. The monk Orderic Vitalis, who was half Norman, wrote that William had committed a great crime. Modern historians debate the extent: Domesday Book shows large parts of Yorkshire still waste in 1086.
[VISUAL: northern villages burning, empty fields]
In 1070 to 1071, he also crushed a revolt led by Hereward the Wake in the fens of East Anglia.

## 7. THE NORMAN SETTLEMENT (15:30–18:00)
**NARRATION**
William changed England, and he did it by replacing the elite. Within twenty years, almost all the great English landowners had lost their estates to Normans and other French followers. By 1086, only a handful of English-born men held land directly from the king. Bishops and abbots were replaced too. His friend, Lanfranc of Pavia, became Archbishop of Canterbury.
[VISUAL: land changing hands, Lanfranc]
The new rulers built castles, mostly wooden mounds with a fenced courtyard, the motte-and-bailey type, which could be put up in days. Later, they built them in stone, like the White Tower in London, which became the core of the Tower of London.
[VISUAL: motte-and-bailey castle, White Tower]
The most famous result is Domesday Book. At Christmas 1085, William ordered a great survey of all England. Commissioners visited every county, and asked who held each piece of land, its value before and after the conquest, how many ploughs, mills, and villagers. The results were finished by 1086. The name Domesday, doomsday, was given later by English people who saw it as a final judgment, because its decisions could not be appealed. It is the most complete record of any medieval European kingdom.
[VISUAL: Domesday Book pages, commissioners at work]
The language also changed. For some three centuries, French was the language of the court, law and aristocracy, while English survived among ordinary people. Thousands of French words, such as beef, mutton, justice and court, entered English.
[VISUAL: word cloud English and French]

## 8. THE END AND LEGACY (18:00–19:30)
**NARRATION**
William spent his last years mostly in Normandy, fighting the French king and his own eldest son, Robert Curthose, who rebelled. In July 1087, during a raid on the town of Mantes, William was injured, according to one account by the pommel of his saddle. The official announcement blamed illness, so the cause is contested. He was taken to Rouen and died on 9 September 1087, aged about fifty-nine.
[VISUAL: Mantes burning, William's deathbed]
A grim story follows. At his burial in Caen, his swollen body, according to Orderic, burst when forced into a stone coffin, and the smell drove people from the church. Whether true or not, it is recorded by Orderic Vitalis.
He left Normandy to Robert and England to his second son, William Rufus. Henry, the youngest, got money.
Historians debate: was the Conquest a revolution or an evolution? Did Anglo-Saxon England already have the foundations for the medieval state? And how many English people died in the Harrying of the North?
[VISUAL: three question cards]
Next, we go to France in the seventeenth century, to the king who said, according to legend, I am the state: Louis XIV. Subscribe, and tell me which king you want after that.
