# Ep 27 — Frederick the Great: The Flute-Playing King Who Made Prussia a Great Power
Target: ~10 min (1416 words) · Status: DRAFT — must pass fact-check (docs/04) before voiceover

## 1. HOOK (0:00–0:50)
**NARRATION**
In August 1759, at the Battle of Kunersdorf, the King of Prussia watched his army destroyed. Two horses were shot under him. According to a famous story, a bullet struck a gold case in his pocket and saved his life. That night he wrote to a friend that all was lost.
[VISUAL: battle of Kunersdorf, snuffbox with a bullet dent]
Frederick II of Prussia was a man who wrote poetry in French, composed flute concertos, corresponded with Voltaire, and called himself the first servant of the state. He also began wars that killed hundreds of thousands, and turned a small, poor kingdom into one of the great powers of Europe. This is the story of Frederick the Great.
[VISUAL: Frederick portrait, map of Prussia]

## 2. A DIFFICULT FATHER (0:50–3:30)
**NARRATION**
Frederick was born on 24 January 1712 in Berlin. His grandfather was the first King in Prussia, Frederick I. His father, Frederick William I, the Soldier King, was a very different man. He loved soldiers, hated luxury and trusted only discipline.
[VISUAL: Frederick William I, the Potsdam giants]
Frederick William created one of the best armies in Europe, relative to the size of the country. He built up a treasury, a civil service, and a rule: Prussia would stay at peace and keep a strong army ready. He famously collected tall soldiers for his Potsdam Giants guard.
The father despised his son's interests. The boy loved music, French literature and philosophy. The king saw him as effeminate, and treated him with beatings and humiliation. In 1730, aged eighteen, Frederick tried to flee to England with his friend and probable lover Hans Hermann von Katte, a lieutenant. They were caught. Frederick William had Frederick imprisoned in the fortress of Kustrin. Katte was sentenced to life imprisonment, but the King changed it to death, and had Frederick watch the beheading from his cell window.
[VISUAL: the escape attempt, Kustrin, Katte's execution]
This is one of the most disturbing episodes in Prussian history. Frederick was forced to learn government at Kustrin, and later submitted to his father. He married Elisabeth Christine of Brunswick-Bevern in 1733, a marriage arranged by his father. He never lived with her.
In 1736 he moved to Rheinsberg Castle, where he spent happy years reading, writing and playing music. He wrote the Anti-Machiavel, criticising the cold realism of Machiavelli, and corresponded with Voltaire.

## 3. THE SEIZURE OF SILESIA (3:30–6:30)
**NARRATION**
Frederick became king on 31 May 1740, when his father died. Within a few months, an opportunity arose. In October 1740, the Habsburg Emperor Charles VI died, and his twenty-three-year-old daughter Maria Theresa inherited the Austrian lands. Charles had arranged for this by a treaty, the Pragmatic Sanction, which the powers had agreed to respect. Frederick did not.
[VISUAL: Charles VI's death, Maria Theresa]
In December 1740, without declaring war, Frederick invaded Silesia, a rich province of Austria. He had an old claim on parts of it, but the real reason was power. He later admitted, in his memoirs, that ambition and the desire for fame played a part in his decision.
He won at Mollwitz in April 1741, though the king himself fled the field early, and his generals won the day. After two wars, in 1740 to 1742 and 1744 to 1745, Prussia kept Silesia. It grew by a third in population and by a larger share in wealth.
[VISUAL: Prussian invasion of Silesia, Mollwitz]
The Silesian Wars made Frederick a European figure, and Maria Theresa an enemy for life.

## 4. THE ENLIGHTENED KING AT SANSSOUCI (6:30–9:00)
**NARRATION**
In the following years of peace, 1745 to 1756, Frederick turned to governing. He called himself the first servant of the state. He worked hard from dawn, reading reports and writing notes in the margins in his own hand.
[VISUAL: Frederick at his desk, annotations]
He built the palace of Sanssouci at Potsdam, meaning without worries, from 1745 to 1747. He invited philosophers and artists to his court. In 1750 Voltaire came, and stayed for nearly three years. The friendship ended in a bitter quarrel, and Voltaire left Prussia in 1753.
[VISUAL: Sanssouci, Voltaire at dinner]
Frederick's government was a mix of enlightenment and discipline. He abolished torture in most cases, in 1740. He declared that everyone in his kingdom could be saved in his own way; he granted religious tolerance to Catholics, Jews, and Protestants. He encouraged schools, though in practice many villages had no teacher. He promoted the potato, ordering farmers to plant it, and a popular story, which historians doubt, goes that he posted soldiers to guard the fields in order to make peasants think it was valuable, so they would steal it.
[VISUAL: potato fields, soldiers guarding]
But he also kept society as it was. Nobles were given the officer posts. Serfdom remained in the east. Jews were taxed heavily and limited in occupation. Frederick himself despised the German language and wrote in French.

## 5. THE SEVEN YEARS' WAR (9:00–14:00)
**NARRATION**
In 1756, Frederick found himself surrounded. Austria, led by Maria Theresa and her chancellor Kaunitz, had made an alliance with France, long its enemy, in what historians call the Diplomatic Revolution. Russia, under Empress Elizabeth, was also hostile. Frederick had only Britain as an ally.
[VISUAL: map of Prussia surrounded by Austria, France and Russia]
Believing that attack was the best defence, Frederick invaded Saxony in August 1756, starting the Seven Years' War. At first he did well. On 5 November 1757 at Rossbach, he beat a French and imperial army twice his size, with fewer than 25,000 men, in less than two hours. A month later, on 5 December, at Leuthen, in Silesia, he defeated an Austrian army of 65,000 with 36,000. He used the oblique order, concentrating on one flank while holding back the other. Leuthen is considered his masterpiece.
[VISUAL: Rossbach, Leuthen, oblique order diagram]
But the enemies were many. In 1758 the Russians took East Prussia and invaded Brandenburg. In August 1759, Frederick met Russian and Austrian armies at Kunersdorf, near Frankfurt on the Oder, and was beaten badly. He lost about 20,000 men in a single day. In October 1760, the Russians briefly took Berlin. Frederick became bitter and depressed. He carried poison with him, and thought of suicide.
[VISUAL: Kunersdorf aftermath, Russians in Berlin]
By 1761, Prussia was near collapse, its army reduced, its treasury empty. Britain stopped its subsidies. Then on 5 January 1762, Empress Elizabeth of Russia died. Her successor Peter III admired Frederick, and made peace at once, returning all of Russia's conquests. This is called the Miracle of the House of Brandenburg. Although Catherine overthrew Peter in June, she did not return to war.
[VISUAL: Elizabeth's death, Peter III, the miracle]
Frederick fought on, and the war ended with the Treaty of Hubertusburg in February 1763. Prussia kept Silesia. The war killed perhaps 180,000 Prussian soldiers and civilians, and perhaps a million in all Europe.

## 6. REBUILDING AND POLAND (14:00–16:30)
**NARRATION**
After the war, Frederick devoted himself to rebuilding. He drained the marshes of the Oder, Warthe and Netze rivers, creating new farmland, and settled colonists. He said: here I have conquered a province in peace. He gave grain from state stores to farmers and reduced taxes in the worst-hit places. He supported legal reform: the Prussian Code, which was finished after his death in 1794, began under his orders.
[VISUAL: drained marshes, settlers, law books]
In 1772, with Russia and Austria, he took part in the first Partition of Poland. Prussia gained West Prussia, which joined the separated territories of Brandenburg and East Prussia. It was a deal that took no account of Polish wishes. It is hard to justify, and Frederick admitted that his only reason was need.
[VISUAL: First Partition of Poland on a map]

## 7. THE OLD FRITZ (16:30–18:00)
**NARRATION**
In his last years, Frederick grew solitary. His marriage had been empty, and he spent his old age at Sanssouci with his greyhounds, whom he called his only friends. His clothes were old and stained, and his snuff-covered coat became famous. His subjects called him Old Fritz.
[VISUAL: Old Frederick with greyhounds at Sanssouci]
He died on 17 August 1786 at Sanssouci, aged seventy-four. He had asked to be buried next to his dogs on the terrace. He was buried in the Garrison Church in Potsdam instead, next to his father. In 1991, after German reunification, his remains were moved to Sanssouci, to the terrace, as he had wished.
[VISUAL: the terrace burial, 1991]
He had no children. His nephew, Frederick William II, succeeded him. In 1806, twenty years later, at Jena, Napoleon destroyed the Prussian army which was still trained as Frederick had left it.

## 8. LEGACY AND DEBATE (18:00–19:30)
**NARRATION**
Frederick made Prussia a great power. Prussia would later lead the unification of Germany in 1871. For this reason, his image was used by nationalists, and later by the Nazis, who admired him as a symbol. Historians today reject simple claims. Tim Blanning, Christopher Clark and others show a complex man: a cultured, cynical, tolerant and ruthless ruler.
[VISUAL: Prussia becoming Germany, Frederick in different eras]
Questions remain. Was he an enlightened ruler, or an enlightened despot only in words? Was Silesia a crime or a calculation? And how much of the Frederick legend was created in later ages?
