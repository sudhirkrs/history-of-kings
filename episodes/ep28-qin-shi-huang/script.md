# Ep 28 — Qin Shi Huang: The First Emperor of China
Target: ~10 min (1482 words) · Status: DRAFT, fact-checked by web search (see factcheck/); still needs a human check against the cited books

## 1. HOOK (0:00–0:50)
**NARRATION**
In 1974, farmers digging a well near the city of Xi'an in China broke into a buried chamber. Inside stood row after row of life-sized clay soldiers, each with a different face, together with horses and chariots. They had been buried for over two thousand years to guard the tomb of one man.
[VISUAL: terracotta army pits, close-up of faces]
That man was Qin Shi Huang, the First Emperor. In just twenty-five years he united warring states into one empire, standardised writing, money and measures, and began a political system that lasted for two thousand years. He was also accused of burning books, burying scholars, and working his people to death. This is his story, and the story of how we know it.
[VISUAL: map of unified China in 221 BCE]

## 2. THE WARRING STATES (0:50–3:00)
**NARRATION**
Before China was one country, it was many. From about 475 BCE, the land of the Zhou kings had broken into rival states in the period called the Warring States. Seven big powers fought for supremacy: Qin in the west, Chu in the south, Qi in the east, Yan in the north, and Han, Zhao and Wei in the centre.
[VISUAL: map of the seven states]
Qin was the least civilised in the eyes of the others, a frontier state on the edge of the steppe. But it had an advantage. Under the minister Shang Yang, in the fourth century BCE, Qin adopted a harsh set of ideas known as Legalism. Rewards for military service, heavy punishments, and a state that registered and controlled every household. Nobility was based on merit in war, not birth. Qin became a machine for war.
[VISUAL: Shang Yang, rewards for war merit, law tablets]

## 3. THE BOY KING (3:00–6:00)
**NARRATION**
Our main source is the Records of the Grand Historian, written by Sima Qian around 100 BCE, over a century after the First Emperor died, under the Han dynasty, which replaced Qin and had reason to portray it as cruel. Keep that in mind.
[VISUAL: Sima Qian, bamboo scrolls]
According to Sima Qian, the future emperor was born in 259 BCE in Handan, the capital of the state of Zhao, where his father Zichu was a hostage from Qin. He was named Ying Zheng. A rich merchant, Lu Buwei, had helped his father to become heir of Qin. A legend says Zheng was Lu Buwei's own son, because his mother had been Lu's concubine. Most historians doubt this story, which may be Han propaganda.
In 247 BCE, aged thirteen, Zheng became King of Qin. For years, Lu Buwei ruled as regent. The queen mother took a lover, Lao Ai, who had two sons with her and plotted a coup. In 238 BCE, Zheng, aged twenty-one, crushed the rebellion, executed Lao Ai, and later dismissed Lu Buwei, who killed himself. Now Zheng ruled alone.
[VISUAL: Handan, Lu Buwei, Lao Ai's rebellion]
He found good advisors, the Legalist Li Si, and others. In 227 BCE, an assassin named Jing Ke, sent by the state of Yan, hid a dagger inside a map and tried to stab him in the royal hall. Zheng's sword was too long to draw. He ran around a pillar, until he could draw it and wound the attacker. The story is famous, and told in Sima Qian.
[VISUAL: Jing Ke and the map scroll, king running around a pillar]

## 4. THE CONQUEST (6:00–8:30)
**NARRATION**
Qin's armies were the best in China. Between 230 and 221 BCE, Zheng conquered all six of the other states, one by one. Han fell in 230, Zhao in 228, Wei in 225, Chu in 223 after a hard war, Yan in 222, and Qi in 221. The Qin army was large, well supplied by roads and canals, and armed with iron weapons and crossbows with bronze triggers. Qin commanders such as Wang Jian and Meng Tian were skilled.
[VISUAL: animated conquest map with years]
In 221 BCE, Zheng became ruler of all the Chinese states. He wanted a title greater than king. He chose Huangdi, from huang, august, and di, a name for the ancient divine rulers. He called himself Shi Huangdi, the First Emperor, and said his successors would be Second, Third, and so on, for ten thousand generations. The dynasty would fall in fifteen years.
[VISUAL: the title, throne, ten thousand generations]

## 5. BUILDING AN EMPIRE (8:30–12:00)
**NARRATION**
The First Emperor's government was built to last. He abolished the old feudal states. China was divided into 36 commanderies at first, later more, each headed by appointed officials, not hereditary lords. Each was divided into counties. Officials were moved around and could be dismissed, a system that later dynasties continued.
[VISUAL: map of commanderies, officials]
He standardised weights and measures, coins, the axle width of carts so that they could run in the same ruts, and the writing system. Before the Qin, each state had its own forms of characters. The Qin adopted a simplified standard script, small seal script. This one decision, historians argue, helped hold China together for millennia, because people who spoke different dialects could read the same text.
[VISUAL: bronze weights, round coins with square holes, script comparison]
He built roads, a network reported to total about 6,800 kilometres of imperial highways, and a canal, the Lingqu, linking the Yangtze and Pearl river systems. He ordered the connection of earlier walls on the northern border into a long defensive barrier. This was not the stone Great Wall that tourists see today, which is mostly from the Ming dynasty, but a rammed-earth structure.
[VISUAL: roads, canal, the Qin wall]
The cost was huge. Hundreds of thousands of conscripts were sent to build the wall and the roads, and to garrison the frontier. Taxes were heavy. Laws were strict: Qin legal texts found in 1975 on bamboo strips at Shuihudi in Hubei show detailed rules and harsh punishments.
[VISUAL: bamboo legal strips, conscripts]

## 6. BOOKS AND SCHOLARS (12:00–14:00)
**NARRATION**
Now the most notorious part. In 213 BCE, at a banquet, scholars criticised the emperor for not restoring the old feudal system. Li Si replied that scholars used the past to criticise the present. He proposed that books of history, poetry and philosophy kept outside the imperial library be burned, with exceptions for books on medicine, divination and agriculture. Those who discussed the Classics in public would be executed.
[VISUAL: burning scrolls]
The next year, 212 BCE, two alchemists who had been seeking elixirs for the emperor fled, and criticised him. Furious, he investigated, and 460 scholars, according to Sima Qian, were buried alive.
Historians debate the story. The live burial first appears in Sima Qian, more than a century later, and some modern historians doubt it. The book burning is confirmed in outline, but many works survived in the imperial library until it was destroyed in the fall of the dynasty. As for the burial of scholars, some historians think the number is a Han exaggeration, and that those killed were mostly alchemists and magicians, not Confucian scholars. But the event, whatever its size, became the emperor's evil reputation.
[VISUAL: question mark over the buried scholars]

## 7. THE TOMB AND THE QUEST FOR IMMORTALITY (14:00–16:30)
**NARRATION**
The emperor feared death. He sent expeditions to find the islands of the immortals. One of them was led by Xu Fu, who sailed east, in some accounts with thousands of young boys and girls, and never came back. The numbers come from late sources. He took medicines made with mercury, which probably hastened his death.
[VISUAL: Xu Fu's ships, mercury pills]
He began building his tomb when he was thirteen. According to Sima Qian, 700,000 workers laboured on it. Inside the burial chamber were rivers of mercury, a map of the heavens on the ceiling, and crossbows set to shoot intruders. Modern soil tests near the mound found elevated levels of mercury, supporting the account. The tomb itself has not been opened.
[VISUAL: tomb mound cutaway, mercury rivers]
The terracotta army, discovered in 1974, is a small part of the complex. Archaeologists estimate there are about 8,000 soldiers in three pits. Each figure was built from moulded parts and individually finished. They once carried real bronze weapons, and were painted in bright colours, which faded when exposed to air.
[VISUAL: terracotta soldiers painted in colour reconstruction]

## 8. DEATH AND COLLAPSE (16:30–18:30)
**NARRATION**
In 210 BCE, during a tour of the eastern provinces, the emperor fell ill and died at Shaqiu, aged 49. The chief eunuch Zhao Gao and Li Si hid his death, travelling back with the carriage, and put rotten fish in the wagon, according to Sima Qian, to hide the smell of the body in the summer heat. They forged an edict ordering the heir, Fusu, to commit suicide, and put the younger son, Huhai, on the throne as the Second Emperor.
[VISUAL: carriage on the road, forged edict]
It was a disaster. Zhao Gao murdered Li Si and then forced the Second Emperor to suicide. In 209 BCE, two conscripts, Chen Sheng and Wu Guang, delayed by rain and facing execution for lateness, started a rebellion. Within a few years, the empire collapsed. In 206 BCE the last Qin ruler surrendered to the rebel Liu Bang, who founded the Han dynasty in 202.
[VISUAL: Chen Sheng and Wu Guang, Liu Bang]

## 9. LEGACY AND DEBATE (18:30–20:00)
**NARRATION**
Qin lasted only fifteen years. Yet its structure of centralised bureaucracy, standard script and unified empire continued. The Han kept most of Qin's institutions, though with a softer Confucian approach. The English word China may derive from Qin.
[VISUAL: line from Qin to modern China]
Historians still debate. Was he a tyrant or a visionary? How much of the cruel image is Han propaganda? What was the real extent of the burning of the books? What is inside the tomb? Chinese authorities have not opened it, citing preservation concerns.
Next, we meet the Han emperor who sent explorers west and fought the Xiongnu: Emperor Wu of Han. Subscribe, and tell me which king you want after that.
