# Ep 23 — Alexander II: The Tsar Who Freed the Serfs
Target: ~9 min (1364 words) · Status: DRAFT, fact-checked by web search (see factcheck/); still needs a human check against the cited books

## 1. HOOK (0:00–0:50)
**NARRATION**
On the morning of 13 March 1881, the Tsar of Russia rode through the streets of St Petersburg in a closed carriage, reportedly a gift from Napoleon III. That day he had signed a plan that could have given Russia its first national assembly. A bomb thrown by a young revolutionary exploded beside the carriage. The Tsar survived, got out to help the wounded, and a second bomb was thrown at his feet. He died a few hours later.
[VISUAL: St Petersburg canal, armoured carriage, explosion]
The man was Alexander II. Twenty years before, he had freed more than twenty million serfs, the biggest social reform in the history of Russia. Yet he was killed by people who said he had not done enough. This is the story of the Tsar Liberator.
[VISUAL: Alexander II portrait, manifesto of emancipation]

## 2. THE SON OF NICHOLAS I (0:50–3:00)
**NARRATION**
Alexander was born on 29 April 1818, in Moscow. His father was the future Tsar Nicholas I, a stern ruler who built a police state, crushed the Decembrist revolt of 1825, and ruled by censorship and fear. Alexander's tutor was the poet Vasily Zhukovsky, who taught him to value humanity and learning. As a young man he toured Russia, visiting Siberian exiles.
[VISUAL: young Alexander, Zhukovsky, Nicholas I]
In 1855, during the Crimean War, Nicholas died. Russia was fighting Britain, France and the Ottomans, and was losing. The siege of Sevastopol ended in September 1855 with Russian defeat. In March 1856 the Treaty of Paris forced Russia to give up the Black Sea fleet.
[VISUAL: siege of Sevastopol, Treaty of Paris]
Alexander, 36, became Tsar in the middle of a national humiliation. The war showed that Russia was backward: no railways to the front, old weapons and a serf army. He concluded that reform was necessary. In a speech in 1856 to nobles in Moscow, he is reported to have said that it is better to abolish serfdom from above than to wait until it begins to abolish itself from below. The exact words vary in the sources.

## 3. WHAT WAS SERFDOM? (3:00–5:00)
**NARRATION**
By 1860, about a third of Russia's population were serfs, around 23 million people, most belonging to noble landowners. Serfs were tied to the land. They worked for the landlord, paid dues, and could not leave without permission. Landowners could sell them, separately from the land, break up families and, until limits were set, punish them. They were not slaves in law, but in practice often were treated like property.
[VISUAL: serf village, landlord's manor, auction notice]
Many nobles, especially in the Russian heartland, depended on serf labour. Reformers knew that ending serfdom would hit the nobility. Peasants, however, expected freedom, and the risk of revolts was real. There had been hundreds of serf disturbances in the 1850s.

## 4. THE EMANCIPATION (5:00–8:30)
**NARRATION**
Alexander set up a secret committee in 1857, which became a public effort, with regional committees of nobles to give their view. A team of reformers including Nikolai Milyutin and Yakov Rostovtsev wrote the final text.
[VISUAL: committee at work, Milyutin]
The Emancipation Manifesto was signed on 19 February 1861, according to the Russian calendar, 3 March in the new one. It declared that serfs were free. They had personal freedom: they could marry, own property, and go to court without the landlord's consent.
But the land was the key question. The peasants did not get the land for free. They were to receive a plot, the size depending on the region, but they had to pay for it. The state paid the landowner at once, and the peasants repaid the state over 49 years in redemption payments, with interest. In practice, the peasant communes, the mir, held the land collectively and were responsible for the payments.
[VISUAL: manifesto read in a village, land plots on a map]
The result disappointed everyone. Many peasants got less land than they had farmed before, and felt cheated. Peasant riots broke out in spring 1861, and in some places troops opened fire; in the village of Bezdna dozens of peasants were killed, about fifty by the official count and perhaps more. Many nobles were unhappy too, though they were paid.
Most historians say that the reform was a start but incomplete. It ended legal serfdom, but left peasants poor, indebted and tied to the commune. The redemption payments were finally ended in 1907, after the Revolution of 1905.
[VISUAL: peasant unrest, Bezdna]

## 5. THE GREAT REFORMS (8:30–11:30)
**NARRATION**
Emancipation led to a series of other changes, called the Great Reforms.
[VISUAL: montage of reforms]
In 1864, he set up the zemstvo, elected local councils in rural districts, which looked after schools, roads, medical care and food supplies. Elections were weighted to give property owners the majority, but peasants took part too. For the first time, Russians outside the nobility had a role in local government.
In 1864, the judicial reform introduced public trials, juries, independent judges, and defence lawyers. It was the most liberal reform. The old system, where courts were secret and written, was replaced.
In 1874, the military reform, led by Minister Dmitry Milyutin, introduced universal conscription for all social classes, with shorter service terms, instead of 25 years for peasants. The army was modernised.
Universities got more autonomy, and censorship was eased. Education spread. Industry and railways grew fast. In these years, Russia produced the novelists Tolstoy and Dostoevsky, and the composer Tchaikovsky.
[VISUAL: zemstvo meeting, jury trial, conscripts, Tolstoy]

## 6. EXPANSION AND ALASKA (11:30–13:30)
**NARRATION**
Alexander also expanded the empire to the east and south. In the 1860s and 1870s, Russia conquered Central Asia: Tashkent in 1865, Samarkand in 1868, Khiva and Kokand in the 1870s. In the Far East, Russia gained the Amur and the Pacific coast from China, founding Vladivostok in 1860.
[VISUAL: map of Central Asia, Samarkand, Vladivostok]
In 1867, Russia sold Alaska to the United States for 7.2 million dollars, about two cents an acre. Russia had little money, the colony was far away and hard to defend, and the Tsar believed Britain would take it in a future war. At the time, many Americans called it Seward's Folly.
In the Russo-Turkish War of 1877 to 1878, Russia fought to help the Slavs of the Balkans against the Ottomans. After the Siege of Plevna, the Russian army was close to Constantinople. But at the Congress of Berlin in 1878, the European powers forced Russia to give up much of what it had gained. This angered Russian nationalists.
[VISUAL: Alaska purchase check, Plevna, Congress of Berlin]
In 1863, a Polish uprising was crushed with harsh measures, ending hope for a more liberal policy towards Poland.

## 7. THE REVOLUTIONARIES (13:30–16:30)
**NARRATION**
Reform created hope, and the hope created impatience. In the 1860s and 1870s, young Russians known as the Populists went to the villages to educate the peasants. The authorities arrested hundreds. Some turned to terror.
[VISUAL: students going to the villages, arrests]
In 1866, a student named Dmitry Karakozov tried to shoot the Tsar. In 1879, Alexander Solovyov fired five shots at him in the street, and missed. The same year, a group called Narodnaya Volya, the People's Will, decided to kill the Tsar. They tried to blow up his train in December 1879. In February 1880, one of them, Stepan Khalturin, who worked as a carpenter in the Winter Palace, set off a bomb in the palace dining room, killing or wounding dozens, with eleven or twelve killed, depending on the source. The Tsar was late for dinner and survived.
[VISUAL: attempts on the Tsar, Winter Palace explosion]
Alexander appointed a general with liberal views, Mikhail Loris-Melikov, to restore order and plan reforms. In early 1881, Loris-Melikov proposed to include elected representatives in a commission to consider laws. On the morning of 1 March (old calendar), Alexander approved it for publication. Some historians call it a draft constitution, others say it was much more limited. The truth is debated.
[VISUAL: Loris-Melikov, the document]

## 8. THE ASSASSINATION AND LEGACY (16:30–18:30)
**NARRATION**
On 1 March 1881, the People's Will struck. Their bomb-maker Nikolai Rysakov threw the first bomb, which wrecked the carriage. Alexander got out. Then Ignacy Hryniewiecki, a Polish member, threw the second bomb at his feet. The Tsar was gravely wounded. He died in the Winter Palace an hour or two later. Hryniewiecki also died. The conspirators were hanged.
[VISUAL: the scene on the canal, the Winter Palace]
His son Alexander III took the throne. He cancelled Loris-Melikov's plan, increased censorship and tightened police rule. A church, the Church of the Saviour on Spilled Blood, was built on the spot, and it stands today.
[VISUAL: Church of the Savior on Spilled Blood]
Historians debate his legacy. Some say the emancipation was a great step, and the assassination killed any chance of a peaceful path to constitutional government. Others say reforms came too late and too little, and show why Russia faced revolution in 1905 and 1917.
