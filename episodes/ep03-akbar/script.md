# Ep 3 — Akbar: The Boy Emperor Who Built the Mughal Empire
Target: ~13 min (1885 words) · Status: DRAFT — must pass fact-check (docs/04) before voiceover

## 1. HOOK (0:00–0:50)
**NARRATION**
In January 1556, a thirteen-year-old boy became the emperor of a kingdom that barely existed. His father had just died after falling down a flight of stairs. The family had been homeless exiles only months before. And an enemy general was marching on Delhi with an army and a hundred elephants.
[VISUAL: boy portrait in Mughal miniature style, then a map with an enemy army approaching Delhi]
Fifty years later, this boy had built one of the largest and richest empires in the world, with a system of government that outlived him by more than a century.
[VISUAL: empire map growing from 1556 to 1605]
This is Akbar. In this video, we will see how he survived his first year, how he conquered much of India, how he ruled it, and why historians still argue about him.

## 2. A FAMILY IN EXILE (0:50–3:30)
**NARRATION**
Akbar's story starts with his grandfather, Babur. Babur was a prince from Central Asia, descended from both Timur and Genghis Khan, who had lost his homeland. In 1526, at the first battle of Panipat, he defeated the Delhi sultan Ibrahim Lodi, and founded Mughal rule in India.
[VISUAL: Babur portrait, Panipat 1526 battle scene]
Babur died in 1530. His son Humayun was a gentle, learned man, but a weak ruler. In 1540 he was beaten by an Afghan commander named Sher Shah Suri, and driven out of India.
[VISUAL: Humayun fleeing, Sher Shah Suri portrait]
Akbar was born on 15 October 1542, at the fort of Umarkot in Sindh, while his parents were on the run. His mother was Hamida Banu Begum. When Humayun fled to Persia, the baby was left behind, and raised by his uncle in Kabul.
[VISUAL: Umarkot fort, toddler Akbar]
Accounts say Akbar loved hunting, riding and animals, but he never properly learned to read. Some historians doubt this and think the story is exaggerated, because as emperor he built a huge library and had books read aloud to him daily.
In 1555, with help from the Persian king, Humayun took back Delhi. But he did not enjoy it for long. In January 1556, he slipped on the steps of his library and died a few days later.
[VISUAL: Humayun's library steps, Din Panah]
Akbar was in Punjab, fighting Afghan rebels. On 14 February 1556, at Kalanaur, he was crowned. He was thirteen.

## 3. THE REGENT AND THE GREAT TEST (3:30–7:00)
**NARRATION**
In those first years, the empire was run by Bairam Khan, Humayun's most trusted general, who became Akbar's regent.
[VISUAL: Bairam Khan portrait]
The danger was an unusual man called Hemu, or Hemchandra. He had started as a trader and become a minister and general under the Sur rulers. In October 1556, while the Mughals were weak, he took Delhi in a battle at Tughlaqabad, and crowned himself with the title Vikramaditya.
[VISUAL: Hemu on an elephant, Delhi map]
On 5 November 1556, the two armies met at Panipat, the same field where Babur had won thirty years earlier. Hemu had more soldiers and many war elephants, and his side was winning.
Then an arrow struck him in the eye. He fell unconscious inside his howdah, and his army, thinking he was dead, broke and ran. Hemu was captured and executed.
[VISUAL: Panipat 1556 battle map, arrow, army fleeing]
Some accounts say Bairam Khan urged the young Akbar to strike the final blow, to earn the title of ghazi, a holy warrior. The sources disagree on whether Akbar actually did, and many historians doubt that he did. Hemu died, whoever struck.
What is certain is that the Mughal Empire was saved by luck as much as skill. If that arrow had missed, Akbar might be remembered as a footnote.

## 4. TAKING CONTROL (7:00–9:50)
**NARRATION**
For four years, Bairam Khan ran things. But Akbar was growing up, and he wanted to rule. In 1560, he removed Bairam Khan from power and told him to go on pilgrimage to Mecca. On the way, in 1561, Bairam was killed by an Afghan whose father had died fighting the Mughals.
[VISUAL: Akbar taking the reins, Bairam departing]
Akbar still had to deal with powerful people close to him. His foster-brother Adham Khan, the son of his nurse Maham Anga, murdered a minister in 1562. Akbar had him thrown from a terrace at Agra Fort, twice, to be sure. The message was clear: the emperor was in charge.
[VISUAL: Agra Fort terrace, stylised silhouette]
Next, he made a decision that changed Mughal history. In 1562, he married the daughter of Raja Bharmal of Amer, a Rajput kingdom near modern Jaipur. In the following years, he abolished the tax on Hindu pilgrims, around 1563, and the jizya, the special tax on non-Muslims, in 1564. A later emperor, Aurangzeb, brought the jizya back in 1679.
[VISUAL: wedding procession, tax receipt]
This was a new approach. Rulers before him had mostly seen Rajput chiefs as enemies. Akbar made them partners. Raja Man Singh of Amer, Raja Todar Mal and Raja Birbal became some of his top officials. By the end of his reign, a large share of the highest-ranking nobles were Hindus, mostly Rajputs.
[VISUAL: Man Singh, Todar Mal, Birbal portraits]

## 5. CONQUEST (9:50–13:20)
**NARRATION**
But Akbar was no pacifist. He conquered a huge region, using both force and negotiation.
[VISUAL: map of Rajputana, Gujarat and Bengal]
Many Rajput kings accepted his rule. Mewar, whose capital was the great fort of Chittorgarh, did not. In 1567, Akbar laid siege to Chittor. The siege lasted months. The defenders, led by Jaimal and Patta, fought hard, and their names are still remembered. When the fort fell on 23 February 1568, thousands of its defenders died. The defender Jaimal was shot, by some accounts by Akbar himself. Akbar's chronicler says that about thirty thousand people were killed afterwards, in a massacre that stands among the darkest moments of his reign.
[VISUAL: Chittorgarh fort, siege lines, solemn silhouette]
Then came Gujarat in 1572 and 1573, with its rich ports and cloth trade. Bengal followed in 1576.
In June 1576 at Haldighati, Mughal forces led by Man Singh fought Maharana Pratap, who had taken the Mewar throne and refused to submit. The Rajput forces were pushed back, but Pratap escaped and continued to resist for years. Who won is still debated, and we will look more closely at Pratap in a later episode.
[VISUAL: Haldighati map, Pratap on his horse]
Akbar took Kashmir in 1586, Sindh and Kandahar by 1595, and parts of the Deccan, including Khandesh and Ahmednagar, which fell after the death of the brave queen Chand Bibi, by 1600 and 1601.
[VISUAL: animated map 1556 to 1605]

## 6. HOW HE RULED (13:20–16:10)
**NARRATION**
Conquest was only half of Akbar's work. The other half was system.
First, the mansabdari system. Every officer received a rank, called a mansab. The rank decided his salary and the number of soldiers and horses he had to supply. Ranks could be raised or lowered by the emperor, and nobles were moved around, so none could build a private power base.
[VISUAL: pyramid of ranks]
Second, land revenue. His finance minister, Raja Todar Mal, had officials measure the land and record crop prices and yields for ten years. The tax was then fixed as a share of the average produce, paid in cash. It brought order and predictability, though local collectors could still be harsh.
[VISUAL: measuring fields, ledger, coins]
Third, a new capital. In 1571, Akbar began building Fatehpur Sikri, near Agra, in red sandstone, in honour of the Sufi saint Salim Chishti, who had predicted the birth of his son. But after about fifteen years, the court moved away. Water shortage is the usual explanation, but historians still debate the reasons.
[VISUAL: Buland Darwaza, Fatehpur Sikri courtyards]
His court was famous for talent. Tradition speaks of the Navaratnas, the nine jewels: people such as the minister Birbal, the singer Tansen, the poet Abu'l Fazl, and Todar Mal. But the exact list of nine is traditional, not an official institution.
[VISUAL: court scene, Tansen singing]

## 7. FAITH AND IDEAS (16:10–18:50)
**NARRATION**
Akbar was endlessly curious about religion. In 1574 or 1575 he built the Ibadat Khana, the House of Worship, at Fatehpur Sikri, where scholars of Islam argued with each other. Soon he invited Hindus, Jains, Parsis, and Jesuit priests from Goa.
[VISUAL: debate scene, Jesuit missionaries]
In 1582 he started the Din-i-Ilahi, the Divine Faith. Many people think it was a new religion. Most historians say it was a small circle of courtiers who pledged personal loyalty to the emperor, with a few moral rules. It had very few members, and died with him.
[VISUAL: Din-i-Ilahi emblem, small group of courtiers]
More important was an idea called sulh-i-kul, universal peace, or peace with all. It meant the state should treat all religions fairly. Many admired this. Conservative Muslim scholars thought he had gone too far, and some accused him of moving away from Islam. Historians are still divided on how far he really went.
[VISUAL: scales balancing religious symbols]

## 7B. THE MAN AND HIS COURT (18:50–21:30)
**NARRATION**
What kind of man was Akbar? We have unusually rich evidence, including the writings of Jesuit priests who lived at his court.
[VISUAL: Jesuit missionaries at Fatehpur Sikri]
Father Antonio Monserrate, who visited in the 1580s, describes a man of medium height, strongly built, with a loud voice, who loved to wrestle and to ride elephants. He was restless and curious, slept little, and was said to have great energy for work. Akbar spoke to scholars in the evenings, long after the day's business had ended.
Although he is traditionally described as unlettered, he ran one of the greatest book projects of his age. His royal library held tens of thousands of volumes. He ordered the Mahabharata to be translated from Sanskrit into Persian, under the title Razmnama, the Book of Wars. The Ramayana, the Atharva Veda and many other works were also translated. Persian became the shared language of administration, so Hindu clerks and Muslim scholars could work in one system.
[VISUAL: translation workshop, Razmnama miniature]
Akbar was also a patron of painting. His workshop employed Hindu and Muslim painters together, and created the Hamzanama, a giant illustrated manuscript with some fourteen hundred large paintings, which took about fifteen years. This gave birth to the Mughal style of painting, blending Persian, Indian and later European elements.
[VISUAL: Hamzanama paintings, Mughal miniature workshop]
He was a patron of music too. The singer Tansen, who came from Gwalior, became one of the most famous musicians in Indian history, and is still revered today.
Akbar's household was large. His mother Hamida Banu and his chief wife, Mariam-uz-Zamani, the Rajput princess from Amer, held great influence. Women of the royal family ran their own households, funded charities and sometimes travelled on pilgrimage.
[VISUAL: women of the Mughal court]
The economy under Akbar grew steadily. He standardised the silver coin, the rupiya, and gold coins were struck for display and for large payments. Historians estimate that India under Akbar had a population of well over a hundred million, and that the empire collected an enormous revenue, making it among the richest states in the world.
[VISUAL: Akbari silver rupee, markets, ships at Surat]
He also tried to reform some practices. He discouraged forced sati, taxed or restricted some cruel practices and banned the enslavement of war captives' families, according to Abu'l Fazl. But we should be cautious: these claims come from the court's own history, and enforcement across such a large empire must have varied.
In 1579 he issued a decree, called the mahzar, that made the emperor the final judge when Islamic scholars disagreed. His critics saw this as an attack on the clergy. His supporters said it was needed to stop the clash between rival scholars. It tells us that Akbar saw religion and state as closely linked, but wanted the state to have the last word.
[VISUAL: mahzar decree, scholars arguing]

## 8. THE LAST YEARS (18:50–20:10)
**NARRATION**
His last years were unhappy. His son Salim, the future emperor Jahangir, rebelled and set up his own court. In 1602, Salim arranged the murder of Abu'l Fazl, Akbar's friend and chronicler.
[VISUAL: Salim, then Abu'l Fazl portrait]
Akbar died on 27 October 1605, at Agra, according to most sources. He was buried at Sikandra, in a tomb that he had planned himself.
[VISUAL: Sikandra tomb]

## 9. LEGACY AND DEBATE (20:10–21:20)
**NARRATION**
Akbar left an empire in northern India and a system of administration that lasted. Most of what we know comes from the Akbarnama and the Ain-i-Akbari, by Abu'l Fazl. He was a court historian, so he praises his master, and historians read him with care.
[VISUAL: Akbarnama miniature]
Some questions remain. How tolerant was he really? Was Chittor the cruelty of one ruler, or the way wars were fought in that age? Was he a visionary or a pragmatic king who knew an empire cannot rule by force alone?
