# Ep 3 — Akbar: The Boy Emperor Who Built the Mughal Empire
Target: ~20 min · Status: DRAFT — must pass fact-check (docs/04) before voiceover

## 1. HOOK (0:00–0:45)
**NARRATION**
In January 1556, a thirteen-year-old boy became emperor of a kingdom that barely existed. His father had just died after falling down a flight of stairs. The family had been exiles only months earlier. The enemy was marching on Delhi. Fifty years later, this boy had built one of the largest and richest empires in the world, and a government that outlived him by a century.
[VISUAL: boy portrait (Mughal miniature) → empire map growing]
This is Akbar. In this video: how he survived his first year, how he won India, how he ruled it, and why people still argue about him.

## 2. A FAMILY IN EXILE (0:45–3:00)
**NARRATION**
Akbar's grandfather, Babur, founded Mughal rule in India in 1526 after defeating the Lodi sultan at Panipat. Babur died in 1530. His son Humayun lost the throne to the Afghan Sher Shah Suri in 1540.
[VISUAL: Babur, Panipat 1526, Humayun in flight]
Akbar was born on 15 October 1542 at Umarkot in Sindh, while his parents were on the run. As a child he was raised by relatives and nurses. Many accounts say he was a keen hunter and loved animals, but never learned to read well. Some historians think this may be exaggerated, as he later built a huge library and had texts read aloud to him every day.
[VISUAL: Umarkot fort; manuscripts being read aloud]
In 1555 Humayun recaptured Delhi. But in January 1556 he slipped on the steps of his library and died. Akbar was in Punjab, fighting Afghans. He was crowned at Kalanaur on 14 February 1556, aged thirteen.

## 3. THE REGENT AND HEMU (3:00–6:30)
**NARRATION**
The real ruler in those early years was Bairam Khan, Humayun's trusted commander, who became regent.
[VISUAL: Bairam Khan portrait]
The danger was Hemu, also known as Hemchandra, a Hindu minister of the Sur rulers, who had risen to become a general. In October 1556 he seized Delhi and took the title Vikramaditya.
[VISUAL: Hemu on an elephant, Delhi map]
On 5 November 1556, the two sides met at Panipat. Hemu was winning. Then an arrow struck his eye, and he fell unconscious. His army panicked and broke. Hemu was captured and killed. Later accounts say Akbar was asked to strike the fatal blow and refused, but this story comes from one source and historians doubt it.
The Mughal empire was saved by luck as much as by skill.

## 4. TAKING CONTROL (6:30–9:30)
**NARRATION**
For four years, Bairam Khan ran things. But Akbar grew up, and in 1560 he removed Bairam from power and sent him on pilgrimage to Mecca. On the way, in 1561, Bairam was killed by an Afghan.
[VISUAL: Akbar taking the reins]
Akbar still had to fight court factions. In 1562 he had his foster-brother Adham Khan, who had murdered a minister, thrown from a terrace to his death. The message was clear: the emperor was in charge.
[VISUAL: Agra fort terrace]
Then came a set of wise decisions. In 1562 he married a Rajput princess, the daughter of Raja Bharmal of Amer. In 1563 he abolished the tax on Hindu pilgrims, and in 1564 he abolished the jizya, the special tax on non-Muslims.
[VISUAL: Rajput marriage procession; tax receipts]
This was a new approach. Instead of ruling by force alone, he brought the Rajput nobles into the empire as partners, such as Raja Man Singh and Raja Todar Mal.

## 5. CONQUEST (9:30–13:00)
**NARRATION**
He was no pacifist. In 1567–68 he besieged Chittorgarh, the great Rajput fortress of Mewar. After a long siege, the fort fell. The defenders Jaimal and Patta became legends. Reports say tens of thousands of civilians were killed afterwards, one of the darkest moments of his reign.
[VISUAL: Chittor fort; siege lines]
Next came Gujarat in 1572–73, with its rich ports, then Bengal in 1576. In 1576 at Haldighati, Mughal forces led by Man Singh fought Maharana Pratap of Mewar. Pratap was forced to retreat but never submitted.
[VISUAL: Haldighati map; Pratap horse]
Akbar later took Kashmir in 1586, Sindh and Kandahar by 1595, and parts of the Deccan, including Khandesh and Ahmednagar, by 1601.
[VISUAL: animated map 1556 → 1605]

## 6. HOW HE RULED (13:00–16:00)
**NARRATION**
Conquest was only half of it. Akbar built a system.
First, the *mansabdari* system. Every officer was given a rank, which decided his salary and the number of soldiers he must supply. Ranks could be changed, and nobles were moved around, so no one could build a private power base.
[VISUAL: rank pyramid]
Second, land revenue. His finance minister Todar Mal measured land and recorded crop yields for ten years, then fixed the tax as a share of the average produce. It brought order and fairness, though collectors could still abuse it.
[VISUAL: measured fields, ledger]
Third, a new capital. From 1571 he built Fatehpur Sikri, near Agra, in red sandstone. But within about fifteen years the court left, possibly because of water shortage. Historians still discuss the exact reason.
[VISUAL: Buland Darwaza, Fatehpur Sikri]
His court is also known for the "nine jewels" or Navaratnas, such as Birbal, Tansen, Abu'l Fazl, and Todar Mal. The exact list of nine is traditional, not an official institution.

## 7. FAITH AND IDEAS (16:00–18:30)
**NARRATION**
Akbar was curious about religion. In 1575 he built the Ibadat Khana, the "House of Worship" at Fatehpur Sikri, where scholars of Islam argued. Later he invited Hindus, Jains, Parsis, and Jesuit priests from Goa.
[VISUAL: debate scene, Jesuit missionaries]
In 1582 he introduced Din-i-Ilahi, the "Divine Faith". Many people think it was a new religion. Most historians say it was a small group of followers who pledged loyalty to the emperor, with a few moral rules. It had very few members, and died with him.
[VISUAL: Din-i-Ilahi seal]
His idea of *sulh-i-kul*, "peace with all", meant that the state should treat all faiths equally. His critics, including conservative Muslim scholars, thought he had gone too far. The debate is still alive.

## 8. THE LAST YEARS (18:30–19:30)
**NARRATION**
His last years were sad. His son Salim, later Jahangir, rebelled. In 1602, Abu'l Fazl, Akbar's friend and chronicler, was murdered on Salim's plot by Bir Singh Bundela.
[VISUAL: Abu'l Fazl portrait]
Akbar died on 27 October 1605 at Agra and was buried at Sikandra.
[VISUAL: Sikandra tomb]

## 9. LEGACY + DEBATE + CTA (19:30–20:30)
**NARRATION**
Akbar left a large, strong empire and a model of government that lasted. We know this from the *Akbarnama* and *Ain-i-Akbari* by Abu'l Fazl, a court historian, so they praise him, and historians read them with care.
