# Ep 11 — Ogedei Khan: The Generous Emperor Who Sent Armies to Europe
Target: ~8 min (1212 words) · Status: DRAFT — must pass fact-check (docs/04) before voiceover

## 1. HOOK (0:00–0:50)
**NARRATION**
In December 1241, at the Mongol court in Karakorum, the Great Khan Ogedei died after a night of heavy drinking. A few weeks earlier, his armies had crushed the Hungarian and Polish forces in Europe, burned Kiev and stood at the edge of Vienna. When the news of his death arrived, the Mongol princes in Europe turned back. Historians have debated whether a single death saved Europe.
[VISUAL: Karakorum at night, the empty throne, then a map of Mongol armies in Europe turning around]
This is the story of Ogedei, the third son of Genghis Khan, a generous, easygoing ruler who nevertheless led the Mongols to their greatest territorial expansion.
[VISUAL: Ogedei portrait]

## 2. THE CHOSEN HEIR (0:50–3:00)
**NARRATION**
Ogedei was born around 1186. As the third son of Genghis Khan and Borte, he stood between the brilliant Chagatai and the capable Tolui. The eldest, Jochi, whose paternity was questioned, had died in 1225.
[VISUAL: the four sons, with Jochi marked deceased]
The Secret History says that the sons argued before Genghis Khan about the succession. Jochi and Chagatai fought bitterly, and Genghis chose Ogedei as a compromise. He was known as a gentle, wise man, who could settle disputes.
When Genghis died in 1227, the youngest son, Tolui, ruled as regent for two years, as was the Mongol custom. In 1229, a kurultai elected Ogedei as Great Khan. According to the sources, he showed little interest at first, and Tolui had to be persuaded to support him.
[VISUAL: kurultai of 1229, Ogedei accepting the title]
Tolui died soon after, in 1232. The Secret History says he drank a cup of poisoned wine to save Ogedei, who was ill with shamanic sickness. Historians doubt the story. Tolui's sons, however, would later claim the throne.

## 3. THE END OF THE JIN (3:00–5:30)
**NARRATION**
Ogedei's first goal was to finish what his father had started: the conquest of the Jin dynasty in northern China.
[VISUAL: map of the Jin empire, Mongol armies]
In 1231 to 1232, a Mongol army under Tolui and Subutai went around the Jin defences by crossing Song territory. At the Battle of Sanfeng in 1232, the Jin army was destroyed. Then the siege of Kaifeng, the capital, began. Mongols used gunpowder weapons of the Jin, including the thunder-crash bombs, against them. The Jin Emperor fled.
[VISUAL: siege of Kaifeng, thunder-crash bombs]
In 1234, Mongol and Song armies together besieged Caizhou. The last Jin emperor killed himself. The Song, hoping to regain old lands, had joined the Mongols. Soon they found out their mistake: the Mongols would not leave the territory. Within a year, the two were at war.
[VISUAL: the end of the Jin, Song-Mongol alliance turning to war]
In the 1230s, the Mongols also advanced into Korea, Persia and the Caucasus. In Persia, the general Chormaqan took Tabriz and Georgia. In Korea, a long war led to the king's retreat to Ganghwa island in 1232.

## 4. ADMINISTRATION AND KARAKORUM (5:30–9:00)
**NARRATION**
Ogedei is remembered for turning a nomad army into a functioning empire. His chief advisor was a Khitan scholar named Yelu Chucai, who is said to have saved northern China from destruction. When some Mongol nobles wanted to turn China's farmland into pasture and kill the peasants, Yelu Chucai argued that taxing the farmers would produce more wealth. This was a classic argument, and Ogedei accepted it.
[VISUAL: Yelu Chucai, farmers, tax ledger]
A census was conducted in 1234 to 1236 across northern China, to organise taxation. Ogedei also expanded the yam relay system and set up depots and granaries.
In 1235, he began building a capital in Mongolia: Karakorum. It was a mix of tents and permanent buildings, with a Chinese-style palace, a mosque, a Buddhist temple, and a Christian church. The famous silver tree fountain, made by the French goldsmith Guillaume Boucher, which poured out four kinds of drinks through silver lions, was added later, under Ogedei's successor Mongke, and was seen by the friar William of Rubruck in 1254.
[VISUAL: Karakorum reconstruction, silver tree fountain]
Ogedei also introduced paper money in the Mongol lands. He issued notes called chao, probably in the 1230s, based on Chinese and Jin examples. Historians say he did not overprint, and the notes kept their value, which was an unusual achievement.
[VISUAL: chao banknote]
He was famous for generosity. The Persian historian Juvaini says he gave away treasury goods, to merchants and others, to the point of wastefulness. He valued honesty: when he was told that a merchant had cheated, he paid the debts himself. But this also shows that the court was spending far beyond its income, and that the khan often listened to merchants who got rich, and tax-farmers who squeezed the peasants.

## 5. THE WESTERN CAMPAIGN (9:00–13:00)
**NARRATION**
In 1235, a kurultai decided to launch a great campaign to the west. The aim was to conquer the lands of the Kipchaks, Bulgars and Rus', and to make good the earlier raid of Subutai and Jebe.
[VISUAL: kurultai of 1235, map of the western campaign]
The army was led by Batu, son of Jochi, with the veteran general Subutai as the strategist. Many Mongol princes took part, including Ogedei's son Guyuk, and Mongke, the son of Tolui. It was a huge force of perhaps 50,000 to 120,000 men; sources vary.
In 1236 they crushed the Volga Bulgars. In the winter of 1237 to 1238, they attacked the Russian principalities one after another, using the frozen rivers as roads. Ryazan, Moscow and Vladimir fell. In 1240, they took Kiev. That story we will tell in the video on Batu.
[VISUAL: winter campaign on frozen rivers, burning towns]
In spring 1241, the army split. One part went into Poland, and defeated the Polish and German forces at Legnica on 9 April. Another went through the Carpathians into Hungary, and on 11 April crushed King Bela IV at the Sajo river, near Mohi. Parts of the army were in Croatia and Austria by the end of the year.
[VISUAL: Legnica and Mohi, Hungary's flatlands]
It looked as if nothing would stop them.

## 6. THE DEATH THAT CHANGED THE MAP (13:00–15:30)
**NARRATION**
Then, in December 1241, Ogedei died. He had been drinking heavily, a habit he had had for years. His advisors had warned him, and his brother Chagatai had even put guards over his cups. But he told them that he could not stop. His death, according to the Persian sources, came after a long night's drink with a favourite courtier.
[VISUAL: the last banquet, cup of wine, empty cup]
There was another story: that his wife, Toregene, or a female rival had him poisoned. It is not accepted by most historians.
When news of the death reached Batu in 1242, he turned his army back. Mongol custom required the princes to attend the kurultai to choose a new khan. Batu also had a bitter rivalry with Guyuk and could not afford to be away.
[VISUAL: Batu receiving the news, army turning east]
Some historians say this saved Europe. Others say the Mongols were already overstretched. The Hungarian plains offered limited pasture for their horses, the country was full of castles, and the Mongols may have decided to withdraw anyway. Whatever the reason, the Mongols did not return in strength.

## 7. LEGACY AND DEBATE (15:30–17:30)
**NARRATION**
After Ogedei's death, his widow Toregene ruled as regent for five years, and then his son Guyuk became khan in 1246. Guyuk died in 1248. In 1251, the throne passed to Mongke, a son of Tolui. The house of Ogedei lost the khanate. In later years, Ogedei's grandson Kaidu would fight Kublai for decades.
[VISUAL: succession diagram: Ogedei to Guyuk to Mongke]
The reign of Ogedei saw the empire at its widest in territory and also saw the first signs of its strain: a monarchy which depended on personal ties, a growing split between the Chinese-style and steppe-style parts, and the problem of succession.
Historians still ask: did his death save Europe? Was his tolerance a weakness or a strength? Was Yelu Chucai really as influential as the Chinese sources claim?
