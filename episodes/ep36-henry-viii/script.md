# Ep 36 — Henry VIII: The King Who Broke with Rome
Target: ~7 min (1029 words) · Status: DRAFT, facts checked by search first (see factcheck/), still needs a human check against books

## 1. HOOK (0:00–0:50)
**NARRATION**
In 1509, an eighteen-year-old prince became King of England. He was tall, athletic, a good musician, a keen jouster, and a scholar who spoke several languages. He was widely seen as the golden hope of the kingdom. By the time he died in 1547, he had married six times, executed two queens, had his closest advisers beheaded, broken England's centuries-old tie to the Pope, and closed every monastery in the country.
[VISUAL: young Henry VIII, then an older, heavier king]
This is the story of Henry VIII, and how a king's search for a male heir changed England forever.
[VISUAL: Tudor rose, map of England]

## 2. THE SPARE HEIR (0:50–3:00)
**NARRATION**
Henry was born on 28 June 1491, the second son of Henry VII, the founder of the Tudor dynasty. His father had won the crown at Bosworth in 1485, ending the Wars of the Roses, and the family's hold on the throne was still fragile. Henry's elder brother Arthur was the heir. In 1502 Arthur died, and Henry became heir to the throne.
[VISUAL: Henry VII, Prince Arthur, young Henry]
Henry VII died in April 1509. His son took the throne, at 17, and a few weeks later married his brother's widow, Catherine of Aragon, daughter of the king and queen of Spain. For that he needed a dispensation from the Pope, since the Church forbade marriage to a brother's widow. The matter would return later.
In his early reign, Henry was more interested in war and glory than in government. His chief minister was Thomas Wolsey, a butcher's son who became Archbishop of York and Cardinal. In 1520, at the Field of the Cloth of Gold in France, Henry met Francis I in a spectacular show of wealth.
[VISUAL: Catherine of Aragon, Wolsey, Field of the Cloth of Gold]

## 3. THE GREAT MATTER (3:00–6:30)
**NARRATION**
By the mid-1520s, Henry had a problem. After about eighteen years of marriage, Catherine had borne him only one surviving child: a daughter, Mary. Henry wanted a son to secure the Tudor dynasty. He began to believe that his marriage was cursed, because he had married his brother's widow, citing a passage in the Book of Leviticus.
[VISUAL: Henry and Catherine, Princess Mary]
He also had fallen in love with Anne Boleyn, a lady at court. Henry asked the Pope for an annulment. The Pope, Clement VII, was under the control of Catherine's nephew, the Holy Roman Emperor Charles V, whose troops had sacked Rome in 1527. He could not and would not annul the marriage.
Wolsey, who failed to obtain the annulment, fell from power in 1529, and died in 1530 on his way to trial. Thomas More succeeded him as Lord Chancellor.
[VISUAL: Anne Boleyn, Pope Clement VII, Wolsey's fall]
Henry now looked for another way. With the help of Thomas Cromwell, a lawyer and administrator, he turned to Parliament. Over the early 1530s, a series of laws cut England's ties with Rome. In 1533 Thomas Cranmer, the new Archbishop of Canterbury, declared Henry's marriage to Catherine invalid. Henry had already secretly married Anne Boleyn, in January 1533. In 1534, the Act of Supremacy made the king Supreme Head of the Church of England.
[VISUAL: Cromwell, Cranmer, the Act of Supremacy]

## 4. THE COST OF DISSENT (6:30–9:00)
**NARRATION**
Many in England did not accept the change. Thomas More, the humanist scholar and former Chancellor, refused to swear an oath accepting Henry as head of the Church. He was executed on 6 July 1535. So was Bishop John Fisher. They became martyrs for the Catholic cause.
[VISUAL: Thomas More, Fisher, the Tower]
Anne Boleyn gave birth to a daughter, Elizabeth, in 1533, not the son Henry wanted. In 1536, she was accused of adultery, incest and treason, charges that most historians believe were false, and she was executed on 19 May 1536. Henry married Jane Seymour days later. In October 1537, she bore him the son he wanted, Edward, and died within two weeks.
[VISUAL: Anne's trial, Jane Seymour, baby Edward]

## 5. THE DISSOLUTION OF THE MONASTERIES (9:00–11:30)
**NARRATION**
Henry also needed money. His wars were costly, and the Church was rich. Between 1536 and 1540, on Cromwell's plan, the monasteries of England were closed. The first act in 1536 dissolved the smaller houses, those with incomes below two hundred pounds a year. A second act in 1539 dissolved the larger ones. The Crown seized their lands and treasures, and then sold much of the land to the gentry, creating a new class with a stake in the changes.
[VISUAL: ruined abbeys, Fountains, Tintern]
Many people were angry. In October 1536, a large rebellion broke out in the north of England, the Pilgrimage of Grace, led by the lawyer Robert Aske. The rebels demanded the end of the changes and the removal of Cromwell. Henry promised a pardon, then crushed the rebellion, executing Aske and, by some sources, about 178 others.
[VISUAL: Pilgrimage of Grace, Robert Aske]

## 6. THE LATER WIVES AND THE LATER YEARS (11:30–14:30)
**NARRATION**
In 1540, Henry married Anne of Cleves, a German princess, for political reasons, after seeing a portrait of her. He found her unattractive. The marriage was never consummated and was annulled after about six months. The same year, Cromwell, who had arranged it, fell and was executed on 28 July 1540. On that same day, Henry married Catherine Howard, a teenager, who was beheaded for adultery in February 1542.
[VISUAL: Anne of Cleves, Cromwell's execution, Catherine Howard]
His last wife, Catherine Parr, married him in 1543, and survived him. She was a scholar and caring nurse who looked after the king's painful leg ulcers and his daughters' education.
Henry's health declined. A jousting accident in 1536 left him with a leg wound that never healed. He became very heavy. In his last years he was often ill and in pain, and paranoid, ordering executions of people he suspected of disloyalty.
[VISUAL: Catherine Parr, ageing Henry]
He also fought costly wars with France and Scotland. In 1545 his flagship, the Mary Rose, sank in the Solent as he watched. He died on 28 January 1547. His son Edward VI became king at nine. After Edward, two daughters would rule: Mary I and Elizabeth I.
[VISUAL: the Mary Rose, Henry's death, the three children]

## 7. LEGACY AND DEBATE (14:30–16:30)
**NARRATION**
Henry's reign created the Church of England, made Parliament more important in law-making, and changed the ownership of land. He built a navy, and the first royal dockyards. He also left a country in religious turmoil, a damaged currency, and a debt.
[VISUAL: Hampton Court, royal navy]
Historians still debate. How much was Henry's own doing, and how much Cromwell's? Was the English Reformation popular or imposed? And was Henry a gifted ruler who became a tyrant, or a self-absorbed ruler from the beginning? Historians such as Eric Ives, G.W. Bernard and J.J. Scarisbrick offer different answers.
