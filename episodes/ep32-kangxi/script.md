# Ep 32 — Kangxi: The Emperor Who Ruled China for 61 Years
Target: ~10 min (1398 words) · Status: DRAFT — must pass fact-check (docs/04) before voiceover

## 1. HOOK (0:00–0:50)
**NARRATION**
In 1669, in the Forbidden City in Beijing, a fifteen-year-old emperor invited the most powerful man in the empire, the regent Oboi, into his chamber. Oboi had been the strongman of the court for years, and had killed rival ministers. The young emperor had gathered a group of strong young bodyguards. They seized Oboi, and arrested him. The teenage emperor was now in control.
[VISUAL: young emperor, Oboi's arrest]
He was Kangxi, the second Qing emperor and the longest-reigning ruler in Chinese history, with 61 years on the throne. He crushed a rebellion that nearly ended the dynasty, took Taiwan, signed China's first treaty with a European power, and made the Manchu conquerors accepted rulers of China. This is his story.
[VISUAL: Kangxi portrait, Qing empire map]

## 2. THE MANCHU CONQUEST (0:50–3:00)
**NARRATION**
The Qing dynasty was founded not by Chinese but by Manchus, a people from north-eastern Asia, who had been united by the chieftain Nurhaci in the early seventeenth century. In 1644, the Ming dynasty collapsed after a peasant rebel, Li Zicheng, took Beijing, and the last Ming emperor hanged himself. The Ming general Wu Sangui, who guarded the pass at Shanhaiguan, let the Manchu army enter China to defeat Li Zicheng. The Manchus took Beijing and stayed.
[VISUAL: Shanhaiguan pass, Manchu cavalry entering Beijing]
The conquest of the south took years and was brutal. The Manchus ordered all Chinese men to shave the front of their heads and wear a queue, or braid, as a sign of submission. Many resisted, and thousands died. Cities like Yangzhou and Jiading were massacred.
Kangxi's father, the Shunzhi emperor, died in 1661, probably of smallpox, aged only 22. His son, born in 1654 as Xuanye, became emperor aged seven. A council of four regents, including Oboi, ruled for him.
[VISUAL: queue edict, Yangzhou, the child emperor]

## 3. TAKING POWER (3:00–5:00)
**NARRATION**
Kangxi grew up quickly. He studied hard, in Manchu, Chinese and Mongolian, as well as archery and horse riding. He survived smallpox as a child, which left light scars on his face, and which is said to have been a reason for his father to choose him as heir, since he would have immunity.
[VISUAL: Kangxi studying, riding]
His grandmother, the Grand Empress Dowager Xiaozhuang, a Mongol, was a strong influence. Oboi's growing arrogance made him enemies. In 1669, Kangxi, aged fifteen, set up the trap. Oboi was sentenced to death, but Kangxi reduced it to imprisonment, and he died in prison. The young emperor took personal control.

## 4. THE REVOLT OF THE THREE FEUDATORIES (5:00–8:00)
**NARRATION**
His first test was the greatest danger to his dynasty. After the conquest, the Manchus had given three Chinese generals, who had helped them, almost independent territories in the south: Wu Sangui in Yunnan and Guizhou, Shang Kexi in Guangdong, and Geng Jingzhong in Fujian. These domains had their own armies and collected their own taxes. They cost the Qing court about half of its annual revenue.
[VISUAL: map of the three feudatories]
Kangxi decided to abolish them. In 1673, Shang Kexi asked to retire, and Kangxi accepted. Wu Sangui and Geng also offered to resign, expecting that the court would refuse. Kangxi accepted. Wu Sangui rebelled in December 1673, and the other two joined.
For a few years it looked as though the Qing might be driven out. Wu's army swept through the south, to the Yangtze. Kangxi was only 19. He refused to negotiate. He took command of strategy, and relied on Manchu and Chinese generals, adapting. He used the Green Standard Army of Chinese soldiers, and gave rewards to those who defected.
[VISUAL: Wu Sangui's rebellion, Kangxi at war council]
Wu Sangui declared himself emperor of a new Zhou dynasty in 1678 and died a few months later, of dysentery. The rebellion faded. In 1681, the last stronghold, Kunming, fell. Kangxi had saved the dynasty.

## 5. TAIWAN AND RUSSIA (8:00–11:00)
**NARRATION**
Next came Taiwan. Since 1662, the island had been held by the family of Zheng Chenggong, known in the West as Koxinga, who had driven the Dutch out. Zheng's descendants were loyal to the Ming and a danger to the Qing coast. In 1683, a fleet under Admiral Shi Lang, a former Zheng follower, defeated them in the Penghu islands, and the Zheng ruler surrendered. Taiwan became a prefecture of Fujian province, for the first time part of a Chinese empire. Kangxi also lifted the ban on sea trade, in 1684.
[VISUAL: Shi Lang's fleet, Penghu, Taiwan]
In the north, Russian Cossacks had moved into the Amur river valley, building forts such as Albazin. Kangxi sent an army to drive them out, and besieged Albazin in 1685 and 1686. Then, in 1689, the Treaty of Nerchinsk was signed between the Qing and Russia. It fixed the border on the Amur. It was written in Latin, Manchu and Russian, and Jesuit priests, Jean-Francois Gerbillon and Thomas Pereira, served as interpreters. It was the first treaty between China and a European power, signed on terms of equality.
[VISUAL: Albazin siege, Nerchinsk negotiations, Jesuit interpreters]

## 6. THE MONGOLS AND TIBET (11:00–12:30)
**NARRATION**
The last major threat was the Dzungars, a western Mongol people led by Galdan, who united a vast territory in Central Asia and threatened the Khalkha Mongols. In 1690, Kangxi fought Galdan at Ulan Butong, with mixed results. In 1696, he led an army across the Gobi in person, and at Zuunmod (or Jao Modo), Galdan's forces were crushed. Galdan died the next year, in 1697. The Dzungars remained a threat. Kangxi's grandson Qianlong would finish them. In 1720, Qing forces entered Lhasa and expelled the Dzungars from Tibet.
[VISUAL: Kangxi leading the army, Zuunmod, Lhasa]

## 7. THE SCHOLAR EMPEROR (12:30–15:30)
**NARRATION**
Kangxi was one of the most learned of Chinese emperors. He studied the Confucian classics, wrote poetry, practised calligraphy, and took an interest in Western science. He brought Jesuit priests to court, including Ferdinand Verbiest, who taught him geometry, astronomy and mathematics. Verbiest cast cannon for the Qing army and was head of the Imperial Board of Astronomy. Kangxi once tested the Jesuits against Chinese and Muslim astronomers in predicting the length of a shadow, and the Jesuits won.
[VISUAL: Kangxi with Verbiest, astronomical instruments]
When Kangxi had malaria, the Jesuits cured him with quinine. He was sympathetic to Christianity at first, and in 1692 issued an edict of toleration. But the Rites Controversy changed that. The Pope ruled in 1715 that Chinese converts could not take part in rites for ancestors and Confucius. Kangxi was angry, and in 1721 banned Christian missions that did not accept the rites.
[VISUAL: quinine, edict of toleration, the Pope's ruling]
He also sponsored huge scholarly works: the Kangxi Dictionary, completed in 1716, with over 47,000 characters, which remained the standard for centuries; the Complete Tang Poems, with almost 50,000 poems; and the Encyclopedia Gujin Tushu Jicheng, completed after his death.
He took six tours of the south, to inspect river works on the Yellow River and the Grand Canal, and meet local people. He wanted to win the support of Chinese scholars. He had critics. His regime censored books and punished writers in several literary inquisitions, such as the Ming History case of 1661 to 1663, under the regents.
[VISUAL: dictionary pages, southern tour, river works]

## 8. TAXES AND SUCCESSION (15:30–18:00)
**NARRATION**
In 1712, Kangxi announced that the poll tax would be fixed at the level of the previous year, and would not be raised for new births, a policy meant to ease the burden on the people. In 1713 he celebrated his sixtieth birthday with a banquet for thousands of elderly men in the Forbidden City.
[VISUAL: banquet of the elderly, population growth]
His greatest failure was the succession. He had around 35 sons. His second son, Yinreng, was made heir at the age of one. He was educated with great care. But as he grew up, Yinreng became arrogant, and was accused of misconduct. In 1708, Kangxi deposed him, restored him in 1709, and deposed him again in 1712. The princes then competed for the throne with plots and factions. Kangxi never named another heir.
[VISUAL: Yinreng, rival princes]
He died on 20 December 1722, at his villa in Beijing. The throne passed to his fourth son, Yinzhen, who became the Yongzheng emperor. Rumours spread that Yongzheng had forged the will, changing the character for fourteenth to fourth. Historians have examined this and consider it unlikely, but the question remains debated.
[VISUAL: Kangxi's deathbed, the will, the characters]

## 9. LEGACY AND DEBATE (18:00–19:30)
**NARRATION**
Kangxi's reign established the Qing as a legitimate dynasty. He expanded the empire, stabilised the economy, and began what is called the High Qing, the time of peace and prosperity under him, his son and his grandson Qianlong. China's population rose from perhaps 150 million to over 300 million during this period.
[VISUAL: population growth, map of Qing at its peak]
Historians still debate how to see him: as a Chinese emperor or as a Manchu conqueror; how far he relied on his Manchu identity; and how effective his conciliation of Chinese elites was. Pamela Crossley and Mark Elliott argue that the Manchu identity was central. Others stress his adoption of Chinese ways.
