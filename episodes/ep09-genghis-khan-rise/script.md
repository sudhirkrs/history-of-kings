# Ep 9 — Genghis Khan, Part 1: The Boy Who Was Left to Die
Target: ~10 min (1510 words) · Status: DRAFT, fact-checked by web search (see factcheck/); still needs a human check against the cited books

## 1. HOOK (0:00–0:55)
**NARRATION**
Imagine a boy of about nine years old, standing outside his family's tent on the grassland of Mongolia. His father has been poisoned by enemies. The tribe that once followed his father now refuses to follow a child. They pack up their tents and leave. They abandon a widow, her seven children, and two elderly servants, to starve through the winter.
[VISUAL: windswept steppe, a lone ger, a family watching the tribe ride away]
That boy was Temujin. Forty years later, he would be known as Genghis Khan, and he would rule the largest connected land empire in history.
[VISUAL: map of the Mongol Empire at its largest, over Asia]
In this first part, we follow his life from the day he was abandoned to the day in 1206 when the tribes of Mongolia declared him their universal ruler. In part two, we will follow what he did with that power.

## 2. THE WORLD OF THE STEPPE (0:55–3:20)
**NARRATION**
In the twelfth century, the Mongolian plateau was not a country. It was a patchwork of tribes: Mongols, Tatars, Kereits, Naimans, Merkits and others. They lived as herders, moving with their horses, sheep, goats, camels and cattle, in felt tents called gers.
[VISUAL: steppe tribes map with names, herds, gers]
They fought constantly, over pastures, animals and women, and over old insults. Alliances changed quickly. A family's strength depended on how many loyal warriors it could gather, and in this world, blood ties and personal loyalty mattered more than any state.
South of the steppe lay China, divided between the Jin dynasty in the north, ruled by Jurchens, and the Song dynasty in the south. The Jin liked to play the tribes against each other, to keep them weak.
[VISUAL: Jin court, tribal chiefs]
Our main source for this period is the Secret History of the Mongols, a long account of Genghis Khan's life, written in Mongolian by an unknown author soon after his death. Scholars date it to 1228 or 1240, or later, and still debate which. It was kept in the royal archives. It is detailed and vivid, but it is also a family story told from the winner's point of view, so it needs care. Other sources are the Persian historians Juvaini and Rashid al-Din, and Chinese records.
[VISUAL: Secret History manuscript, Persian text, Chinese chronicle]

## 3. BORN WITH A BLOOD CLOT (3:20–5:30)
**NARRATION**
Temujin was born around 1162, though some sources say 1155 or 1167. Historians cannot settle the date. The Secret History says that he was born near the Onon river with a blood clot in his hand, a sign of a great destiny.
[VISUAL: Onon river, newborn with clenched fist]
His father was Yesugei, a chief of the Borjigin clan. His mother was Hoelun, whom Yesugei had seized from another man, a Merkit, on her wedding day. This will matter later.
Yesugei named his son after a Tatar warrior he had defeated, Temujin-uge. The name probably means something like blacksmith or iron-worker.
When Temujin was about nine, his father took him to find a bride, and left him with the family of a girl named Borte. On the way home, Yesugei met some Tatars, shared a meal, and was poisoned. He died within days.
[VISUAL: Yesugei, the bride's family, the poisoned meal]
When the news came, Temujin hurried back. But the clan refused to follow the young heir. They abandoned the family.

## 4. THE YEARS OF HARDSHIP (5:30–8:30)
**NARRATION**
Hoelun kept her children alive by gathering roots, fishing and hunting small animals. The children grew up hard.
[VISUAL: Hoelun digging roots, children fishing]
One incident shows how hard. Temujin had a half-brother named Begter, and a quarrel arose over a fish and a bird. Temujin and his full brother Khasar shot Begter with arrows. Hoelun, enraged, called them murderers. The Secret History does not hide this crime. Historians think it shows how the Mongols saw the incident: as a necessary act that secured Temujin's place as head of the family.
Soon after, a rival group, the Tayichiud, captured Temujin and held him as a prisoner with a wooden collar. One night, at a festival, he escaped by hitting a guard and hiding in a river until a friendly family hid him. This escape earned him loyal followers.
[VISUAL: Temujin with a wooden collar, hiding in the river]
At about sixteen, he returned to claim his bride, Borte. Her father agreed. As a wedding present, he gave a black sable coat. Temujin did not keep it. He presented it to the powerful Kereit chief Toghrul, also known as Ong Khan, who had once been his father's blood brother. In return, Toghrul promised to support him.
[VISUAL: sable coat presented to Toghrul]
This was the first step: a poor young man with a clever sense of how to build alliances.

## 5. THE KIDNAPPING OF BORTE (8:30–10:30)
**NARRATION**
Then came the second blow. Some years later, the Merkits, whose chief's bride Hoelun had been taken by Yesugei long ago, struck back. They raided Temujin's camp at dawn, and carried off Borte.
[VISUAL: dawn raid, Borte being taken]
Temujin, with Toghrul's army and the help of his childhood friend, Jamukha, attacked the Merkits and rescued her. But she was pregnant. Her first son, Jochi, was born soon after. Whether Jochi was Temujin's child was a question that haunted the family for the rest of their lives. Temujin always accepted him publicly as his son, but his brothers would later use the doubt against him.
[VISUAL: rescue scene, Jochi's birth]
Jamukha is an important figure. He and Temujin had sworn to be anda, blood brothers, as boys. Together they led the alliance of the steppe. But as they grew more powerful, they grew apart. Jamukha was from a noble family; Temujin was building a following that included commoners.

## 6. TEMUJIN AGAINST JAMUKHA (10:30–13:00)
**NARRATION**
Around the late 1180s, the two split. Each gathered supporters. In a battle called Dalan Balzhut, in around 1187, Jamukha beat Temujin. According to some accounts, Jamukha boiled captured enemies alive, which made many leaders turn against him. Historians treat the story with caution, but it shows how later traditions contrasted the two men.
[VISUAL: two camps, Dalan Balzhut]
Temujin attracted men who valued loyalty and talent. He rewarded people by merit. Subutai, a blacksmith's son, would become his greatest general. Jebe was an enemy archer who had shot his horse in battle; Temujin liked his honesty and promoted him. Muqali came from a minor family.
[VISUAL: Subutai, Jebe, Muqali as portraits]
In 1189, a group of nobles elected Temujin as Khan of the Mongols, taking the title Genghis Khan or Chinggis Khan. Historians are not sure of this date or of the title at this stage.
Then he began to destroy his enemies, one by one. In 1196, with Jin help, he defeated the Tatars, who had poisoned his father. In 1203, after a break with Toghrul, he crushed the Kereit. In 1204, he defeated the Naimans, the last major rival. Jamukha was betrayed by his own men and brought to Temujin. According to the Secret History, Temujin offered friendship again, but Jamukha asked for a noble death without bloodshed. He was executed, probably in 1206.
[VISUAL: victory maps, Jamukha's last meeting]

## 7. THE KURULTAI OF 1206 (13:00–15:00)
**NARRATION**
In the spring of 1206, near the source of the Onon river, the chiefs of all the tribes met in a great assembly, the kurultai. They raised a nine-tailed white standard and proclaimed Temujin as Chinggis Khan. The name probably means something like the universal or oceanic ruler. The Mongols were now one people under one ruler.
[VISUAL: kurultai gathering, nine-tailed white banner]
He did not just rule. He reorganised everything.
First, the army. He reorganised it into decimal units: tens, hundreds, thousands and ten thousands. The decimal system was an old steppe tradition, but Genghis made it the backbone of the state. Soldiers were loyal to their unit and commander, not to their old tribes. He broke up old tribes and mixed their members. Leaders were promoted by ability.
[VISUAL: decimal army diagram arban, jaghun, mingghan, tumen]
Second, he created a personal guard of about ten thousand, the keshig, picked from the sons of nobles and commoners. They served as bodyguards, administrators and a training school for officers.
Third, he ordered a written script. The Mongols had no writing. Genghis took a captured Uyghur scribe, Tata-tonga, and had Mongolian written in Uyghur script. This allowed record-keeping and laws.
Fourth, a set of laws, often called the Yassa. Historians debate how much of it was written down and how much was custom. It banned the kidnapping of women, theft of animals, and it favoured loyalty and order.
[VISUAL: Uyghur script, law scroll]
Finally, he rewarded his followers. He gave out grants of people and herds to 95 commanders, who became the new elite.

## 8. THE FIRST CAMPAIGNS (15:00–17:30)
**NARRATION**
With the steppe united, Genghis had a problem: a large army with nothing to do. His answer was to look outwards.
[VISUAL: map of Mongol attacks on neighbours]
In 1205 to 1209, he attacked the Tangut kingdom of Western Xia, in north-west China. After several campaigns, the Tangut ruler submitted and gave a daughter in marriage. In 1209, the Uyghurs, a Turkic people in the Tarim basin, voluntarily submitted. They became useful allies, supplying scribes and administrators.
In 1211, he turned on the Jin dynasty. This was a vast empire with a population of tens of millions, against a Mongol nation of perhaps one or two million. But the Mongols were better at war. They used speed, feigned retreats, and a deadly mounted archer.
[VISUAL: Mongol archers, Great Wall, Jin cities]
Siege warfare was the Mongols' weakness at the start. They learned fast from Chinese and Muslim engineers. In 1215, they took the Jin capital, Zhongdu, today's Beijing. The Jin court moved south, and the dynasty was doomed.
[VISUAL: siege of Zhongdu, engineers]
Genghis left the Chinese campaign to his general Muqali and turned west. A new, much larger target was waiting.
