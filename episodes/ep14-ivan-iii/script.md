# Ep 14 — Ivan III: The Man Who Made Russia
Target: ~10 min (1396 words) · Status: DRAFT — must pass fact-check (docs/04) before voiceover

## 1. HOOK (0:00–0:50)
**NARRATION**
In the autumn of 1480, two armies stood on opposite banks of a small river called the Ugra, about 200 kilometres south-west of Moscow. On one side was Akhmed Khan of the Great Horde, successor of the Mongols who had ruled Russia for 240 years. On the other, the Grand Prince of Moscow. For weeks they watched each other across the water. They exchanged arrows, but no real battle took place. Then, in November, the Tatar army turned and left.
[VISUAL: two armies on opposite banks of a river, autumn mist]
That was the end of the Mongol yoke in Russia. And the man standing on the Russian bank was Ivan III, later called Ivan the Great. In his 43-year reign, he turned Moscow from a modest principality into the core of a new state, and laid the foundations of the Russia we know. This is his story.
[VISUAL: map of Muscovy growing over time]

## 2. MOSCOW'S RISE (0:50–3:00)
**NARRATION**
In the previous video, we saw how Batu Khan's Mongols crushed the Russian lands in 1237 to 1240. Moscow was then a small town. Within a few generations, its princes found a way to rise under Mongol rule. They collected tribute for the khans, and in return were given the title of Grand Prince. They bought land, married well, and in 1326 the head of the Orthodox Church, the Metropolitan, moved to Moscow. This gave the city religious prestige.
[VISUAL: Moscow princes at Sarai, Metropolitan moving to Moscow]
In 1380, Dmitri Donskoi, prince of Moscow, beat the Mongol general Mamai at Kulikovo. It was a famous victory, but two years later the Horde's ruler Tokhtamysh burned Moscow, and Moscow had to pay tribute again.
By the middle of the fifteenth century, the Horde was breaking up into separate khanates, including Kazan and Crimea. Moscow had a rival, the Grand Duchy of Lithuania, which had taken much of the old Rus' lands, including Kiev. And Moscow itself was torn by a civil war between members of the ruling family.
[VISUAL: Kulikovo, Horde splitting into Kazan, Crimea, Great Horde, Lithuania expanding]

## 3. A BLIND FATHER AND A YOUNG SON (3:00–5:00)
**NARRATION**
Ivan was born on 22 January 1440, the son of Grand Prince Vasily II. His father's reign was a nightmare. His cousin Dmitry Shemyaka rebelled, captured Vasily in 1446 and had him blinded, which is why Vasily is known as Vasily the Blind. Ivan, then six years old, was held hostage, then released.
[VISUAL: young Ivan, Vasily the Blind]
Vasily recovered his throne in 1447. He made Ivan co-ruler, perhaps around 1449, to ensure his succession. Ivan grew up in the shadow of civil war, and learned that the greatest danger to a prince was other princes. As a teenager he took part in military campaigns against the Tatars, and learned to be careful and calculating.
Vasily II died in 1462. Ivan, aged twenty-two, became Grand Prince of Moscow.

## 4. GATHERING THE RUSSIAN LANDS (5:00–9:00)
**NARRATION**
Ivan's great work was the gathering of Russian lands. He did it patiently, with a mix of purchase, marriage, diplomacy and war. When Moscow took over a neighbour, it often kept the local elite on, but moved them away from their old base.
[VISUAL: map, Yaroslavl 1463, Rostov 1474, Novgorod, Tver]
He absorbed Yaroslavl in 1463 and Rostov in 1474 by agreement. The big prize was Novgorod, the great trading republic in the north. It controlled a huge territory stretching to the Arctic, and was governed by an assembly, the veche, and a mayor, the posadnik. Novgorod had links with the Hanseatic League.
In the 1470s, a faction in Novgorod, led by the widow Marfa Boretskaya, looked to Lithuania for protection. Ivan treated this as treason. In July 1471, at the Battle of the Shelon river, a smaller Muscovite army beat a much bigger Novgorod force. Ivan imposed a heavy fine, and treaty. Then in 1477 to 1478 he returned, and Novgorod surrendered. The veche bell, the symbol of Novgorod's liberty, was taken to Moscow. Hundreds of leading families were deported and replaced by Muscovite settlers.
[VISUAL: Shelon river battle, the veche bell being taken down]
Tver, Moscow's old rival, fell in 1485. Ryazan and Pskov were brought under control, though not annexed until Ivan's son's time. In wars with Lithuania, in 1487 to 1494 and 1500 to 1503, Ivan gained territory including Chernigov and Bryansk.
[VISUAL: Tver annexed, Lithuanian border moves west]
By his death, Muscovy had almost tripled in size, and its title changed. Ivan began to style himself Sovereign of All Rus'.

## 5. THE BYZANTINE BRIDE (9:00–11:30)
**NARRATION**
In 1453, the Ottoman Turks under Sultan Mehmed II took Constantinople, ending the Byzantine Empire. The last emperor, Constantine XI, died in the fighting. His niece, Sophia Palaiologina, was brought up in Rome under the care of the Pope.
[VISUAL: fall of Constantinople, Sophia Palaiologina]
Ivan's first wife, Maria of Tver, died in 1467. The Pope proposed that Ivan marry Sophia, hoping to bring Russia into the Catholic Church. Ivan accepted. They married in Moscow in 1472. Sophia did not convert Russia to Catholicism; she became an Orthodox believer, and had a major impact on the court, bringing Italian ideas and craftsmen.
Ivan adopted symbols of Byzantium, especially the double-headed eagle, which became the Russian emblem. Its first documented use as a Russian state emblem is on a seal of 1497. Some historians think that the importance of the marriage has been overstated. After his time, a monk named Filofey would write that Moscow was the Third Rome, since the first Rome had fallen to heresy and the second, Constantinople, to the Turks. This idea was developed under his son and grandson, not under Ivan himself. But Ivan prepared the ground.
[VISUAL: double-headed eagle, Third Rome letter]
Historians debate how big Sophia's role was. Some say she pushed Ivan to assert autocratic authority; others say these claims come from later writers who disliked her.

## 6. THE GREAT STAND ON THE UGRA (11:30–14:00)
**NARRATION**
Since the 1460s, Ivan had slowly reduced his payments to the Horde. In 1476 he stopped paying altogether. The Great Horde's khan Akhmed was furious, but busy with other enemies. Ivan also allied with the Crimean khan Mengli Giray, an enemy of Akhmed.
[VISUAL: tribute stopped, alliance with Crimea]
In 1480, Akhmed gathered his army and marched towards Moscow, hoping to join forces with the Lithuanian king Casimir. He reached the Ugra river, which marked the border. Ivan's army met him there.
Ivan hesitated. He left the front, apparently on the advice of the Church, and then returned. Some chronicles record that he wavered, and that churchmen urged him to be brave. Meanwhile, Casimir did not come, because his lands were being attacked by the Crimean Tatars.
[VISUAL: the river standoff, Ivan hesitating, Archbishop Vassian's letter]
After weeks of waiting, Akhmed left on 11 November. He was killed the following year by a rival.
Historians point out that this was not a battle, and not a total break. The Horde was already weak, and some Mongol-descended khans continued to receive payments or honours. But symbolically, the Ugra stand marked the end of the Tatar yoke. Many Russian writers made it into a national founding moment.
[VISUAL: two views, symbolic end versus gradual decline]

## 7. THE KREMLIN AND THE STATE (14:00–16:30)
**NARRATION**
Ivan also rebuilt Moscow. He hired Italian architects and engineers. The Bolognese engineer Ridolfo Fioravanti, nicknamed Aristotele, Aristotle, built the Dormition Cathedral in the Kremlin from 1475 to 1479. Italians also designed the new brick walls and towers of the Kremlin, built between 1485 and 1495: Pietro Solari designed the walls and towers, and Marco Ruffo worked on the palace and the eastern side. The red-brick walls still stand today.
[VISUAL: Dormition Cathedral, Kremlin walls and towers]
Ivan also built an administration. He gave land to military servitors, called pomeshchiki, in return for service, which created a loyal army. He collected laws in a code, the Sudebnik of 1497, which set rules for courts, fixed the time when peasants could leave their landlords, around St George's Day in autumn, and punished corrupt officials.
[VISUAL: soldiers with landgrants, Sudebnik manuscript]
He also built diplomacy. He exchanged ambassadors with the Holy Roman Empire, Hungary, Venice, Denmark, the Ottoman Sultan and the Crimean Khan, and was the first Russian ruler to be recognised internationally as a sovereign power.
Not all was positive. He crushed dissent, and he exiled and executed his opponents, including boyars. The treatment of Novgorod's elite is a harsh example. He also laid the base of an autocracy, in which the prince was unquestioned master and the boyars his servants.

## 8. THE END AND LEGACY (16:30–18:30)
**NARRATION**
His last years were dominated by a succession struggle. His eldest son, Ivan the Young, from his first marriage, died in March 1490, of an illness. A court battle followed between the faction of Ivan the Young's son Dmitry and the party of Sophia's son Vasily. In 1498 Ivan crowned Dmitry as heir; in 1502 he threw Dmitry into prison and named Vasily as heir. Dmitry died in prison in 1509.
[VISUAL: two heirs, the crowning, the prison]
Ivan died on 27 October 1505, aged sixty-five. His son Vasily III continued the work, annexing Pskov in 1510, Smolensk in 1514 and Ryazan in 1521.
[VISUAL: Ivan's tomb, Archangel Cathedral]
Historians debate: did Ivan plan the rise of Muscovy, or did he take chances as they came? Was the Ugra standoff truly a victory? How important was Sophia's role? And did the shape of the Russian state, with its autocracy and servants, begin here?
