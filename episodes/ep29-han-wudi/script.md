# Ep 29 — Emperor Wu of Han: The Emperor Who Opened the Silk Road
Target: ~10 min (1479 words) · Status: DRAFT, fact-checked by web search (see factcheck/); still needs a human check against the cited books

## 1. HOOK (0:00–0:50)
**NARRATION**
In 139 or 138 BCE, an officer named Zhang Qian left the Han capital with about a hundred men. His mission was to find an unknown people in the far west and persuade them to join a war against the Xiongnu, the steppe nomads who threatened China. He was captured by the Xiongnu, held for about ten years, or thirteen in some sources, married a Xiongnu wife, and then escaped, still carrying his emperor's staff. He travelled on to Central Asia. When he returned, thirteen years after leaving, only one companion was still with him.
[VISUAL: a lone envoy with a staff crossing a desert]
The emperor who sent him was Wu of Han, who ruled for 54 years. His wars, his taxes and his officials made Han China a great power. His reign also opened the routes we call the Silk Road, and brought Confucianism to the centre of the state. Yet he ended his life with a public apology for his mistakes.
[VISUAL: Emperor Wu, Han empire map]

## 2. THE HAN BEFORE WU (0:50–3:00)
**NARRATION**
After the First Emperor's Qin dynasty collapsed in 206 BCE, the rebel Liu Bang defeated his rivals and became Emperor Gaozu of Han in 202 BCE. The early Han rulers worked to restore the country after years of war. Taxes were light, and the government was modest.
[VISUAL: Liu Bang, early Han capital Chang'an]
One problem was the Xiongnu, a powerful confederation of steppe tribes under their ruler, the Chanyu. In 200 BCE, Gaozu was surrounded by Xiongnu forces at Baideng and barely escaped. After that, the Han followed a policy called heqin, peace through marriage. They sent Han princesses to marry Xiongnu rulers, and paid gifts of silk, grain and wine each year. The Xiongnu raided anyway.
[VISUAL: Baideng, a princess leaving for the steppe]
Wu was born in 156 BCE as Liu Che, the tenth son of Emperor Jing. In 141 BCE, aged fifteen, he became emperor. His grandmother, Empress Dowager Dou, a follower of Daoist ideas, held much power in the first years. After she died in 135 BCE, Wu began to rule on his own.

## 3. THE WAR WITH THE XIONGNU (3:00–7:00)
**NARRATION**
Wu decided to end heqin and go to war. In 133 BCE, the Han tried a trap at Mayi, hoping to ambush the Chanyu. It failed, when the Xiongnu realised they were being lured. After that, war was open.
[VISUAL: the trap at Mayi]
The turning point came with a new kind of army: mobile cavalry. Han forces had been mostly infantry and chariots. Wu built up large horse herds and trained cavalry. His two commanders were the general Wei Qing, brother of Empress Wei Zifu, a former singer, and Wei Qing's nephew Huo Qubing, a brilliant young cavalry officer.
[VISUAL: Wei Qing, Huo Qubing, Han cavalry]
In 127 BCE, Wei Qing recaptured the Ordos region, the bend of the Yellow River. In 121 BCE, Huo Qubing, only 18 or 19, led a force into the Hexi Corridor, the passage between the mountains and the desert, and defeated the Xiongnu there. This opened the road to the west.
In 119 BCE, the Han made a huge expedition across the Gobi desert, with some 100,000 cavalry and hundreds of thousands of support troops. Wei Qing and Huo Qubing struck deep into Mongolia. The Chanyu fled. Huo Qubing performed a ritual at Mount Langjuxu. It was a massive victory, but the cost was heavy: the Han are said to have lost most of their horses.
[VISUAL: expedition across the Gobi, ritual on the mountain]
Huo Qubing died young in 117 BCE, aged 23. Wu had a magnificent tomb built for him near his own, with a stone horse trampling a barbarian, which can still be seen.
Wars with the Xiongnu continued for decades without final victory. But the Han secured the northern frontier, set up military colonies and extended the wall west.

## 4. THE ROAD TO THE WEST (7:00–9:30)
**NARRATION**
Zhang Qian's second mission, in 119 BCE, took him to the Wusun people, and his deputies reached Parthia and other lands. He brought back reports about Ferghana, with its fine horses, about Bactria, India and Parthia. He also brought back grapes, alfalfa, and other plants.
[VISUAL: Zhang Qian's routes on a map, grapes and alfalfa]
Wu wanted these Heavenly Horses, which sweated blood, according to the legend. When the king of Dayuan, or Ferghana, refused to hand them over and killed an envoy, Wu sent an army under Li Guangli. The first expedition in 104 BCE failed. The second, in 102 to 101 BCE, with some 60,000 men, besieged the capital and forced Ferghana to give thousands of horses. Only about a third of the army returned.
[VISUAL: Ferghana horses, Li Guangli's army]
Trade followed. Silk went west and glass, grapes, and horses came east. The Silk Road was not one road but a network. It started in Han times. The name is modern, and is usually credited to the German geographer Ferdinand von Richthofen, in 1877, though some scholars say others used it earlier.

## 5. GOVERNMENT AND CONFUCIANISM (9:30–12:00)
**NARRATION**
War costs money. Wu faced a financial crisis. His government introduced several measures. The state took over salt and iron production, creating government monopolies, around 119 to 117 BCE. It controlled the minting of coins. It introduced a tax on merchants' property and on carts and boats. It also created a system for buying grain cheaply and selling it when prices rose, to stabilise prices.
[VISUAL: salt works, iron foundry, coins]
Officials who devised this, such as Sang Hongyang, a merchant's son, were controversial. After Wu's death, in 81 BCE, a court debate on salt and iron was recorded in a book, the Discourses on Salt and Iron, where Confucian scholars attacked the monopolies.
Wu also made Confucianism the basis of state training. On the advice of Dong Zhongshu, in 136 BCE he set up academicians for the Five Classics, and in 124 BCE the Imperial Academy, where students studied the classics. Officials were chosen by recommendation, with knowledge of the Classics valued. In practice, Wu used Legalist methods too, and relied on harsh officials. Historians say the Han combined both, a mix sometimes described as Confucian on the outside and Legalist on the inside.
[VISUAL: Dong Zhongshu, Imperial Academy, classics]

## 6. EXPANSION IN THE SOUTH AND EAST (12:00–13:30)
**NARRATION**
Wu's armies also moved south and east. In 111 BCE, they conquered Nanyue, a kingdom in the area of Guangzhou and northern Vietnam. In 109 to 108 BCE, they overthrew the kingdom of Wiman Joseon in northern Korea, and set up four commanderies there, including Lelang. Parts of the south-west were also brought in.
[VISUAL: map of Nanyue, Korea, south-west]
By the end of his reign, the Han Empire was almost twice as large as when he began. 

## 7. THE HISTORIAN (13:30–15:00)
**NARRATION**
Wu's reign produced China's most famous historian. Sima Qian was the court astrologer and historian, who inherited the job from his father, Sima Tan, and planned a history of everything from the legendary Yellow Emperor to his own time.
[VISUAL: Sima Qian at work]
In 99 BCE, a general named Li Ling was defeated by the Xiongnu and surrendered. Everyone at court condemned him. Sima Qian defended him. Wu was furious and sentenced Sima Qian to death. In the Han, a man could be spared death by paying money or by castration. Sima Qian had no money, and chose castration, a humiliation, so that he could complete his book. The Records of the Grand Historian, about 526,000 characters in 130 chapters, is the foundation of Chinese historical writing.
Sima Qian wrote in the Records that Wu was greedy and extravagant, in his chapter on the Xiongnu and the Fengshan sacrifice. It took courage.
[VISUAL: the punishment, bamboo scrolls]

## 8. THE LAST YEARS (15:00–17:30)
**NARRATION**
Wu's last decades were dark. Costs of war had left the treasury empty, people were crushed by taxes, and revolts broke out. His faith in magicians and immortality made him vulnerable to charlatans.
[VISUAL: Wu with magicians, empty granaries]
In 91 BCE, a court scandal known as the witchcraft affair broke out. Wu was ill and suspected that someone was cursing him with witchcraft. Officials, especially a man named Jiang Chong, accused the crown prince Liu Ju and Empress Wei. Liu Ju, in fear, rose in revolt and was defeated. He and Empress Wei killed themselves. Tens of thousands of people died in the purge. Later, Wu realised that the charges were false, executed Jiang Chong's family, and built a Palace of Longing for Son.
[VISUAL: the witchcraft affair, the Palace of Longing]
In 89 BCE, he issued an edict, the Edict of Luntai, in which he expressed regret for his wars, and announced an end to expansion and a focus on agriculture. Historians debate whether this is genuine, or a later creation, but it fits what followed. He chose an eight-year-old son, Liu Fuling, as heir, and appointed regents, led by Huo Guang. Wu died on 29 March 87 BCE, though some modern sources give 86 BCE.
[VISUAL: the edict, young heir, Huo Guang]

## 9. LEGACY AND DEBATE (17:30–19:00)
**NARRATION**
Wu left a much larger empire, a sophisticated administration, and a state ideology that would last for centuries. But also exhausted finances, a decreased population, and a succession crisis. Some historians estimate that the population dropped by up to half during his reign, though the evidence is not firm.
[VISUAL: empire map, population graph with a note: disputed]
Historians still ask: was Wu a great emperor or an overreaching one? Was the Xiongnu war necessary? How much of his reputation depends on Sima Qian and Ban Gu? And how far was the Edict of Luntai his real thought?
