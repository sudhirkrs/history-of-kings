# Ep 12 — Kublai Khan: The Mongol Who Became Emperor of China
Target: ~9 min (1361 words) · Status: DRAFT — must pass fact-check (docs/04) before voiceover

## 1. HOOK (0:00–0:50)
**NARRATION**
In 1274 and again in 1281, two huge fleets set sail from Korea and China to invade Japan. The second fleet, according to the sources, had 4,400 ships and over 100,000 men. It was the largest seaborne invasion the world had seen. Then a great storm hit, and much of the fleet was wrecked. The Japanese called it the kamikaze, the divine wind.
[VISUAL: massive fleet, typhoon, ships breaking apart]
The man who ordered this was Kublai Khan, grandson of Genghis. He completed the conquest of China, founded the Yuan dynasty, and ruled the biggest empire on earth. In this video, we follow how a prince from the steppe became emperor of the Chinese world, and why the empire he led began to break apart.
[VISUAL: Kublai Khan portrait, map of the Yuan dynasty]

## 2. A PRINCE IN CHINA (0:50–3:30)
**NARRATION**
Kublai was born in 1215, the fourth son of Tolui and a Christian princess, Sorghaghtani Beki. She was a remarkable woman who made sure that her four sons got an excellent education. One of them, Mongke, became Great Khan; another, Hulagu, conquered Persia; another, Ariq Boke, would fight Kublai for the throne; and Kublai himself became the ruler of China.
[VISUAL: Sorghaghtani Beki with her four sons]
As a young man, Kublai was drawn to China. In 1251, when Mongke became khan, he made Kublai the governor of the Mongol territories in northern China. Kublai gathered Chinese scholars, Buddhist and Daoist monks, and Tibetan lamas. He invited a Chan Buddhist monk, Haiyun, and a Confucian scholar, Liu Bingzhong, who advised him that a conqueror could win the land on horseback but could not rule it on horseback.
[VISUAL: Kublai with Chinese advisors, Haiyun, Liu Bingzhong]
He built a new city in Inner Mongolia called Kaiping, later Shangdu, which Europeans would call Xanadu. And in the early 1250s he led a campaign in the south-west, into Yunnan, conquering the kingdom of Dali.
Mongke and Kublai started the war on the Southern Song in 1258. During this campaign, in August 1259, Mongke died, possibly of dysentery, at the siege of the Diaoyu fortress in Sichuan. Everything changed.

## 3. THE CIVIL WAR (3:30–6:00)
**NARRATION**
Mongke had not named an heir. Kublai heard the news while he was near the Yangtze. He negotiated a truce with the Song and rushed north. In the spring of 1260, at Kaiping, his supporters held a kurultai and named him Great Khan.
[VISUAL: Kublai's kurultai at Kaiping]
In Mongolia, his younger brother Ariq Boke, who had been left in charge of the capital Karakorum, held his own kurultai and was elected khan too. The Mongol Empire now had two Great Khans.
For four years, they fought a civil war. Kublai had the advantage of China's wealth. He cut off Ariq Boke's food supplies from China, and the Mongolian heartland suffered famine. In 1264, Ariq Boke surrendered.
[VISUAL: two armies, grain shortage, Ariq Boke's surrender]
But the empire had changed. Other Mongol princes no longer accepted the authority of the Great Khan. Berke, ruler of the western part, the Golden Horde, and the Chagatai khans in Central Asia pursued their own interests. In 1269, there was a meeting on the Talas river where they agreed to be independent. The Mongol Empire was now four separate khanates: the Yuan in China, the Golden Horde in Russia, the Chagatai in Central Asia and the Ilkhanate in Persia.
[VISUAL: map of four khanates]

## 4. THE FOUNDING OF THE YUAN (6:00–8:30)
**NARRATION**
Kublai made his main capital in the city of Zhongdu, the old Jin capital, and built a new city next to it, Dadu, the Great Capital, which is the origin of today's Beijing. It was a planned city with wide avenues, a palace, a bell tower and a drum tower. The work started in 1267.
[VISUAL: Dadu layout, palace, gates]
In 1271, Kublai adopted the Chinese dynastic name Yuan, from a phrase in the Book of Changes meaning "the origin" or "the primal force". This was a political move: it declared that he was a Chinese emperor and not only a Mongol khan.
But he kept Mongol ways too. He divided his subjects into four classes. At the top were the Mongols. Then came the Semu, a class of Central Asians and others, followed by the northern Chinese, and last, the southern Chinese, the people of the former Southern Song. This system gave the Mongols and their foreign allies the best jobs. Historians still debate how strictly it was applied.
[VISUAL: four-class pyramid]

## 5. CONQUEST OF THE SONG (8:30–11:00)
**NARRATION**
The Southern Song was the richest state in the world. It had a population of tens of millions, a great navy, and the best technology.
[VISUAL: Song cities, Hangzhou, ships]
The key was the twin cities of Xiangyang and Fancheng, on the Han river, which controlled the road to the Yangtze. The Mongols besieged them for five years, from 1268 to 1273. They brought two Persian engineers, who built counterweight trebuchets that could smash city walls. In 1273, Xiangyang fell.
[VISUAL: Xiangyang siege, counterweight trebuchet]
In 1276, the Mongol general Bayan took the Song capital, Lin'an, today's Hangzhou. The Song child emperor surrendered. Some loyalists fled south, and in 1279, in a naval battle at Yamen, the last Song boy-emperor was taken by his minister and jumped into the sea. China was united under foreign rule for the first time.
[VISUAL: surrender at Lin'an, naval battle at Yamen]
Kublai treated the Song court with care. The child emperor was sent to Tibet to become a monk. Many Song officials were kept in service.

## 6. THE BEYOND-CHINA CAMPAIGNS (11:00–13:30)
**NARRATION**
With China conquered, Kublai wanted more. He sent armies to Japan twice, in 1274 and 1281, but failed. The first fleet of about 900 ships landed at Hakata Bay, and then, after a storm, withdrew. The second, much larger force, was ruined by a typhoon in the summer of 1281.
[VISUAL: Hakata Bay, samurai versus Mongol soldiers, typhoon]
In Southeast Asia, the campaigns had mixed results. Burma's Pagan kingdom was weakened in the 1280s. Campaigns against Vietnam, in 1285 and 1287, were beaten back by the Tran dynasty with guerrilla tactics, and the Mongols fought in swamps and jungles with heavy casualties. A naval expedition to Java in 1293 was tricked by a local ruler and withdrew.
[VISUAL: Vietnamese guerrilla warfare, Java expedition]
These campaigns cost a fortune and fell on the Chinese people, who built ships and provided supplies. Kublai also faced rebellion from his own family. In the 1270s and 1280s, the Mongol prince Kaidu, a grandson of Ogedei, led a long war against him in Central Asia.

## 7. HOW HE RULED (13:30–16:00)
**NARRATION**
Kublai made some lasting changes. He built a new canal, the Grand Canal extension from the Yangtze to Dadu, which carried grain to the capital. He improved roads and the yam relay posts.
[VISUAL: Grand Canal, grain barges]
He promoted trade. Merchants, many of them Muslims, travelled in large caravans. He also used paper money, called chao, printed on mulberry bark paper, as the main currency. The government accepted it for taxes, and banned the use of silver for trade. This made trade easier but led to inflation later on, when the government printed too much.
[VISUAL: Yuan paper note, merchant caravans]
He was tolerant in religion. He gave positions of honour to Tibetan Buddhists, such as the young monk Phags-pa, who made a new script for the Mongol language, and he received Christian and Muslim visitors. Muslim astronomers worked in his observatory.
Marco Polo, a Venetian, claimed that he served Kublai for seventeen years from about 1275 to 1292. His book describes the wealth of the court, the postal system, the paper money, and the cities. Historians argue about whether Polo really went to China. Some argue he left out things like tea, footbinding and the Great Wall. Others defend him, noting that he gives details no one could invent. The debate continues.
[VISUAL: Marco Polo's book, caravan, Dadu]

## 8. THE LAST YEARS AND LEGACY (16:00–18:20)
**NARRATION**
Kublai's last years were sad. His favourite wife, Chabi, died in 1281, and his chosen heir, Zhenjin, died in 1285. He grew heavy, suffered from gout, and drank too much. His financial ministers, such as the Muslim Ahmad Fanakati, and later Sangha, were hated for squeezing taxes, and he had to dismiss them.
[VISUAL: ageing Kublai, Chabi's portrait]
He died on 18 February 1294, aged seventy-eight. His grandson Temur Oljeitu succeeded him. The Yuan dynasty lasted until 1368, when Han Chinese rebels under Zhu Yuanzhang founded the Ming.
[VISUAL: Kublai's funeral, Yuan collapse]
Historians debate whether he was a Chinese emperor or a Mongol khan. Some see his rule as a continuation of Chinese history; others see it as a foreign conquest. They debate the effect of the Mongol conquest on China, including the great loss of population, from perhaps 100 million to much less, though the sources are weak. They also discuss his plan for a multicultural empire.
[VISUAL: two views on cards]
