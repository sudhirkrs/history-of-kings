# Ep 4 — Shivaji: The Rebel Who Built a Kingdom
Target: ~13 min (1900 words) · Status: DRAFT — must pass fact-check (docs/04) before voiceover

## 1. HOOK (0:00–0:55)
**NARRATION**
It is November 1659, at the foot of a mountain fort in western India. Two men walk towards each other in a tent, each pretending to be unarmed. One is Afzal Khan, a famous general of the Sultan of Bijapur, sent with a large army to crush a rebel. The other is that rebel, a young chieftain in his late twenties. Within minutes, one of them will be dead.
[VISUAL: mountain fort at dawn, two figures approaching a tent]
That rebel was Shivaji. He started with a handful of hill forts and a few thousand men. Twenty-one years later, he left behind a kingdom that would outlast the greatest empire in India. In this video, we will follow his rise, his battles, his escape from the Mughal court, his coronation, and the system he built. And we will look at where the legend ends and the history begins.
[VISUAL: Maratha territory map growing from a small patch to a coastline kingdom]

## 2. THE WORLD HE WAS BORN INTO (0:55–3:30)
**NARRATION**
Shivaji was born in 1630, at the fort of Shivneri, near Pune. Some scholars prefer 1627, but 1630 is the date most widely used today.
[VISUAL: Shivneri fort, Western Ghats]
To understand him, we have to understand the politics of the Deccan. In the early 1600s, the region was ruled by three Muslim sultanates: Bijapur, Ahmednagar and Golconda. To the north, the Mughal Empire kept pushing south. Ahmednagar fell to the Mughals, and the old order started to break up.
[VISUAL: map of Bijapur, Golconda, Ahmednagar, Mughal frontier]
Shivaji's father, Shahaji Bhonsle, was a Maratha general who served different sultans, changing sides as politics required. His mother, Jijabai, was from a noble family, and she was a strong influence on him. Shahaji was sent to Bijapur's service and posted in Bangalore, while Shivaji and his mother stayed in Pune, where he was brought up under the care of an officer named Dadoji Konddev.
[VISUAL: Jijabai with young Shivaji, Pune's lal mahal]
The land he grew up in was important. The Sahyadri mountains, also called the Western Ghats, are steep, forested and cut by narrow passes. Hundreds of small forts stood on hilltops. The local Maratha chiefs and the hill people, the Mavala soldiers, knew every trail. This terrain would become his greatest weapon.
[VISUAL: forest trails, hill forts, Mavala soldiers]
It is also worth remembering that Shivaji did not start from nothing. He had his father's jagir, or land grant, and a reputation. But he was still a minor chief, and the sultanate of Bijapur was a great power.

## 3. THE FIRST FORTS (3:30–6:00)
**NARRATION**
In 1646, when he was about sixteen, Shivaji captured the fort of Torna, which belonged to Bijapur. The story goes that the commander was not strongly defended, and Shivaji took advantage of the confusion in the sultanate, as the Sultan was ill. He found treasure inside the fort, and used it to build another fort, Rajgad.
[VISUAL: Torna fort, treasure, Rajgad under construction]
Over the next ten years he took more: Kondana, Purandar, and others. He used a mix of bribes, surprise attacks and clever diplomacy. Bijapur was occupied with wars elsewhere and the Mughal threat, and could not respond strongly.
[VISUAL: map with forts lighting up one by one]
One famous episode was the capture of the rich region of Javli in 1656, which belonged to the Maratha chief Chandrarao More. Shivaji's enemies called him a traitor to fellow Marathas. His supporters said that the Mores were loyal to Bijapur and stood in the way of Maratha independence. Historians today recognise both views, and this reminds us that Shivaji's rise was not a simple fight between good and evil.
[VISUAL: Javli hills, rival Maratha chiefs]
By the late 1650s, he controlled a block of territory in the western hills and had built a small army and a few ships.

## 4. AFZAL KHAN AT PRATAPGAD (6:00–8:50)
**NARRATION**
Bijapur had had enough. In 1659, the regent sent Afzal Khan, one of its most experienced commanders, with perhaps ten to twelve thousand cavalry and artillery, to destroy Shivaji.
[VISUAL: Bijapur army marching, banners]
Afzal Khan was known as a brutal man. On the way he desecrated temples at Tuljapur and Pandharpur, according to later Maratha accounts. Shivaji pulled back to the fort of Pratapgad, deep in the forest near Mahabaleshwar. The Bijapur army could not easily follow in such terrain.
After weeks of tension, the two sides agreed to a personal meeting. Both would come alone, with a few attendants, and with no weapons.
[VISUAL: wooden pavilion, forest, tense silhouettes]
On 10 November 1659, they met. What happened next is told in several versions. The Maratha version says that Afzal Khan, a tall man, seized Shivaji in an embrace and tried to stab him. Shivaji, who was wearing armour under his clothes and carried a concealed tiger-claw weapon, the waghnakh, struck first and killed him.
[VISUAL: the embrace, waghnakh, stylised, no gore]
The Bijapur-side accounts say that Shivaji acted treacherously. Historians cannot be sure who struck first. What is certain is that Afzal Khan died, and that Shivaji's hidden troops attacked the leaderless Bijapur army and destroyed it. He captured horses, cannons and a huge amount of treasure.
[VISUAL: Pratapgad fort, ambush from the forest]
After this, his name was known all across India.

## 5. PAVAN KHIND AND THE MUGHALS (8:50–11:10)
**NARRATION**
Bijapur soon sent another army, which besieged Shivaji in the fort of Panhala in 1660. The siege was long. Shivaji planned an escape on a rainy night in July. He slipped out with several hundred men, heading for the fort of Vishalgad. The enemy chased them.
[VISUAL: night escape in the rain, torch lights]
At a narrow mountain pass, called Pavan Khind, a Maratha commander named Baji Prabhu Deshpande held off the pursuers with a small force, so that Shivaji could reach safety. Baji Prabhu, tradition says, fought on although badly wounded, until he heard the signal cannon fire that told him Shivaji had reached the fort. Then he died.
[VISUAL: narrow pass, small band of soldiers, cannon signal]
Meanwhile the Mughals under Aurangzeb, then governor of the Deccan and later emperor, took notice. In 1663, Aurangzeb's maternal uncle, Shaista Khan, was camped in Pune, in the very house where Shivaji had grown up. One April night, Shivaji and a small party sneaked into the house in disguise. In the fight, Shaista Khan lost fingers but escaped. The Mughals were humiliated.
[VISUAL: Lal Mahal at night, a hand with bandaged fingers]
In January 1664, Shivaji attacked Surat, the richest port of the Mughal Empire, and took away a large amount of wealth. Surat was a shocking target, because it was the gateway for Mughal pilgrims to Mecca. The attack cost Shivaji something in reputation, since he plundered rich merchants, but it showed the Mughals could not protect their own ports.
[VISUAL: Surat harbour, merchants, ships]

## 6. DEFEAT, AGRA AND ESCAPE (11:10–14:00)
**NARRATION**
Aurangzeb now sent his best general: Mirza Raja Jai Singh of Amer, a Rajput who had served the Mughals for years, with a large army. Jai Singh was patient. He besieged Purandar fort and cut off Shivaji's supplies.
[VISUAL: Mughal army besieging Purandar]
In June 1665, Shivaji agreed to the Treaty of Purandar. He gave up twenty-three of his thirty-five forts to the Mughals, and agreed to serve Aurangzeb. He also agreed to visit the Mughal court at Agra.
In May 1666, Shivaji arrived in Agra with his young son Sambhaji. At the court, on Aurangzeb's birthday, he was put in the third rank, behind lesser nobles. Furious, he protested openly and walked out. He was placed under guard.
[VISUAL: Agra durbar, Shivaji standing in a low rank, Aurangzeb on the throne]
What happened next is one of the most famous stories in Indian history. Shivaji fell ill, and sent out large baskets of sweets to Brahmins and holy men as charity. After some days, he and Sambhaji slipped out, hidden in the baskets, according to tradition. Historians doubt the exact details, but it is certain that in August 1666 he escaped from Agra, travelled in disguise as a holy man, and reached Maharashtra after months on the road.
[VISUAL: sweets baskets, disguised travellers, map of the long road home]
Over the next few years, he rebuilt his strength, and by 1670 he had retaken many of the forts, along with Surat once more. His great commander Tanaji Malusare had died in the capture of Kondana in 1670, giving rise to the famous saying about the fort being won but the lion lost.
[VISUAL: forts being recaptured, Tanaji's last climb]

## 7. THE CORONATION (14:00–16:00)
**NARRATION**
By 1674, Shivaji ruled a large territory, but he faced a problem of legitimacy. He was a Maratha chief, a vassal's son. The older sultans and the Mughals did not accept him as a king.
So he arranged a grand coronation. On 6 June 1674, at the fort of Raigad, he was crowned Chhatrapati, the king under the umbrella. The ceremony was performed by a famed Brahmin scholar from Varanasi, Gaga Bhatt, who found him a genealogy connecting the Bhonsle family to the Sisodias of Mewar, and carried out the rituals.
[VISUAL: Raigad coronation, water pouring, Gaga Bhatt]
Some scholars see this as a clever move to give a rising chief the religious status of a Kshatriya king. Others note that it also worked as an announcement to everyone that a new independent kingdom had arrived.
After the coronation, he led a major campaign in the south, from 1676 to 1678, taking Jinji and Vellore in what is now Tamil Nadu, and agreed to a treaty with the Sultan of Golconda. This gave the Marathas a base far from the Mughals.
[VISUAL: southern campaign map, Jinji fort]

## 8. HOW HE RULED (16:00–18:20)
**NARRATION**
What kind of state did Shivaji build?
He called it Swarajya, self-rule. He set up a council of eight ministers, the Ashtapradhan, including a prime minister called the Peshwa, a finance minister and a foreign affairs minister. He tried to pay soldiers in cash or salary rather than giving land grants to local chiefs, because he feared that hereditary landlords would weaken the state.
[VISUAL: the eight ministers as icons, a ledger]
He built a navy. The Konkan coast was dominated by the Portuguese, the Siddis of Janjira, and the English. Shivaji created a fleet and built sea forts like Sindhudurg, which he started in 1664, to protect the coast and to trade. Later Maratha admirals like Kanhoji Angre would build on this.
[VISUAL: Sindhudurg sea fort, Maratha ships]
Revenue came from land tax, but also from chauth and sardeshmukhi, which were payments, a quarter and a tenth of the revenue, collected from neighbouring areas that he did not rule. Supporters say it was a tax for protection. Critics say it was extortion in another name. This system of claims would become important for the Marathas later.
Shivaji issued strict orders against harming women, farmers' crops, mosques and holy books during campaigns. Several accounts, including those of his opponents, note that he treated captured women with respect. This is part of his reputation as a just ruler, though armies in this period were rarely gentle with civilians, and his raiding of towns like Surat left bitter memories.
[VISUAL: orders on a scroll, soldiers told to leave farmers alone]
Many Muslims served him, including his artillery chief Ibrahim Khan, and a naval officer Daulat Khan. The idea that Shivaji's fight was only about religion is not supported by his own court records. It was a political struggle for sovereignty and power, with a strong cultural dimension.

## 9. THE END (18:20–19:30)
**NARRATION**
Shivaji died at Raigad on 3 April 1680, aged about fifty, probably after a short illness. The cause of death is debated, and some accounts hint at poisoning, but historians find no firm evidence.
[VISUAL: Raigad at sunset]
His eldest son Sambhaji took the throne after a short conflict within the family. Under Aurangzeb, the Mughals invaded the Deccan for decades. But the Maratha state survived, and eventually grew into one of the major powers of eighteenth-century India.
[VISUAL: map of Maratha power in 1760]

## 10. LEGACY AND DEBATE (19:30–20:40)
**NARRATION**
Shivaji's legacy is huge, and for that reason sometimes disputed. In Maharashtra, he is a hero of the people. Over the past century, he has also been claimed by many political movements. Historians such as Jadunath Sarkar, Gajanan Mehendale and James Laine have written very different books about him, and the debates are lively.
[VISUAL: statue of Shivaji, books]
What can we say safely? He was a skilled military leader who used terrain and speed. He built an administration that lasted. He gave a regional people a state of their own. And he showed that a determined smaller power could stand against an empire.
Next time we travel far south, and almost a thousand years back, to the Chola emperor who built the Brihadisvara temple and sent ships across the sea: Rajaraja Chola. Subscribe, and tell me which king you want after him.
[VISUAL: end screen]
