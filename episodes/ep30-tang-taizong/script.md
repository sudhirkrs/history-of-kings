# Ep 30 — Tang Taizong: The Emperor Who Listened to His Critics
Target: ~10 min (1464 words) · Status: DRAFT — must pass fact-check (docs/04) before voiceover

## 1. HOOK (0:00–0:50)
**NARRATION**
On a summer morning in 626, at the Xuanwu Gate of the Tang capital Chang'an, a prince named Li Shimin and his men ambushed the crown prince Li Jiancheng and another brother, Li Yuanji. Both were killed. Within days, their father, the emperor Li Yuan, made Li Shimin his heir, and within two months, retired. A man who had murdered his brothers and forced his father off the throne became one of the most admired emperors in Chinese history.
[VISUAL: Xuanwu Gate at dawn, ambush]
He is known as Taizong, the Great Ancestor. Under him, China was peaceful, and his reign, the Zhenguan era, was held up by later generations as the model of good government. He took criticism from his ministers, and he was called Heavenly Khan by the nomads. In this video, we look at the man behind the model.
[VISUAL: Taizong portrait, Tang empire map]

## 2. THE FALL OF THE SUI AND THE RISE OF THE TANG (0:50–3:30)
**NARRATION**
China had been divided for nearly 400 years after the Han dynasty fell in 220 CE. In 589, the Sui dynasty reunited the land. The Sui built the Grand Canal, connecting the Yellow River and the Yangtze, a huge project. But the second Sui emperor, Yangdi, was a spendthrift. He raised huge armies for failed wars against Goguryeo, in Korea, and imposed forced labour. By 611, rebellions broke out.
[VISUAL: Grand Canal, Emperor Yang, rebellions]
Among the officials was Li Yuan, Duke of Tang, governor of the Taiyuan region, from a powerful northern family with Turkic connections. In 617, urged by his sons, especially Li Shimin, he rose in revolt, took Chang'an and put up a puppet emperor. In 618, after the murder of Yangdi, Li Yuan proclaimed the Tang dynasty, as Emperor Gaozu.
[VISUAL: Li Yuan's revolt, Chang'an]
Li Shimin, born in 598, was the dynasty's main general. He commanded the armies that defeated the main rivals between 618 and 624, including Xue Rengao, Wang Shichong and Dou Jiande. He recruited talented advisors and developed a reputation as a leader and a fighter.

## 3. THE BROTHERS (3:30–6:00)
**NARRATION**
The problem was succession. Li Yuan named his eldest son, Li Jiancheng, as crown prince. But Li Shimin had won the wars and had a loyal following. Li Jiancheng and the youngest brother Li Yuanji allied against him. Both sides had plots. According to the official histories, which were compiled after Taizong came to power, the brothers tried to poison Li Shimin at a banquet and bribed his generals.
[VISUAL: the three brothers, rivalry]
On 2 July 626 by the modern calendar, Li Shimin struck. He waited with his men at the Xuanwu Gate, the north gate of the palace, which the brothers had to pass on their way to the court. Li Shimin shot the crown prince with an arrow, and one of his generals killed Li Yuanji. The princes' sons were also executed.
Historians note a point of method. The records of this event were rewritten under Taizong, and he even insisted on viewing them later. Many scholars suspect the official story of an imminent plot was exaggerated to justify the coup. The truth, like most history written by the winners, is hard to see.
[VISUAL: the coup at the gate, a pen revising records]
Li Yuan accepted the situation. On 4 September 626, he abdicated, and Li Shimin became Emperor Taizong. He was 28.

## 4. THE GOVERNMENT OF ZHENGUAN (6:00–10:00)
**NARRATION**
Taizong's greatest quality was his willingness to listen. He gathered a group of strong ministers: Fang Xuanling, Du Ruhui, Wei Zheng, and Zhangsun Wuji, his brother-in-law.
[VISUAL: the ministers around Taizong]
Wei Zheng is the most famous. He had been an adviser to the dead crown prince, and had even advised killing Li Shimin. When Taizong asked him why he had tried to divide the brothers, Wei Zheng answered calmly that the crown prince had not listened to him; had he done so, he would not have died. Taizong, impressed by his frankness, made him a counsellor. Wei Zheng criticised the emperor so often and so sharply that Taizong sometimes went home furious, according to tradition, saying he would kill that country bumpkin.
When Wei Zheng died in 643, Taizong wept and said: with a bronze mirror, you can straighten your clothes; with history as a mirror, you can understand rise and fall; with a man as a mirror, you can see your own faults. I have lost one of my mirrors.
[VISUAL: Wei Zheng criticising, Taizong listening, bronze mirror]
The government was organised into three departments: one to draft decrees, one to review them, one to carry them out, with six ministries. The review department could return an order to the emperor, a check on power. The Tang Code, finished in 624 and revised in 637 and 653, is the oldest complete Chinese law code to survive. It was a model for Korea, Japan, and Vietnam.
[VISUAL: three departments diagram, Tang Code]
Taizong also reformed the exam system. He expanded the schools in the capital and, by recruiting men for their ability, reduced the grip of the aristocratic families. The examinations remained a small route to office under Taizong, but they grew.
He lowered taxes, and reduced punishments. He was careful about executions: he insisted on a review of all death sentences by five officials.
In 630 he allowed condemned prisoners to go home to be with their families and return to be executed. The story says all 390 returned, so he pardoned them. Most historians treat this as a legend.

## 5. THE HEAVENLY KHAN (10:00–13:00)
**NARRATION**
Taizong's foreign policy was just as bold. China's northern neighbours, the Eastern Turks, had threatened Chang'an in 626. Taizong, fresh to the throne, bought them off. But he waited.
[VISUAL: Turkish cavalry at the Wei river]
In 629 to 630 a harsh winter and internal quarrels weakened the Eastern Turks. Tang armies under Li Jing and Li Ji attacked and captured their ruler Illig Qaghan in 630. The Eastern Turkish state fell. The Turkish chiefs of the steppe offered Taizong the title of Heavenly Qaghan, Tian Kehan, in addition to Emperor. He accepted. It made him the ruler of both settled China and the steppe. He settled thousands of Turks inside the Chinese borders, and employed many in the Tang army.
[VISUAL: capture of the qaghan, the title Heavenly Qaghan]
In the next years, Tang armies conquered the oasis states of the Tarim basin, including Gaochang in 640 and Kucha in 648, and secured the route of the Silk Road. Chang'an grew to be the largest city in the world, with perhaps a million people, with merchants, monks and envoys from Persia, Central Asia, Japan, Korea and India.
[VISUAL: Silk Road oasis cities, Chang'an street scene]
Relations with Tibet were handled by marriage. In 641, Princess Wencheng married the Tibetan king Songtsen Gampo. She is credited with bringing Buddhism and Chinese culture to Tibet.
Taizong supported religious tolerance. The monk Xuanzang, who left China in 629 without permission to travel to India to study Buddhism, returned in 645 after sixteen years, with over 600 Buddhist texts. Taizong welcomed him, and asked him to write an account of his travels. The Journey to the West, the novel of the Monkey King, written 900 years later, is based on his journey. Taizong also welcomed the Nestorian Christian missionary Aluopen in 635.
[VISUAL: Xuanzang's return, Princess Wencheng, Nestorian priest]

## 6. THE FAILURE IN KOREA (13:00–14:30)
**NARRATION**
But Taizong was not infallible. In 645, he led an army of about 100,000 against Goguryeo, the northern Korean kingdom, which had defeated the Sui. His forces took a number of cities, but at Ansi, the garrison resisted for months. As winter came, Taizong had to retreat, with heavy losses. According to Korean sources, the Goguryeo commander at Ansi shot out Taizong's eye, but the Tang sources do not report this. He is said to have regretted the campaign and wished that Wei Zheng were alive to stop him.
[VISUAL: siege of Ansi, retreat in snow]

## 7. THE LAST YEARS (14:30–17:00)
**NARRATION**
Taizong's last years were clouded. He was troubled by the succession. His eldest son by Empress Zhangsun, Li Chengqian, the crown prince, was disabled, a bully, and reportedly plotted a coup in 643. Taizong deposed him. His younger brother Li Tai, a favourite, was also put aside. Taizong chose a weak, gentle son, Li Zhi, as heir. Empress Zhangsun, his devoted and wise wife, had died in 636. Her death left him bereaved.
[VISUAL: Empress Zhangsun, the sons in rivalry]
He also took Daoist elixirs. In 649, he died, aged fifty, at the Cuiwei palace. Some historians suggest that elixirs containing mercury and other poisons weakened him. He was buried at Zhaoling in a mausoleum, in the mountains. His son became Emperor Gaozong. Gaozong's wife, in the next generation, would change Chinese history. That is the subject of our next video.
[VISUAL: Zhaoling mausoleum, Li Zhi crowned]

## 8. LEGACY AND DEBATE (17:00–18:30)
**NARRATION**
The Zhenguan era was held up as the golden age of good government. Taizong's conversations with his ministers were collected in the Zhenguan Zhengyao, a book which was used for centuries as a guide to being a good ruler, even in Japan and Korea.
[VISUAL: the book, Japanese and Korean readers]
But historians are cautious. Our sources are the official histories, and Taizong himself influenced their writing. He demanded to see the daily records of his reign, against the tradition. He and his officials probably shaped his image: the wise ruler who accepted correction. His wars cost lives. His seizure of the throne remains controversial.
