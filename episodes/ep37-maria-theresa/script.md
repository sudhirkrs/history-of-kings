# Ep 37 — Maria Theresa: The Empress Who Held an Empire Together
Target: ~8 min (1092 words) · Status: DRAFT, facts checked by search first (see factcheck/), still needs a human check against books

## 1. HOOK (0:00–0:50)
**NARRATION**
On 20 October 1740, Emperor Charles VI died at the age of fifty-six. His heir was his twenty-three-year-old daughter, Maria Theresa, who had no training in government. Within two months, the young king of Prussia, Frederick II, invaded her richest province, Silesia, without a declaration of war. Other powers, which had promised to respect her inheritance, began to line up to carve up her lands.
[VISUAL: a young woman in mourning, armies converging on a map of the Habsburg lands]
She was expected to lose. Instead she ruled for forty years, lost Silesia but kept everything else, rebuilt her army and her finances, reformed her schools, and bore sixteen children. This is the story of Maria Theresa.
[VISUAL: Maria Theresa portrait, Schönbrunn]

## 2. THE INHERITANCE (0:50–3:00)
**NARRATION**
The Habsburgs ruled a vast, mixed collection of lands: Austria, Bohemia, Hungary, parts of Italy and the Netherlands, and more. Each had its own laws and nobles. Only the dynasty held them together.
[VISUAL: patchwork map of Habsburg lands]
Charles VI had no sons. In 1713, he issued a decree called the Pragmatic Sanction, which declared the Habsburg lands indivisible and allowed a daughter to inherit if there was no son. The Hungarian Diet accepted it in 1723. He then spent the rest of his reign persuading the European powers to agree, often giving up territory and favours in return. Many of them later broke their word.
Maria Theresa was born on 13 May 1717 in Vienna. She was the oldest surviving child. After her elder brother's death she became the heir. In 1736, she married Francis Stephen of Lorraine, whom she loved, unusually for a royal match. Yet her father kept her out of government. When he died, she knew little about the treasury, the army or foreign affairs.

## 3. THE WAR OF THE AUSTRIAN SUCCESSION (3:00–7:00)
**NARRATION**
On 16 December 1740, Frederick II of Prussia marched into Silesia. Maria Theresa sent an army, but it was beaten at Mollwitz in April 1741. France, Bavaria, Spain and Saxony joined the attack. At one point, the Bavarian Elector, backed by France, was elected Holy Roman Emperor, and it looked as though the Habsburg state would be dismantled.
[VISUAL: Frederick's invasion, Mollwitz, coalition against Austria]
Maria Theresa turned to Hungary. In 1741, she appeared before the Hungarian nobles in Pressburg, carrying her infant son Joseph, and appealed for help. According to tradition, the nobles drew their swords and swore to give their lives and blood for her. Historians say the scene has been embroidered, and that she also had to make concessions to the Hungarian nobility, but Hungarian troops did help turn the tide.
[VISUAL: the Hungarian Diet, with a label: later tradition]
The war went on until 1748. In the end, she lost Silesia, the richest province, to Prussia, and some lands in Italy, but kept the rest of the monarchy. Her husband Francis Stephen was elected Holy Roman Emperor in 1745. She was Empress by marriage, and ruler in her own right.
Treaty of Aix-la-Chapelle in 1748 ended the war. Maria Theresa never accepted the loss of Silesia.

## 4. REBUILDING THE STATE (7:00–10:30)
**NARRATION**
Maria Theresa learned a lesson from the war: her state was too weak. She spent the following years reforming it.
[VISUAL: Vienna, ministers at a table]
She reformed the army and its finances. She created a central administration in Vienna, taking power from the provincial nobles. She raised taxes on the nobility and clergy, who had been largely exempt. She brought in capable advisers, among them Count Haugwitz for the internal reforms, and later Prince Kaunitz, who ran foreign policy for decades.
Kaunitz overturned Austria's old alliances. In 1756, in what historians call the Diplomatic Revolution, Austria allied with its old enemy, France, against Prussia. The result was the Seven Years' War of 1756 to 1763. Maria Theresa hoped to win back Silesia. She did not. At the Treaty of Hubertusburg in 1763, Silesia stayed with Prussia.
[VISUAL: Kaunitz, Austro-French alliance, Seven Years' War]
She also reformed education. Her General School Ordinance of 1774, drafted by the Silesian Catholic priest Johann Ignaz von Felbiger, required boys and girls from six to twelve to attend school or receive private instruction. It was unpopular with poor families, who needed their children to work, and enforcing it took pressure. But it was a step toward public education in Central Europe.
[VISUAL: schoolroom, Felbiger]
In the countryside, she tried to limit the labour that landlords could demand from serfs. A series of decrees in the 1770s, called the Robot patents, limited forced labour in some provinces, though the Hungarian nobility resisted strongly.

## 5. A MOTHER OF SIXTEEN (10:30–12:30)
**NARRATION**
Maria Theresa and Francis Stephen had sixteen children. She used them as tools of policy, arranging marriages across Europe to build alliances. The best known was her youngest daughter, Marie Antoinette, who married the future Louis XVI of France. The proxy wedding took place in Vienna on 19 April 1770, when the bride was fourteen.
[VISUAL: the family, Marie Antoinette's proxy marriage]
Her eldest son, Joseph, became co-regent after the death of his father in 1765, though she kept the final say on most matters. Mother and son clashed often. Joseph was impatient and wanted rapid reform, while Maria Theresa preferred compromise and caution, and was more religious.
[VISUAL: Joseph II and Maria Theresa, a study in contrast]

## 6. THE DARKER SIDE (12:30–14:30)
**NARRATION**
Maria Theresa was a devout Catholic, and her record on religion was harsh. In December 1744, she ordered the Jews to leave Prague, and in 1745 the rest of Bohemia. About ten thousand people in Prague were affected. The order was rolled back in stages under pressure from foreign powers and her own local officials, and was cancelled in 1748, but with a heavy annual tax for the right to stay. Historians see this as an episode driven by prejudice as well as politics.
[VISUAL: expulsion from Prague, tax]
She also persecuted Protestants in some regions, sending them to remote parts. It was her son Joseph who issued the Edict of Toleration in 1781, after her death.
[VISUAL: Joseph's Edict of Toleration, contrast]

## 7. THE FIRST PARTITION OF POLAND (14:30–15:30)
**NARRATION**
In 1772, Russia, Prussia and Austria agreed to the first partition of Poland. Maria Theresa had qualms. She wrote that she did not like to take what did not belong to her. But Austria took a large region, Galicia, as her share. A quotation often attributed to Frederick the Great says: she wept, but she took. The saying is a witty summary, though its source is uncertain.
[VISUAL: the first partition map]

## 8. DEATH AND LEGACY (15:30–17:30)
**NARRATION**
Maria Theresa died on 29 November 1780, aged 63, in Vienna. She had ruled for forty years, a woman in a world of men, and she had saved her inheritance. She left a more centralised, more efficient state, with a stronger army and schools.
[VISUAL: her tomb in the Capuchin Crypt, Vienna]
Historians still discuss several questions. Was she an enlightened ruler or a cautious conservative? How much credit belongs to her advisers? How should one weigh her reforms against her intolerance? And what would have happened if she had held on to Silesia?
