# Ep 24 — Alfred the Great: The King Who Saved England
Target: ~9 min (1344 words) · Status: DRAFT, fact-checked by web search (see factcheck/); still needs a human check against the cited books

## 1. HOOK (0:00–0:50)
**NARRATION**
In January 878, in the dead of winter, a Viking army suddenly attacked the royal town of Chippenham in Wessex, the last strong English kingdom. The king, Alfred, escaped with a small band of followers and vanished into the marshes of Somerset. England seemed lost. Four months later, he reappeared with an army, and won a battle that saved his kingdom.
[VISUAL: night attack, a king fleeing through marshes]
He is the only English monarch ever called the Great. He did not rule all England, and his reputation rests on three things: he survived, he built a state that could stand against the Vikings, and he cared about learning. This is the story of Alfred.
[VISUAL: Alfred statue at Winchester, map of Wessex]

## 2. ENGLAND UNDER ATTACK (0:50–3:00)
**NARRATION**
In the ninth century, England was divided into four kingdoms: Northumbria in the north, East Anglia and Mercia in the middle, and Wessex in the south-west. The people were Anglo-Saxons, descended from settlers who came from northern Germany and Denmark after the Romans left. They had been Christian for about two centuries.
[VISUAL: map of four kingdoms]
In 793, Vikings, pagan raiders from Scandinavia, attacked the monastery of Lindisfarne, on an island off Northumbria. For decades they came as raiders in summer. Then, in 865, a different force landed: the Great Heathen Army, led by sons of Ragnar Lodbrok, according to the Anglo-Saxon Chronicle. They came to conquer, and stay.
In a few years, they took Northumbria, killed the king of East Anglia, Edmund, and shattered Mercia. By 870, only Wessex was left.
[VISUAL: longships, Lindisfarne, the Great Heathen Army]

## 3. THE YOUNGEST SON (3:00–5:00)
**NARRATION**
Alfred was born around 849, at Wantage, in Berkshire, the youngest son of King Aethelwulf of Wessex. As the fifth son, he was not expected to be king. His mother Osburh, according to his biographer Asser, once offered a book of English poems to whichever of her sons could learn it first. Alfred, who could not yet read, took the book to a teacher and learned it, and won.
[VISUAL: Osburh holding a book, young Alfred]
At four, he was sent to Rome, and the Pope, according to the Chronicle, anointed him as king. At six or seven, he travelled to Rome again with his father. The journeys gave him a lasting sense of belonging to a wider Christian world.
His three oldest brothers reigned in turn: Aethelbald, Aethelberht and Aethelred. Alfred served Aethelred as a deputy and military commander. In January 871 at Ashdown, the brothers fought the Danes, and Alfred led a charge uphill that was decisive, according to Asser. That year saw nine battles. In April, Aethelred died. Alfred, aged about twenty-one, became king.
[VISUAL: Ashdown, charge uphill, Aethelred's death]

## 4. THE LOWEST POINT (5:00–8:00)
**NARRATION**
For the next seven years Alfred bought peace, paying the Danes to leave. Then in January 878 the Danes, under their leader Guthrum, struck at Chippenham during Christmas. Wessex was overrun. Many nobles fled or submitted. The Chronicle says that Alfred with a small force made his way through woods and fens.
[VISUAL: Danes at Chippenham, Alfred in the woods]
He hid on the island of Athelney in the marshes of Somerset, and built a small fort. From there he waged a guerrilla war. A famous story tells that Alfred, sheltering in a peasant woman's house, was asked to watch cakes cooking by the fire. Absorbed in his worries, he let them burn, and was scolded. The story first appears in the Life of St Neot, written in the late tenth century, some ninety years after his death. It is probably a legend. It does show how people remembered him: a king who stooped to learn from the humble.
[VISUAL: Athelney, burnt cakes with a note: later legend]
In May 878, he called the men of Somerset, Wiltshire and Hampshire to meet at a place called Egbert's Stone. Asser says they were delighted to see him alive. A few days later, they marched on Guthrum's army at Edington, also called Ethandun. After a hard fight, the Danes were beaten, and fled to their fort. After two weeks of siege, they surrendered.
[VISUAL: gathering at Egbert's Stone, the battle of Edington]

## 5. THE PEACE WITH GUTHRUM (8:00–9:30)
**NARRATION**
Alfred did not kill Guthrum. Instead, he made him a deal. Guthrum agreed to be baptised, with Alfred as godfather and with the Christian name Athelstan, and to leave Wessex. Later, a treaty between Alfred and Guthrum, dated sometime between the late 870s and about 890, set a boundary running roughly along the old Roman road of Watling Street, and the River Lea. East and north of the line was Danish law, the Danelaw. West and south was English.
[VISUAL: baptism of Guthrum, boundary map: Danelaw]
This was a compromise. Alfred could not drive the Danes out, but he stopped them going further. Historians see the treaty as a founding document for the later kingdom of England.

## 6. BUILDING A KINGDOM (9:30–13:00)
**NARRATION**
Alfred spent the next years preparing for the next invasion. He made three big changes.
First, the burhs. He built or repaired about thirty fortified towns across Wessex, so that no one was more than about 20 miles, a day's march, from a fort. A document called the Burghal Hidage lists them, with the number of men needed to defend each. A burh needed four men per pole, a length of about five metres, of wall. Towns like Winchester, Wallingford, Oxford and Wareham started as burhs. Some, like Wallingford, still show the street plan.
[VISUAL: burh network map, Wallingford plan]
Second, the army. He reorganised the fyrd, the part-time levy, into two halves: one half in the field, one at home farming, taking turns. This allowed a standing force. He also built ships, longer than the Viking ones, in 896, to meet raiders at sea. Historians doubt that they were very effective, but they are sometimes seen as the beginning of the English navy.
[VISUAL: fyrd in two groups, Alfred's ships]
Third, the law. He issued a law code which drew on earlier Anglo-Saxon laws, and which opened with the Ten Commandments and parts of the Bible. Alfred said he collected the laws he thought best, and rejected others. He insisted on the king's duty to give justice, and on the value of oaths.
[VISUAL: law code manuscript]
In 886, he took back London from the Danes, and put it under a governor. The Chronicle says that after that, all the English people who were not under Danish rule submitted to him.

## 7. THE SCHOLAR KING (13:00–15:30)
**NARRATION**
Alfred saw the Viking attacks as a punishment for English decline. The monasteries had been destroyed, and learning had collapsed. He wrote that when he became king, there were few south of the Humber who could understand their service books in English, or translate a letter from Latin. 
[VISUAL: ruined monastery, empty library]
So he invited scholars to his court: Asser, a Welsh monk, Plegmund of Mercia, Grimbold from France, and John the Old Saxon. And he started a programme of translations from Latin into English. Alfred is credited with translating, or arranging the translation of, Gregory the Great's Pastoral Care, Boethius's Consolation of Philosophy, Orosius's History against the Pagans, and parts of the Psalms. In the preface to Pastoral Care, he said that all free-born young men with the means should learn to read English.
[VISUAL: scholars at court, Boethius, Pastoral Care]
He also supported the Anglo-Saxon Chronicle, a year-by-year history of England, which was begun in his time and continued for 250 years. It is our best source for the period.
Asser wrote a Life of King Alfred in 893. Some historians have questioned whether it is genuine, but most accept it as authentic.

## 8. THE LAST WAR AND DEATH (15:30–17:00)
**NARRATION**
In 892, the Danes attacked again, with a large force from the Continent, in 250 ships. For four years Alfred fought them, using the burhs and the new army. By 896 they dispersed, and Wessex was secure.
[VISUAL: Danish invasion fleet, burhs holding out]
Alfred died on 26 October 899, aged about fifty, and was buried at Winchester. His son Edward the Elder, and his daughter Aethelflaed, the Lady of the Mercians, would reconquer the Danelaw. By 927, his grandson Aethelstan was king of all England.
[VISUAL: Alfred's death, Aethelflaed, Aethelstan]

## 9. LEGACY AND DEBATE (17:00–18:30)
**NARRATION**
Alfred's reputation grew over time. In the sixteenth century, he was praised as the founder of English law and navy. In the nineteenth, as a model of Victorian virtue. Statues were put up in Winchester and Wantage.
[VISUAL: Winchester statue, Victorian celebrations 1901]
Modern historians say that he did not create England; he made it possible. Some, like Richard Abels, stress his skill in organisation. Others point out that much of his image comes from Asser and the Chronicle, which were written to praise the Wessex dynasty.
