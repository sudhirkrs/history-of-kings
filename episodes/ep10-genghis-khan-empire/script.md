# Ep 10 — Genghis Khan, Part 2: The Conqueror of Asia

## 1. HOOK (0:00–0:50)
**NARRATION**
In 1218, a caravan of Muslim merchants, sent by Genghis Khan, arrived at the border city of Otrar, in what is now Kazakhstan. The governor said they were spies and killed them. Genghis sent ambassadors to demand justice. One was killed, and the others had their beards shaved off. It was a catastrophic mistake.
[VISUAL: caravan at Otrar gates, envoys humiliated]
Within three years, the great cities of Central Asia, Bukhara, Samarkand, Merv, Nishapur and Herat, would be conquered, and the empire of Khwarezm, one of the richest in the Islamic world, would no longer exist.
[VISUAL: map of cities falling]
In this video, we follow the second half of Genghis Khan's life: the campaign against Khwarezm, the riders who crossed the Caucasus, the way he ruled and the mystery of his death.

## 2. KHWARAZM AND THE INSULT (0:50–3:20)
**NARRATION**
Khwarezm was ruled by Shah Muhammad II. His lands reached from the Aral Sea to the Persian Gulf, including Persia, Afghanistan and Transoxiana. It was a large, rich empire, but it was divided. The Shah's mother, Terken Khatun, ran a rival court, and the army was a mix of Turkic tribes with little loyalty.
[VISUAL: map of Khwarezm, Shah Muhammad II, Terken Khatun]
Genghis wanted trade. He had just conquered northern China and the Silk Road was his to protect. He sent a caravan of some hundreds of merchants, with gold and silver, to Khwarezm in 1218.
Inalchuq, the governor of Otrar, seized the caravan. The Persian historian Juvaini says Inalchuq was greedy and thought the merchants were spies. Almost all of them were killed. The Mongol sources say there were 450 men, though the number is uncertain.
[VISUAL: Inalchuq, trading goods seized]
Genghis sent three envoys to ask the Shah to hand over the governor. The Shah had one of the envoys executed. This broke the rules of diplomacy that the Mongols took very seriously. War was now certain.

## 3. THE INVASION (3:20–7:30)
**NARRATION**
In 1219, Genghis led an army, probably between 90,000 and 150,000 men, out of Mongolia. He left his general Muqali to continue the war in China. He took four sons with him, and a number of engineers from China.
[VISUAL: army crossing the Altai, engineers with siege machines]
The Mongols divided into columns. Jochi and Chagatai besieged Otrar. Genghis himself, with his youngest son Tolui, struck at Bukhara. The Shah, instead of fighting a decisive battle, scattered his forces in garrisons. This was a fatal mistake, because the Mongols could take the garrisons one at a time.
[VISUAL: Mongol columns on a map, scattered garrisons]
Bukhara fell in February 1220 after three days. The city was burned. Genghis rode into the main mosque and preached to the people that he was the punishment of God. Samarkand fell in March 1220, despite a garrison of tens of thousands.
The Shah fled west, and died on an island in the Caspian Sea in early 1221, a broken man. Two generals, Subutai and Jebe, were sent with 20,000 men to chase him. They ended up on an astonishing expedition, around the Caspian, through the Caucasus, into the Russian steppe.
[VISUAL: Subutai and Jebe's route around the Caspian]
At the River Kalka in 1223, they defeated a combined army of Russian princes and Cumans, killing many of them. They then turned back, having made the first reconnaissance of Europe. We will return to this story when we reach Russia.

## 4. THE FALL OF CITIES (7:30–10:30)
**NARRATION**
Meanwhile Genghis took the rest of the cities.
In 1221, Merv, Nishapur and Herat were all taken. These were among the great cities of the Islamic world. The sources describe horrifying slaughters. The Persian historians say that 1.3 million people died at Merv, and 1.7 million at Nishapur. Modern historians consider these figures impossible. Merv's population might have been a few hundred thousand at best.
[VISUAL: ruined cities, numbers crossed out with a note, medieval exaggeration]
Still, the destruction was real and huge. Cities that resisted were destroyed, and the people killed or enslaved, while cities that surrendered were often spared. The Mongols used terror deliberately. It made later cities more willing to surrender.
There was lasting damage. The irrigation systems of Khorasan, built over centuries, were wrecked. Historians debate how far Persia recovered. Some say it took centuries; others say the damage has been exaggerated.
[VISUAL: damaged canal system, farmland]
The last Khwarezmian prince, Jalal ad-Din Mingburnu, defeated a Mongol force at Parwan, but in November 1221 was beaten by Genghis at the Indus. He swam his horse across the river and escaped into India. Genghis let him go, and turned back, not entering India. One reason, tradition says, was the heat, and the omens.
[VISUAL: Jalal ad-Din leaping into the Indus]

## 5. THE WISE MAN AND THE RELIGIOUS POLICY (10:30–12:30)
**NARRATION**
In these years, Genghis showed another side. He was curious about religion and life. He heard of a Daoist master in China, Qiu Chuji, also known as Changchun, who was said to know the secret of long life. In 1219, he invited him. The old monk, then in his seventies, made a journey of three years to meet him, across Mongolia and Central Asia. His disciple wrote the Journey to the West of Changchun, which is a valuable eyewitness report.
[VISUAL: Daoist master travelling, the meeting at the Hindu Kush]
They met in 1222. Genghis asked about immortality. The master reportedly answered that there are ways to protect life, but no medicine for immortality. Genghis was impressed by his honesty and gave him tax exemptions for his religion.
This fits a broader pattern. The Mongols were tolerant in religion. Clergy of all faiths, Buddhist, Daoist, Muslim and Christian, were often exempt from taxes. This was partly policy. Genghis wanted the support of religious leaders, and the prayers of everyone for him.
[VISUAL: monks, imams, priests in a row]

## 6. HOW THE MONGOL EMPIRE WORKED (12:30–15:00)
**NARRATION**
Genghis returned to Mongolia in 1225 and organised his empire.
He built the yam, a relay system of stations with horses and riders, across the empire. Messengers could travel hundreds of kilometres in a few days. This tied together a huge area and became one of the empire's main strengths.
[VISUAL: relay stations, riders]
He protected trade. Merchants, including Muslims, Chinese and Uyghurs, were given privileges. Roads were made safe. This is the origin of what historians later called the Pax Mongolica, the period when travellers like Marco Polo could cross Asia.
He employed foreigners. His main administrators included Uyghur scribes, Khitan advisors like Yelu Chucai, and Muslim merchants. He valued ability over origin.
[VISUAL: Yelu Chucai, Uyghur clerks, merchants]
The army relied on speed. Each soldier had several horses, rode fast and could cover 100 km a day. They used composite bows with a range of well over 150 metres. They practised hunting drives in which a large circle of riders closed in on game, training coordination. They also collected intelligence with scouts and spies before attacking.
[VISUAL: composite bow, hunting circle, scouts]

## 7. THE LAST CAMPAIGN AND DEATH (15:00–17:30)
**NARRATION**
The Tangut kingdom of Western Xia had refused to send troops to the Khwarezm campaign. In 1226, Genghis marched against it, and in 1227, as the siege of the Tangut capital continued, he died. It was in August, aged about sixty-five.
[VISUAL: siege of the Tangut capital, the khan's tent]
How did he die? The sources disagree. One says he fell from a horse during a hunt. Another says he died from illness, perhaps typhus or a fever. Marco Polo, writing much later, says he was shot in the knee by an arrow. Another legend says the Tangut queen injured him. We cannot be sure.
[VISUAL: four theories on cards]
His body was taken back to Mongolia and buried in a secret place, probably on or near the mountain of Burkhan Khaldun. Legends say that those who carried him were killed, and that a river was diverted over the grave. No one has found the tomb, and Mongolians today largely discourage its search.
[VISUAL: Burkhan Khaldun mountain]
Before his death, he named his third son Ogedei as his successor. His eldest son Jochi had died, perhaps in 1225. Chagatai and Tolui received their own territories.

## 8. LEGACY AND DEBATE (17:30–19:30)
**NARRATION**
What did Genghis Khan leave? The Mongol Empire, which his sons and grandsons expanded to cover Eurasia from Korea to Hungary. Estimates suggest that it became the largest contiguous empire in history, around 24 million square kilometres at its peak.
[VISUAL: map of the empire at its greatest]
In Mongolia, he is a national hero, shown on money, on vodka and on statues. In many other countries, he is remembered as a destroyer. Both views are part of history.
Historians debate the death toll of the Mongol conquests. Some estimates run into tens of millions, but these are highly uncertain. Another debate concerns his genetic legacy. A 2003 study suggested that about 16 million men carry a Y chromosome that may come from him or his close relatives. Later research is more cautious.
[VISUAL: skulls vs trade routes, scales, DNA tree labelled debated]
