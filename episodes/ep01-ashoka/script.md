# Ep 1 — Ashoka: The King Who Gave Up War

## 1. HOOK (0:00–0:40)
**NARRATION**
Around 261 BCE, on India's east coast, one of the bloodiest battles of the ancient world was fought. The winning king stood on the battlefield and saw thousands of dead. And then he did something almost no conqueror in history has done. He wrote down, in stone, that he was sorry.
[VISUAL: dark battlefield illustration → close-up of a rock edict]
This is Ashoka, the third emperor of the Maurya dynasty. In this video, we will see how he rose to rule most of the Indian subcontinent, what really happened at Kalinga, and why a king who started as a conqueror is remembered for peace.
[VISUAL: title card "ASHOKA — The King Who Gave Up War"]
And we will also separate what we *know* from inscriptions, from what later legends *claim*. Because with Ashoka, that difference matters.

## 2. CONTEXT (0:40–3:00)
**NARRATION**
To understand Ashoka, we first go back about sixty years before him.
[VISUAL: map of north India c. 325 BCE; Nanda kingdom of Magadha]
Around 320 BCE, a young man called Chandragupta overthrew the Nanda dynasty in Magadha, a kingdom in what is now Bihar. Tradition says he was helped by a teacher and strategist, Chanakya, also known as Kautilya. Chandragupta built the Maurya Empire, with its capital at Pataliputra, today's Patna.
[VISUAL: Pataliputra reconstruction; Chandragupta coin/relief]
He also stopped the Greek successors of Alexander in the north-west. Around 305 BCE he made a treaty with Seleucus, and the Greek ambassador Megasthenes came to his court. Megasthenes's account of Pataliputra survives only in fragments quoted by later writers, but it describes a large, well-run city.
Chandragupta's son was Bindusara. He expanded the empire further south. And Bindusara's son was Ashoka.
[VISUAL: family tree: Chandragupta → Bindusara → Ashoka]
So Ashoka was born into an empire. He did not build it from nothing. Remember that, because it changes how we read his story.

## 3. RISE (3:00–8:00)
**NARRATION**
We do not know Ashoka's birth date exactly. Historians usually place his reign between about 268 and 232 BCE.
[VISUAL: timeline 268–232 BCE]
Before he became king, he served as a governor. Buddhist texts say he was sent to Taxila to put down a rebellion, and later governed Ujjain. These stories come from sources written centuries later, but they fit what we know: a prince trained in administration and war.
[VISUAL: map with Taxila, Ujjain, Pataliputra]
Now comes the first big problem. Many popular stories say Ashoka killed ninety-nine of his brothers to seize the throne. The Buddhist texts, such as the Ashokavadana and the Sri Lankan chronicles, tell a violent succession story with a gap of about four years between Bindusara's death and Ashoka's coronation.
[VISUAL: manuscript page; text overlay "Later Buddhist texts"]
But here is the key point. Ashoka's own inscriptions do not say this. In fact, they mention his brothers and sisters as living family members, which suggests the "ninety-nine brothers" is a later, exaggerated tale.
[VISUAL: edict text highlight]
So historians say: there was probably a succession struggle, but the dramatic details are legend. We should hold them lightly.
Once king, tradition calls him "Chandashoka", Ashoka the Fierce. That label too comes from later texts, written to contrast his early and later life.
What we can say safely is this: he inherited a huge empire, he ruled from Pataliputra, and he ruled in a time of strong army, strong taxes, and a large spy and officer network. [SOURCE: Arthashastra context — verify dating]
[VISUAL: administration diagram: king → provincial princes → officers]
And then, about eight years into his reign, he turned to the one region of the east coast that still stood outside Maurya control.
That region was Kalinga.

## 4. PEAK — KALINGA & THE CHANGE (8:00–14:00)
**NARRATION**
Kalinga was roughly today's Odisha and nearby coast. It was rich, it had ports and trade, and it controlled routes between north India and the south.
[VISUAL: map of Kalinga, trade routes, ports]
Around 261 BCE, Ashoka invaded. We have no detailed account of the fighting. What we have is Ashoka's own words, from his Rock Edict 13.
[VISUAL: Rock Edict 13 at Dhauli / Girnar / Kalsi]
He says that a hundred and fifty thousand people were deported, a hundred thousand were killed, and many times that number died afterwards. These are the numbers he gives himself. Historians read them as royal statements, not exact counts, but the scale is clear.
Then comes the most remarkable part. Ashoka says that after conquering Kalinga, he felt deep remorse. He calls it a great sorrow and regret.
[VISUAL: slow zoom on inscription; text "remorse after conquest — Rock Edict 13"]
This is rare. Kings of the ancient world boasted of their victories. Ashoka confessed the cost.
So what did he do next?
He turned to what he calls *dhamma*, in Sanskrit *dharma*. Not a religion with temples and rituals, but a code of conduct: be kind to servants, respect elders, tell the truth, be generous, do not harm living beings, and respect all religious sects.
[VISUAL: bullet icons for each principle]
He wrote these ideas on rocks and polished stone pillars across the empire, in local scripts such as Brahmi, and in the north-west also in Greek and Aramaic.
[VISUAL: map with edict sites, Kandahar Greek inscription]
Ashoka also describes concrete actions in his edicts:
He appointed special officers called *dhamma-mahamattas* to look after the welfare of people.
He built roads, planted shade trees, dug wells, and set up rest houses.
He provided medical care for people and animals.
He limited animal slaughter, especially in the royal kitchen.
[VISUAL: pillar, road, banyan trees, well icons]
Did he become a Buddhist? Yes. In some edicts he says he became a lay follower of the Buddha. He also visited Buddhist sites such as Lumbini, where a pillar still records that the Buddha was born there.
[VISUAL: Lumbini pillar]
But he did not force Buddhism on anyone. Rock Edict 12 explicitly calls for respect for all sects and warns against praising your own faith while attacking others.
[VISUAL: highlighted text; "respect all sects"]
The story goes that Ashoka also sent missions abroad. His edicts name Greek kings, such as Antiochus II of Syria and Ptolemy II of Egypt, as places where his dhamma was promoted. Sri Lankan chronicles say he sent his son Mahinda and daughter Sanghamitta to Sri Lanka, where Buddhism took root.
[VISUAL: map of missions: Greek kingdoms, Sri Lanka]
Notice again the difference between sources. The Greek kings are named in his edicts. The Mahinda mission comes from Sri Lankan chronicles. Both can be right, but they are different kinds of evidence.

## 5. A POWERFUL, NOT PERFECT, KING (11:30–14:00)
**NARRATION**
Was Ashoka a pacifist? Not exactly.
[VISUAL: pause on question]
He did not disband his army. In Rock Edict 13 he warns the forest tribes that he has the power to punish them if they do wrong. He also kept the death penalty; one of his pillar edicts gives condemned prisoners three extra days before execution.
So he did not abolish power; he tried to rule power with moral purpose.
That balance is what makes him interesting to historians. He was an emperor with a huge administration, and he used it to spread a moral message in a way no earlier Indian ruler had.

## 6. FALL (14:00–17:00)
**NARRATION**
Ashoka ruled for about thirty-six years and died around 232 BCE.
[VISUAL: timeline end]
His empire did not last long after him. Within about fifty years, the Maurya dynasty ended. Around 185 BCE, the last Maurya king, Brihadratha, was killed by his general Pushyamitra Shunga.
[VISUAL: map shrinking; Shunga dynasty]
Why did it fall? Historians suggest several reasons: a very centralised system that needed a strong king, a weak succession, financial strain, and pressure from the north-west. No single reason explains it, and scholars still discuss this.
A common claim is that Ashoka's peace policy weakened the army and caused the fall. Most modern historians treat this as too simple. The empire held together for decades after his reign, and many factors were involved.

## 7. LEGACY (17:00–19:00)
**NARRATION**
After the Mauryas, Ashoka was almost forgotten in India. His inscriptions were written in an old script no one could read.
[VISUAL: weathered pillar]
In 1837, the British scholar James Prinsep deciphered Brahmi, and the edicts could be read again. Ashoka was rediscovered, and the king called "Devanampiya Piyadasi", "Beloved of the Gods, of Gracious Look", was finally identified with Ashoka.
[VISUAL: Prinsep portrait, Brahmi chart]
Today the Lion Capital of Ashoka from Sarnath is India's national emblem, and the Ashoka Chakra, the 24-spoked wheel, sits on the national flag.
[VISUAL: Sarnath capital; Indian flag]
Ashoka's message across 2,300 years is simple: power is measured by what you do with it.

## 8. DEBATE + CTA (19:00–20:00)
**NARRATION**
Historians still discuss several things: how violent his rise really was, how much of Buddhism spread because of him versus later monks and merchants, and why his empire fell.
