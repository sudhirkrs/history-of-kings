# Ep 1 — Ashoka: The King Who Gave Up War
Target: ~14 min (2048 words) · Status: DRAFT, fact-checked by web search (see factcheck/); still needs a human check against the cited books

## 1. HOOK (0:00–0:50)
**NARRATION**
Around 261 BCE, on the east coast of India, one of the bloodiest wars of the ancient world came to an end. The winning king rode across the battlefield and saw the dead lying everywhere. And then he did something almost no conqueror in history has done. He had his regret carved into rock, for everyone to read.
[VISUAL: dark battlefield illustration, then a close-up of a rock edict]
This is Ashoka, the third emperor of the Maurya dynasty. He ruled most of the Indian subcontinent. He is remembered not as the king who won the most wars, but as the king who stopped fighting them.
[VISUAL: title card, Ashoka, the King Who Gave Up War]
In this video, we will follow his whole life. His family, his rise, the war in Kalinga, the change that followed, and the empire he left behind. And we will keep one rule: we will always say whether a fact comes from Ashoka's own inscriptions, or from stories written centuries after him. Because with Ashoka, that difference is everything.
[VISUAL: split screen, inscription on left, manuscript on right]

## 2. THE EMPIRE HE INHERITED (0:50–3:40)
**NARRATION**
To understand Ashoka, we first need to meet his family. Ashoka did not build his empire. He inherited it.
[VISUAL: family tree, Chandragupta, Bindusara, Ashoka]
Around 321 BCE, his grandfather Chandragupta Maurya overthrew the Nanda dynasty in Magadha, a rich kingdom in the Ganges plain, in today's Bihar. From there, Chandragupta built the first empire to cover most of northern India. His capital was Pataliputra, which is modern Patna.
[VISUAL: map of the Maurya core around 300 BCE with Pataliputra marked]
Chandragupta also defeated the Greek successors of Alexander the Great in the north-west. Around 305 BCE he made a treaty with the Greek ruler Seleucus. The Greeks sent an ambassador named Megasthenes to Pataliputra. His book is lost, but later writers quoted parts of it, and it describes a large, busy, well-guarded city.
[VISUAL: Pataliputra reconstruction, Megasthenes quote card]
After Chandragupta came his son Bindusara, who ruled for about twenty-five years and pushed the empire south, deep into the Deccan plateau. Greek writers record that he exchanged letters with the Greek king Antiochus, and asked him for sweet wine, dried figs and a philosopher. The king sent the wine and figs, but politely said that Greek law did not allow the sale of a philosopher.
[VISUAL: letter illustration, figs and wine]
So when Ashoka was a young prince, the Maurya Empire was already a giant machine. It had a capital with an army of officials, a network of provinces, taxes, roads and spies. What it did not have was a clear rule about who should come next. That would matter.

## 3. THE YOUNG PRINCE (3:40–6:10)
**NARRATION**
We do not know the exact year of Ashoka's birth. Most historians place his reign from about 268 to 232 BCE, so he was probably born around 304 BCE.
[VISUAL: timeline with 304 BCE, 268 BCE, 232 BCE]
His mother, according to Buddhist texts, was a woman named Dharma, also known as Subhadrangi. The Sri Lankan chronicles say Ashoka had a number of half-brothers, and one full brother, Vitashoka. But we should be careful: these texts were written hundreds of years after Ashoka lived.
[VISUAL: manuscript pages labelled Mahavamsa and Ashokavadana]
Before he was king, Ashoka served as a governor. The tradition says that his father Bindusara sent him to Taxila, in the north-west, to deal with a rebellion. Later he governed Ujjain, a major city in central India. There he is said to have married a merchant's daughter named Devi, whose children, Mahinda and Sanghamitta, appear later in our story.
[VISUAL: map showing Taxila, Ujjain, Pataliputra]
This fits what we would expect. A Maurya prince was trained in administration and war, and sent far from the capital to prove himself.
But there is a mystery here. Taxila, Ujjain, the rebellion, the merchant's daughter: all of this comes from Buddhist tradition. Ashoka himself never mentions any of it. So we can say that he probably served as a governor, and that he was probably a capable one. The details are the stories that later writers told.

## 4. THE STRUGGLE FOR THE THRONE (6:10–8:30)
**NARRATION**
Now we come to the story that most popular videos tell as fact: that Ashoka killed ninety-nine of his brothers to seize the throne.
[VISUAL: dark throne room, ninety-nine candles]
Let us look at the evidence. The Ashokavadana, a Buddhist text written several centuries later, says that when Bindusara was dying, Ashoka was sent away, and the crown prince, Susima, was given the throne. Ashoka returned, fought the supporters of his brother, and won. Sri Lankan chronicles say that he killed many of his brothers, and that he was crowned four years after his father's death.
[VISUAL: text overlay, Later Buddhist texts, 300 to 600 years after Ashoka]
Notice something. These Buddhist authors loved to tell this story. A cruel young Ashoka, later changed by the Buddha's teaching, makes a dramatic lesson. They even gave him a nickname: Chandashoka, Ashoka the Fierce.
But now look at Ashoka's own words. In one of his edicts, he mentions the welfare of his brothers and sisters, and of their families. If he had killed ninety-nine brothers, this would be a very odd thing to write.
[VISUAL: edict text highlight, brothers and sisters]
So what do historians conclude? Most agree there was probably a struggle for the throne, and the four-year gap before his coronation points to a dispute. But the exact numbers and the details are probably exaggerated. When you tell this story, the honest way is to say: there was probably a fight, and later writers made it darker.

## 5. THE MAURYA MACHINE (8:30–10:40)
**NARRATION**
Around 268 BCE, Ashoka became the ruler of this vast machine. How did it work?
[VISUAL: diagram, king, ministers, provinces, districts, villages]
At the top was the king. Under him, the empire was divided into provinces, often governed by royal princes. The provinces were divided into districts, run by officers who collected taxes, kept order and reported back. Pataliputra itself was managed by a board of officials, as Megasthenes described.
The state ran on taxes: mostly a share of the farmers' crops, plus taxes on trade and on craftsmen. It also owned mines, forests and workshops. The army was large, though ancient numbers like six hundred thousand foot soldiers are probably exaggerated.
[VISUAL: coins, punch-marked silver, grain being measured]
Information was power. Royal officers called spies, or informers, travelled the land and reported on officials and the people. The famous book on statecraft, the Arthashastra, describes such a system in detail. Historians debate when the Arthashastra was written and how well it matches the real Maurya state. But the Greek writers confirm the main picture: a strong, watchful government.
[VISUAL: manuscript of Arthashastra labelled authorship and date debated]
This was the empire Ashoka ruled. And in his eighth year as king, he decided to finish a task that his father and grandfather had left unfinished.

## 6. THE WAR IN KALINGA (10:40–13:10)
**NARRATION**
That task was Kalinga. It lay on the east coast, covering roughly modern Odisha and parts of the neighbouring coast.
[VISUAL: map of Kalinga, coastline, rivers, forests]
Kalinga was valuable. It had ports for trade across the Bay of Bengal. It had forests full of elephants. And it stood between the Maurya heartland and the south. Earlier rulers, the Nandas, had apparently held it, but by Ashoka's time it was independent.
Around 261 BCE, Ashoka invaded.
[VISUAL: armies moving, elephants, fortified towns]
We have no battle-by-battle account. What we have is something far better and far stranger: Ashoka's own description, carved in Rock Edict 13. In it, he says the conquest of Kalinga brought deep regret. He states that a hundred and fifty thousand people were carried off as prisoners, a hundred thousand were killed, and many times that number died afterwards.
[VISUAL: Rock Edict 13 at Girnar, numbers on screen, 150,000 deported, 100,000 killed]
Historians read these numbers carefully. They are royal figures, not census results. They may be rounded or exaggerated. But think about what he is doing. A king is publicly admitting the human cost of his own victory.
And then the edict says something that no other ancient emperor ever said. He writes that the Beloved of the Gods felt sorrow and remorse after conquering Kalinga, because during the conquest of an unconquered land, killing, death and carrying away of people take place.
[VISUAL: slow zoom, remorse after conquest, quoted from Rock Edict 13]
This is the turning point of Ashoka's life.

## 7. A NEW KIND OF KING: DHAMMA (13:10–16:30)
**NARRATION**
What did Ashoka do next? He turned to a idea he calls dhamma. That is the Pali form of the Sanskrit word dharma.
[VISUAL: the word dhamma in Brahmi script]
Ashoka's dhamma was not a religion with priests and rituals. It was a way of living. In his edicts, he lists what it means: obey your parents, respect your elders, be generous to the poor and to monks and Brahmins, treat servants and slaves well, be truthful, be gentle, and do not kill living creatures.
[VISUAL: icons for each virtue appearing one by one]
He also wanted his officers to act this way. He created a new post, the dhamma-mahamatta, an officer whose job was to look after the welfare of people of all faiths, and to spread this ethic.
He built things too. In Pillar Edict 7 he lists his works: roads lined with banyan shade trees, mango groves, wells dug at every half kos, which is roughly a mile, rest houses, and medical care for people and animals.
[VISUAL: road with trees, wells, rest house, herbal medicine]
And he told his people about it, in stone. He had his messages cut into rocks and into tall, polished sandstone pillars across the empire, in local languages. In the east, in a script called Brahmi. In the north-west, in Aramaic and in Greek.
[VISUAL: map of edict sites, Kandahar Greek inscription close-up]
Was Ashoka a Buddhist? Yes. In the Minor Rock Edict he says he became a lay follower, and in later years he drew closer to the Sangha, the community of monks. He visited the Buddha's birthplace, Lumbini, where a pillar still tells us that the Buddha was born there and that Ashoka reduced the taxes of the village.
[VISUAL: Lumbini pillar and inscription]
But he did not force his faith on anyone. In Rock Edict 12, he says people should honour all sects, and that those who praise their own faith by criticising others harm their own faith.
[VISUAL: edict quote, honour all sects]
Ashoka also sent messengers abroad. His edicts name five Greek kings: Antiochus of Syria, Ptolemy of Egypt, Antigonus of Macedonia, Magas of Cyrene and Alexander of Epirus or Corinth. Sri Lankan chronicles say he sent his son Mahinda and his daughter Sanghamitta to Sri Lanka, where Buddhism took root and still lives.
[VISUAL: map of missions to Greek kingdoms and Sri Lanka]
Two different kinds of evidence again: the Greek kings appear in his own words, while the Sri Lankan mission comes from chronicles. Both may be true.

## 8. A POWERFUL KING, NOT A PERFECT ONE (16:30–18:00)
**NARRATION**
Was Ashoka a pacifist? The answer is no.
[VISUAL: question card, was Ashoka a pacifist?]
He did not disband his army. In Rock Edict 13, he warns the forest peoples that he has the power to punish them, if they commit crimes. He kept the death penalty, though one pillar edict says that condemned prisoners should get three extra days before execution, so they can prepare. And in Kalinga itself, he told his officers not to be harsh, but he did not give up the land.
Some historians also point to the limits of his reforms. Dhamma was a moral message, not a legal code. It depended on the king's own energy.
So the best way to describe Ashoka is as a powerful ruler who tried to use his power for moral purposes. That, historians argue, makes him unusual, even if it does not make him a saint.

## 9. THE END OF THE EMPIRE (18:00–19:40)
**NARRATION**
Ashoka ruled for about thirty-six years, and died around 232 BCE.
[VISUAL: timeline ends, 232 BCE]
His empire did not last. Within about fifty years, the Maurya dynasty was over. Around 185 BCE, the last Maurya king, Brihadratha, was killed by his own commander, Pushyamitra Shunga.
[VISUAL: map shrinking, Shunga dynasty emerges]
Why did it fall? Historians list several reasons. The empire was highly centralised, and needed a strong king. The royal family may have divided the empire after Ashoka. The treasury was strained by a big army and administration. And pressure grew from the north-west.
A popular claim says Ashoka's peace policy weakened the army. Most modern historians reject that as too simple. The empire survived decades after his death, and many factors played a role.

## 10. LEGACY (19:40–21:00)
**NARRATION**
After the Mauryas, Ashoka was largely forgotten in India. His inscriptions were written in Brahmi, a script nobody could read anymore.
[VISUAL: weathered pillar in a field]
In 1837, a British scholar named James Prinsep finally deciphered Brahmi. Suddenly the edicts could be read. They spoke of a king who called himself Devanampiya Piyadasi, Beloved of the Gods, of Gracious Look. Only later, after another inscription, was it confirmed that this was Ashoka.
[VISUAL: James Prinsep portrait and Brahmi chart]
Today the Lion Capital of Ashoka, found at Sarnath, is India's national emblem. The Ashoka Chakra, the wheel with twenty-four spokes, is at the centre of India's flag.
[VISUAL: Sarnath lion capital, Indian flag]
His message from 2,300 years ago is simple: real power is measured by what you do with it.

## 11. WHAT HISTORIANS STILL DEBATE (21:00–22:00)
**NARRATION**
Historians still disagree about three things. How violent was his rise to power? How much did Buddhism spread because of Ashoka, and how much because of monks and merchants? And why did his empire fall so quickly?
