# Ep 22 — Catherine the Great: The German Princess Who Ruled Russia
Target: ~10 min (1404 words) · Status: DRAFT, fact-checked by web search (see factcheck/); still needs a human check against the cited books

## 1. HOOK (0:00–0:50)
**NARRATION**
On the night of 27 June 1762, a woman named Catherine was woken in the middle of the night in a villa outside St Petersburg. Alexei Orlov, an officer of the Guards, told her that a plot was discovered and she must leave at once. She dressed, got into a carriage, and drove to the barracks of the Izmailovsky regiment. The soldiers swore loyalty to her. By the next morning, she was Empress of Russia, and her husband, the Tsar, was a prisoner.
[VISUAL: night carriage ride, soldiers kneeling]
She was not Russian by birth. She had no claim to the throne. Yet she ruled for 34 years, expanded the empire by hundreds of thousands of square kilometres, corresponded with Voltaire and Diderot, and built the Hermitage collection. This is the story of Catherine the Great.
[VISUAL: Catherine portrait, Hermitage]

## 2. A GERMAN PRINCESS (0:50–3:00)
**NARRATION**
She was born Sophie Friederike Auguste, Princess of Anhalt-Zerbst, on 2 May 1729 in Stettin, in Prussia. Her father was a minor German prince and a Prussian general. Her mother, Johanna, was ambitious and well connected, but Sophie's family was poor and had no great importance.
[VISUAL: Stettin, young Sophie]
Then came a stroke of luck. The Empress of Russia, Elizabeth, daughter of Peter the Great, had no children and needed an heir. She chose her nephew, Peter, a Holstein prince, and in 1744 she summoned Sophie to Russia as a possible bride. At fourteen, Sophie arrived with her mother.
She worked hard to please. She learned Russian, studying at night, even when ill. She converted to Orthodoxy, taking the name Ekaterina, Catherine. In August 1745, she married the young Grand Duke Peter.
[VISUAL: journey to Russia, conversion ceremony, wedding]
The marriage was a disaster. Peter was immature, played with toy soldiers, and admired Frederick the Great of Prussia. Catherine, in her memoirs, describes years of loneliness, and she read widely, including Voltaire, Montesquieu and Tacitus. She later took lovers, and her son Paul was born in 1754. Her memoirs hint that her lover Sergei Saltykov was the father, but she later took the hint back, and historians are still divided.

## 3. THE COUP OF 1762 (3:00–5:30)
**NARRATION**
Empress Elizabeth died on 5 January 1762, and Peter became Peter III. He was unpopular within months. He pulled Russia out of the Seven Years' War, giving back all the conquests to Prussia, just as Russia was winning. He took away the Church's lands. He insulted the Guards, and spoke contemptuously of Russians. And he threatened to send Catherine to a convent.
[VISUAL: Peter III with toy soldiers, the Guards offended]
Catherine had friends among the Guards, especially the Orlov brothers, led by her lover Grigory Orlov. On 28 June 1762, with a few weeks of planning and a bit of luck, she took power. Peter, who was in Oranienbaum, was arrested and forced to abdicate.
Eight days later, he died at Ropsha, in the custody of Alexei Orlov. The official cause was colic and a stroke, a diagnosis that the historian Simon Sebag Montefiore called absurd. Most people believe Orlov killed him, though historians find no evidence that Catherine ordered it. A letter from Orlov, which survives in a copy, says it was an accident in a drunken fight.
[VISUAL: abdication, Ropsha, the Orlov letter]
She was crowned in September. From the start, she was a usurper who had to prove herself.

## 4. THE ENLIGHTENED EMPRESS (5:30–9:00)
**NARRATION**
Catherine wanted to be seen as an enlightened ruler. She wrote letters to Voltaire, whom she called her teacher, and invited the French philosopher Diderot to St Petersburg when he was short of money. She bought his library, and left it with him as a gift for life.
[VISUAL: Voltaire and Diderot, letters]
In 1767, she called a Legislative Commission, with delegates from nobles, towns, state peasants and Cossacks, to draft a new code of laws. She wrote a long instruction, the Nakaz, mostly borrowed from Montesquieu and Beccaria. It said that all men are equal, that torture should be abolished and that the aim of government is the good of its citizens. The Commission dissolved in 1768 without a new code, when the Turkish war started.
In practice, Catherine was an autocrat. She never questioned her absolute power. And she never abolished serfdom; she made it worse. Serfs could be sold, and in 1767 were forbidden to petition against landlords. Catherine gave away hundreds of thousands of state peasants with estates to her favourites.
[VISUAL: Nakaz manuscript, serfs and landlords]
But she did introduce some important changes. She took on smallpox, which killed millions. In 1768, she invited an English doctor, Thomas Dimsdale, to inoculate her, and her son, to show courage and set an example. She founded the Smolny Institute in 1764, the first state school for girls. She reformed provincial government in 1775 and issued the Charter to the Nobility in 1785, giving nobles freedom from state service, and the Charter to the Towns.
[VISUAL: Dimsdale inoculating the Empress, Smolny girls]

## 5. WAR AND EXPANSION (9:00–12:30)
**NARRATION**
Catherine's reign saw Russia rise in territory. The main enemies were the Ottoman Empire in the south and Poland in the west.
[VISUAL: map of Russia's growth]
In the Russo-Turkish War of 1768 to 1774, Russian generals Rumyantsev and Suvorov won big victories on land, and a Russian fleet sailed from the Baltic around Europe and destroyed the Ottoman fleet at Chesme in 1770. The Treaty of Kuchuk Kainarji in 1774 gave Russia access to the Black Sea and made the Crimean Khanate independent of the Ottomans. In 1783, Catherine annexed Crimea. A second war followed, 1787 to 1792, which gave Russia the northern Black Sea coast, with new cities such as Odessa, Kherson, and Sevastopol. She called the region New Russia.
[VISUAL: Chesme, Suvorov, Odessa, Sevastopol]
The man behind much of this was Grigory Potemkin, a general and statesman, possibly Catherine's secret husband, though the evidence is thin, and certainly her closest advisor. He governed New Russia and founded the Black Sea fleet. A famous story says that, when Catherine travelled to Crimea in 1787, Potemkin built fake villages along the road to impress her. Historians now think this is largely a myth, spread by his enemies.
[VISUAL: Potemkin, a fake village facade with a note: probably a myth]
In the west, she took part in the partitions of Poland, with Prussia and Austria, in 1772, 1793 and 1795. Poland disappeared from the map for 123 years. Russia gained most of Lithuania, Belarus and western Ukraine. Catherine justified it as reunion of Orthodox lands, and used the pretext of defending religious minorities.
[VISUAL: three partitions of Poland on a map]

## 6. THE PUGACHEV REVOLT (12:30–14:30)
**NARRATION**
Her reign also saw the greatest rebellion in Russian history. In 1773, a Don Cossack named Yemelyan Pugachev claimed to be the dead Peter III. He promised freedom from serfdom, taxes and military service. Cossacks, serfs, Old Believers, and Bashkir and Kazakh tribes joined him. He captured Kazan and threatened Moscow.
[VISUAL: Pugachev with his peasant army, Kazan in flames]
The revolt was crushed in 1774. Pugachev was betrayed by his own officers, taken to Moscow in a cage, and executed in January 1775. Catherine was shaken. She was reminded that her rule rested on the nobles' support. She reorganised provincial government, and she grew more conservative. The French Revolution of 1789 pushed her further. She banned the books she had once praised, and she exiled the writer Alexander Radishchev, who criticised serfdom.

## 7. CULTURE AND COURT (14:30–16:30)
**NARRATION**
Catherine was a great patron of art. She collected European paintings on a huge scale. In 1764, she bought 225 paintings from a Berlin merchant, which became the start of the Hermitage collection. By her death, it had several thousand works. She built the Hermitage Theatre, the Smolny, and the Marble Palace, and she wrote plays, comic operas and a long autobiography.
[VISUAL: Hermitage, Winter Palace, paintings]
Her personal life is famous. She had a series of favourites. The best known were Grigory Orlov, Potemkin and the young Platon Zubov, who was 38 years younger. She lavished them with titles, money and serfs. Historians point out that male rulers had mistresses without criticism. But Catherine was judged more harshly. Many of the stories about her, such as the horse legend, were invented by her enemies. Her death was natural: she had a stroke on 5 November 1796, and died on the 6th (17 November in the new calendar), aged 67.
[VISUAL: favourites in portraits, with a note: the horse story is a myth]

## 8. LEGACY AND DEBATE (16:30–18:00)
**NARRATION**
Catherine left a larger, richer and stronger Russia. She also left an empire that depended on serfdom, and a nobility that had learned French. Her son Paul I, who hated her, succeeded her, and her grandson, Alexander I, would face Napoleon.
[VISUAL: map of Russia 1796, Paul I]
Historians still debate. Was she an enlightened absolutist or a cynical politician? Was the Charter to the Nobility a liberation, or a surrender to the nobles? Did she cause the harsher serfdom, or did she follow a trend? And how much of her self-image, built in letters to philosophers, was propaganda?
[VISUAL: three question cards]
